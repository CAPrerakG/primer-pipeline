Wires, Cables & Conductors

Everything that carries electricity or signals from one place to another: the wire inside your wall, the heavy cable buried under a city street, the conductor strung between transmission towers, the fine enamelled copper wound inside every motor and transformer, and the optical fibre carrying the internet. It is the most copper-intensive industry in the market, and in 2026 it produced the strongest returns in the Power family — with the two largest companies left behind.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

A copper business that makes its money on conversion, brand and reach

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Electricity needs a road to travel on, and that road is metal — usually copper or aluminium. A wire is a thin road for a house; a cable is a thick, well-protected road that can go underground; a conductor is a bare road hung high between towers. Inside every fan and transformer there is also a long, thin, coated copper wire wound round and round like thread on a spool. Companies buy big rods of copper and aluminium, stretch them thinner, twist them together and wrap them in plastic. The metal itself is most of the cost. What the company earns is the "stretching and wrapping" fee — plus a little extra if people trust its name.

Say it to a client in one minute

2026 has been a boom year for this sector, but the returns have gone to the smaller companies rather than the leaders.

  • Copper at a record: $6.86/lb on 8 September; the ICSG expects a 150,000-tonne deficit in 2026 after a surplus in 2025, and US tariffs on refined copper are under discussion.
  • Results are excellent: Polycab revenue +39%, RR Kabel +54%, Finolex +44%, KEI +23% with margin +247 bps, APAR profit +78%, Diamond Power sales +128%.
  • Part of the growth is copper: revenue rises when metal prices rise, even at flat volumes. Volume was strong too — cable demand from transmission, data centres, solar and exports.
  • New competitors: UltraTech's ₹1,800 cr Ultravolt went on sale on 3 September; Adani's cables arm is expected around the end of 2026. Polycab (+10%) and KEI (+4%) have lagged as a result.
  • The smaller names ran: V-Marc +205%, Birla Cable +199%, Diamond Power +166%, APAR +125%; Diamond Power is valued at ₹23,600 cr on quarterly profit of ₹58 cr.

Analogy

The industry is like a bakery that buys flour at whatever the market price is and sells bread. When flour prices rise, the price of bread rises too and the bakery's sales grow — but that is not the same as selling more loaves. What matters is the fee for baking (the conversion margin), how many loaves go out (volume), and whether customers ask for the bakery by name (brand). Every result in this industry has to be read in those three parts.

Where volume grew fastest

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Metal: big companies make pure copper and aluminium; cable makers buy it at the world price.
  • Drawing: thick rods are pulled through smaller and smaller holes until they are as thin as needed.
  • Wrapping: plastic is melted around the metal so it is safe to touch.
  • Testing: every drum is checked; big cables for power companies need special approval.
  • Shops: electricians choose which brand to buy for your house, so companies reward electricians.
  • Big projects: power grids, data centres and foreign customers buy cable by the kilometre.
Fund-manager lens

The critical question for any company in this section is where it earns its margin. Building wires sold through dealers earn the highest margin because the electrician's choice of brand protects price — that is what UltraTech and Adani are now attacking with their existing cement and building-materials distribution. Power cables earn margin through technical approvals, especially at extra-high voltage. Conductors and winding wire earn a thin conversion fee on a large metal cost, so their revenue swings with copper and aluminium but their profit per tonne barely moves. A company's valuation should follow its mix: Polycab and Havells deserve a brand multiple; Precision Wires, at a 4.8% EBITDA margin, does not, however fast its revenue grows.

How to read a cable company's quarter

  1. Separate volume from price. With copper up about 40% year on year, a 40% revenue increase can mean zero volume growth. Managements usually disclose volume growth; use it.
  2. Look at EBITDA per unit, not margin percentage. When the metal price rises, margin percentage falls even if profit per kilometre is unchanged — Polycab's 70 bps decline and Precision's 4.8% against 5.3% are largely this effect.
  3. Strip out inventory gains. Metal bought cheaper and sold after a price rise produces a one-off gain; it reverses when copper falls.
  4. Watch working capital. Higher metal prices lock more cash in inventory and receivables.
Analogy

A petrol pump owner who filled the tank last month and sells at today's higher price looks very profitable this month — but only once. Cable makers in a rising copper market are partly in that position; the question for 2027 is how much of this year's profit was the tank, and how much was the pump.

Copper is not the driver of the stocks

Copper fell 17% in 2014 and the basket rose 162%; copper rose 42% in 2025 and the basket fell 6.5%. Over this record the stocks have been driven by volume — construction, transmission, GST formalisation, exports — and by competition, not by the metal price. Copper matters for reported revenue, working capital and quarterly inventory gains; it is a poor guide to share prices.

7 · The business calendar

Construction season, dealer stocking and utility tenders

  • April–June: the peak of the construction season before the monsoon; new-year utility ordering; dealers restock.
  • July–September: monsoon slows site work and house wiring; project cable dispatches continue.
  • October–December: the festive and post-monsoon construction season; the strongest quarter for retail wires.
  • January–March: year-end project billing is high, but dealers destock before March, and the stocks have historically been weak.
  • Copper price revisions: list prices for wires are revised several times a quarter when copper moves sharply.

  • Strongest months vs the Nifty: Apr +8.6 pts (beat in 13 of 15 years); Dec +4.6 pts (beat in 11 of 15 years); Jun +4.5 pts (beat in 10 of 15 years).
  • Weakest months: Mar -3.1 pts (beat in 2 of 15 years); Feb -2.7 pts (beat in 4 of 15 years); Jan -0.6 pts (beat in 8 of 15 years).
  • Windows: Feb–Mar beat the Nifty in 3 of 15 years (median -4.4 pts); Apr–Jul beat the Nifty in 11 of 15 years (median +21.1 pts); Aug–Sep beat the Nifty in 8 of 14 years (median +0.2 pts); Oct–Dec beat the Nifty in 12 of 14 years (median +12.7 pts); Sep–Nov beat the Nifty in 13 of 14 years (median +7.0 pts); Jan–Mar beat the Nifty in 6 of 15 years (median -6.9 pts).

Control: small caps as a whole (Nifty Smallcap 100) beat the Nifty in April in 11 of 15 years, by an average of 3.0 points, and lag in January–February. Part of any April strength is this market-wide effect. After removing it, this industry's own April edge is about +5.6 points.

Reading it: one of the most reliable seasonal patterns in the library. September–November has beaten the Nifty in 13 of 14 years (median +7.0 points; the only miss was 2020) and October–December in 12 of 14 (median +12.7). April is the best single month, 13 of 15 (+8.6 points), and April–July works 11 of 15 with a median of +21.1. The weak stretch is equally consistent: March has beaten the Nifty in only 2 of 15 years, February–March in 3 of 15 and January–March in 6 of 15 (median −6.9). The pattern follows the construction season and the year-end destocking of the dealer channel.

A tendency, not a rule: small samples, survivorship bias (only names still listed) and equal weighting. Use it for timing context, not as a signal.

The exceptions, and why

  • September–November failed only in 2020 (−12.0), a Covid year in which the Nifty's recovery outran the cable stocks. The best years were 2017 (+31.8) and 2023 (+15.8).
  • October–December failed in 2020 (−6.3) and 2025 (−2.1); the 2025 miss came in the year of the UltraTech and Adani announcements.
  • April–July failed in 2019 (−19.3), 2013 (−9.0), 2018 (−8.0) and 2012 (−0.7) — two slowdown years and two small-cap sell-offs. Its best runs were 2014 (+90.6) and 2026 (+59.0).
  • January–March succeeded in only 6 of 15 years, and 2025 (−30.1) was the worst, as the UltraTech announcement in late February hit the sector. The best was 2023 (+8.0).
  • March itself beat the Nifty in only 2 of 15 years (2019 and 2015), with 2020 (−9.0) and 2013 (−8.3) the worst.
How to use this

This is one of the most consistent calendars in the library, and it has a business logic: the dealer channel destocks before the financial year ends and restocks in April, while construction peaks before and after the monsoon. The practical rule is to add in March and let the April–July and September–December windows work, and to be wary of January–March. The caveat is that 2025 broke the October–December pattern on a structural event — new entrants — which is exactly the kind of risk a seasonal pattern cannot capture.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Eight drivers

  1. Construction and real estate: house wires follow housing completions with a lag of about two years.
  2. Power transmission and distribution: conductors and HT/EHV cables, including underground cabling in cities (see 039).
  3. Data centres: 12% of Diamond Power's order book; a source of high-value cable demand for the leaders too.
  4. Renewables and solar: solar DC cable and plant cabling (see 038); Polycab's solar products more than doubled.
  5. Exports: APAR's record ₹5,245 cr export order book; KEI's exports fell 7% this quarter.
  6. Copper and aluminium prices: reported revenue, inventory gains and working capital.
  7. Competitive intensity: UltraTech, Adani and capacity additions by incumbents.
  8. Formalisation: GST, BIS certification and quality rules move share from unorganised makers to branded ones.

The anatomy of the cycles

  1. The infrastructure re-rating (2014–2017): the election, then GST, moved demand toward organised, branded makers; +162% in 2014 and +124% in 2017.
  2. The small-cap and telecom bust (2018–2020): three weak years as small caps crashed, optical-fibre prices collapsed and Covid shut sites.
  3. The long boom (2021–2024): housing, transmission, exports and renewables, with four consecutive years of outperformance (+90%, +44%, +163%, +83%).
  4. The entrant shock (2025): UltraTech's and Adani's announcements de-rated the sector while copper rose 42%.
  5. Record copper and record results (2026): +88%, led by small and mid-sized names while the leaders lag.

Patterns that repeat

  • Volume and formalisation drive the stocks, not copper.
  • The small caps make the extreme moves: Paramount +424% (2017), Diamond Power +861% (2024), Delton −79% (2015).
  • Telecom cable is a separate cycle — up in 2018, down 49–69% in 2019 — tied to fibre prices and telecom capex.
  • New entrants cause de-ratings before they cause losses: the 2025 fall happened before either entrant sold a metre of wire.

11 · The global picture

A global copper squeeze, and India as a cable exporter

Global cable demand is being pushed up by grid reinforcement in Europe and North America, offshore wind interconnectors, data centres and electrification, while copper supply struggles: mine output fell in the first half of 2026, smelters face acid and concentrate constraints, and the possibility of US tariffs on refined copper has pulled metal into the US and tightened the rest of the market. For Indian cable makers this has two effects. Reported revenue and working capital rise with the metal price. And export demand has improved, because Western utilities and developers need suppliers outside China — APAR's export order book and its new UK and Brazilian entities are the clearest example.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • From unorganised to branded: GST, BIS certification and electrician loyalty programmes moved share to the organised makers after 2017.
  • From copper to aluminium in some power cables: cost pressure makes aluminium-conductor cables more attractive in distribution.
  • From domestic to exporter: India's cable and conductor makers have become suppliers to Western utilities.
  • From specialists to conglomerates: UltraTech and Adani bring distribution scale and backward integration into metals.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. HTLS and advanced conductors: carry roughly twice the current on existing towers, useful when new corridors are hard to secure (see 039).
  2. EHV underground cables: 220–400 kV cable for cities and data centres; KEI's EHV sales rose 48%.
  3. Fire-safe and halogen-free insulation: FR, FRLS and LSZH wires are standard in modern buildings.
  4. Solar and EV cables: specialised DC and flexible cables for renewables and charging.
  5. Backward integration into metal: Precision Wires' trial cathode production; conglomerates with their own copper and aluminium.
  6. Optical fibre for data centres: high-fibre-count cables for AI data centres revive a depressed segment.

The honest read

Operating conditions are as good as they have been: strong volumes in almost every end market and a copper price that inflates revenue and produces inventory gains. That combination flatters 2026 results, and part of it will reverse if copper falls. Meanwhile the market has done something unusual: it has de-rated the two strongest franchises, Polycab and KEI, because of the conglomerate entry, and bid up smaller companies with less brand protection and much thinner records. If UltraTech and Adani compete on price in building wires, the small branded makers are more exposed than the leaders, not less. The sector's best-placed businesses on a three-year view are those with technical barriers (EHV cable, export conductors) or a dominant brand at a sensible price; the least safe are the high-multiple small caps whose 2026 profits owe most to copper.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What was volume growth, separately from price, in wires and in cables?
  2. How much of this quarter's margin came from inventory gains on copper?
  3. What share of the order book is fixed-price, and what share carries price-variation clauses?
  4. What are you seeing from Ultravolt in the dealer channel — pricing, schemes, electrician incentives?
  5. What is the export order book, by region, and at what margin compared with domestic?
  6. What is EHV cable capacity and utilisation?
  7. How have working-capital days changed with copper?
  8. What is the capex plan, and how much of it is backward integration into metal?

Red flags

  • Revenue growth close to copper's price rise with no volume disclosure.
  • Margin expansion that disappears in a quarter when copper falls.
  • Rising receivables at small caps selling to EPC contractors.
  • Distributor-level price cutting in building wires.

"Revenue growth of 40–60% means demand is booming."Partly; copper rose about 40% over the year. Volume growth is the number to ask for.
"UltraTech and Adani will destroy the incumbents."Unproven: Ultravolt has only just gone on sale. The leaders have brand, electrician networks and EHV capability; the more exposed are smaller brands.
"The small caps are the better way to play the cycle."They have outperformed in 2026, but on thinner records and higher multiples, and they fell hardest in 2013, 2018 and 2025.
"Winding-wire makers are high-growth companies."Their revenue follows copper; Precision Wires' EBITDA margin is 4.8%.
"Copper is sure to keep rising."Part of 2026's rise reflects tariff-driven stockpiling in the US, which can reverse; the tariff has not yet been imposed.

19 · Glossary

Every term you'll meet

Products and materials

Wire
A single conductor or small group of conductors with light insulation, used in buildings.
Cable
One or more insulated conductors with a protective outer sheath, often armoured.
LT, HT and EHV cable
Low-tension (up to 1.1 kV), high-tension (about 3.3–33 kV) and extra-high-voltage (66–400 kV) cables.
Armoured cable
A cable wrapped in steel wire or tape for burial or rough use.
Control and instrumentation cable
Low-voltage cables carrying signals in factories and plants.
Flexible cable
Cable made of many fine strands so it can bend — for appliances and machines.
Solar DC cable
UV- and weather-resistant cable for solar plants.
ACSR
Aluminium conductor steel reinforced — the standard overhead conductor.
AAAC
All-aluminium alloy conductor — lighter, corrosion-resistant.
HTLS conductor
High-temperature, low-sag conductor carrying roughly twice the current of ACSR on the same towers.
OPGW
Optical ground wire: the top wire on a transmission line with optical fibres inside.
Winding (magnet) wire
Enamel- or paper-insulated copper or aluminium wire wound in motors and transformers.
Enamelled wire
Copper wire coated with a thin insulating enamel.
Paper-insulated copper conductor (PICC)
Winding conductor for large transformers.
Continuously transposed conductor (CTC)
A bundle of insulated strands for large power-transformer windings.
Optical fibre cable (OFC)
Glass fibres carrying light signals; used in telecom and data centres.
XLPE
Cross-linked polyethylene, the standard insulation for HT and EHV cable.
PVC
Polyvinyl chloride, the most common insulation for building wires (see 002).
FR, FRLS, LSZH
Fire-retardant, fire-retardant low-smoke, and low-smoke zero-halogen insulation grades.
Copper cathode
Refined copper plates of about 99.99% purity; the starting metal.
Copper rod
Continuous-cast copper rod, usually 8 mm, that wire makers draw down.
Wire drawing
Pulling rod through progressively smaller dies to reduce its diameter.
Annealing
Heating drawn wire to make it soft and conductive again.
Stranding
Twisting several wires together into one conductor.
Extrusion
Melting insulation compound and forming it around the conductor.
Square millimetre (sq mm)
The cross-section of a conductor, which sets how much current it can carry.
Copper-clad aluminium
Aluminium wire with a copper coating, a cheaper substitute in some uses.

Commercial and market

LME
London Metal Exchange, the global price reference for copper and aluminium.
Comex
The US copper futures market; its premium to the LME has widened on tariff expectations.
Conversion margin
What a maker earns over the metal cost for processing it.
Price-variation clause (PVC)
A contract term passing metal-price changes to the customer.
Inventory gain
Profit from metal bought at a lower price and sold after a price rise.
Channel stocking
Dealers buying ahead of expected price rises, which boosts a quarter's sales.
List-price revision
A change in the published dealer price of wires, made several times a quarter when copper moves.
Electrician loyalty programme
Rewards for electricians who choose a brand — the retail moat.
Organised market
Branded, tax-compliant makers, about ₹80,000 cr of a ₹1 lakh cr market.
FMEG
Fast-moving electrical goods — fans, lights, switches, solar products.
BIS certification
The Bureau of Indian Standards mark required on many wires and cables.
Type test
Certification testing of a cable design, required by utilities (see 042).
Volume versus value growth
Growth in kilometres or tonnes versus growth in rupees.
Working-capital days
How many days of sales are locked in inventory and receivables net of payables.
ICSG
International Copper Study Group, which publishes supply-demand balances.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. Why can a cable company's revenue rise 40% without it selling more?

Because copper is most of the product cost and is passed through in the price; copper rose about 40% over the year.

2. Which part of the industry earns the highest margin, and why?

Branded building wires, because electricians' brand preference protects price.

3. Why did Polycab and KEI lag in 2026 despite strong results?

UltraTech's Ultravolt went on sale on 3 September and Adani's entry is expected around year-end, raising fears of competition in building wires.

4. What is the evidence that copper does not drive the stocks?

2014: copper −17%, basket +162%. 2025: copper +42%, basket −6.5%.

5. What margin does a winding-wire maker earn?

About 4–6%; Precision Wires reported 4.8% against 5.3%.

6. What made APAR's quarter a record?

Revenue +29%, EBITDA +63%, profit +78%, with oils EBITDA +214% and a record ₹5,245 cr export order book.

7. What is HTLS conductor?

High-temperature, low-sag conductor carrying roughly twice the current of standard ACSR on existing towers.

8. Which is the most reliable seasonal window?

September–November: 13 of 14 years, median +7.0 points; the only miss was 2020.

9. Which month is weakest?

March: the basket beat the Nifty in only 2 of 15 years, as dealers destock before the financial year ends.

10. What is an inventory gain, and why does it matter now?

Profit on metal bought cheaper and sold after a price rise; with copper at a record, part of 2026's margins may not repeat.
