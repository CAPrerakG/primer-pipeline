Industry primer · 25 of 278 · Auto family

Tyres

The only part of a vehicle that touches the road, and the only major component a vehicle owner is guaranteed to buy again and again. That makes tyres two businesses at once: a low-margin supplier to carmakers, and a high-margin consumer brand in the replacement market. Everything about the sector — margins, seasonality, stock returns — is decided by one number that moves independently of both: the price of rubber.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

A commodity processor wearing a consumer brand

Explain it to a 10-year-old

A tyre is mostly rubber, some soot, and a skeleton of steel wire, all baked together in a hot mould. Some of the rubber comes from trees in Kerala and Thailand, where people cut the bark and collect the milky sap. Some is made from oil. So a tyre company buys tree sap and oil, bakes them into a doughnut, and sells it twice: once to the company that builds the car, and again — years later, at a much better price — to you, when your tyres wear out. When tree sap and oil get cheap, tyre companies make a lot of money. When they get expensive, they suffer, because they cannot raise their prices as fast as the sap price rises.

Say it to a client in one minute

Tyres are the clearest raw-material-spread business in the auto chain. Raw materials are roughly 60–65% of the selling price, and the biggest of them, natural rubber, is an agricultural commodity nobody in the industry controls.

  • 2026 is a squeeze year. Volumes are strong — JK Tyre's domestic volumes +25%, exports +16% — but rubber is up more than 50% from 2023 and margins fell almost everywhere.
  • The replacement market is where the money is, typically two-thirds of industry volume and materially better margin than supplying carmakers.
  • MRF is the premium brand and the price leader; Apollo and CEAT are the scale players; Balkrishna is the outlier — an off-highway exporter with margins twice the industry's; JK Tyre is the truck-radial specialist and the most cyclical.
  • The strategic story is CEAT's Camso deal with Michelin and Balkrishna's move into commercial-vehicle radials: both are attempts to escape the Indian replacement market's price war.

Buy this industry when rubber is falling, not when volumes are rising. The 2014 record (+166%) came in a year of falling rubber and crude, not a year of record vehicle sales.

Analogy

Think of tyres as shoes. Original-equipment tyres are the shoes that come free with a new outfit: the shop barely makes anything on them, but you learn the brand. Replacement tyres are the shoes you buy yourself, where the shop makes its living. Retreading is resoling an old pair — it thrives when money is tight and dies when it is not.

Everyday examples

The tyres on a new Swift are probably MRF's or Apollo's, sold at a thin margin. The set the owner buys at 45,000 km is the profitable sale. The tyres on a tractor in Punjab are likely Balkrishna's or Goodyear's. The ones on the truck carrying your parcel have probably been retreaded twice before they are thrown away.

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Plantation: a worker cuts a spiral groove in the bark before dawn and a cup fills with white latex. It takes about seven years for a new tree to be ready, which is why supply cannot respond quickly to a high price.
  • Rubber and chemicals: latex is dried into sheets or blocks. Synthetic rubber is made from oil, so crude prices matter too. Carbon black — essentially fine soot — is what makes tyres black and strong.
  • Reinforcement: steel and fabric cords are the tyre's bones; without them it would stretch out of shape.
  • Manufacturing: everything is mixed like dough, shaped, and baked in a mould that stamps in the tread pattern.
  • Original equipment and replacement: the first set is sold cheaply to the carmaker; the second set, bought by the owner, is where the profit lives.
  • Retreading: a worn truck tyre's body is still good, so a new tread is glued and cured onto it for a fraction of the price.
Fund-manager lens

Three structural questions separate these companies. What share is replacement? More replacement means better and steadier margin. What share is exported or off-highway? Balkrishna's off-highway export model has consistently earned roughly double the domestic industry's margin. How radial is the mix? Radialisation raises capital intensity but also the barrier to entry. Everything else — a good quarter, a price increase — is noise against the rubber price.

The four levers

  1. Raw-material basket: roughly 60–65% of the selling price. Natural rubber, synthetic rubber, carbon black, steel cord and chemicals — four of the five move with crude or with rubber.
  2. Original equipment versus replacement mix: carmakers negotiate annually and hard; owners buy on brand and availability. Two-thirds replacement is the comfortable shape.
  3. Utilisation: a tyre plant is enormously capital-intensive, so a volume shortfall hits profit much harder than revenue.
  4. Price increases, and their lag: raw-material inflation shows up immediately; price increases take one to two quarters to pass through, and competitive intensity decides whether they stick at all. CEAT's 5–6% increases against a 15–20% cost rise is the arithmetic of 2026.
Analogy

A tyre company is a bakery where flour prices change daily but the menu board can only be repainted twice a year. In a year when flour falls, the bakery looks like a genius. In a year when flour jumps 50%, no amount of selling more bread helps — which is exactly what JK Tyre's 25% volume growth and 73% profit fall look like.

Why rubber supply cannot respond

A rubber tree takes about seven years from planting to first tapping, so a price spike cannot bring on new supply for most of a decade. Meanwhile yields fall when leaf-fall disease spreads or temperatures rise, and growers hold back stock when they expect higher prices. India consumes far more natural rubber than it produces and imports the balance, which is why domestic prices can trade well above international ones. The industry's own answer is the ATMA-led INROAD project, which aims to plant two lakh hectares of rubber in the north-east at a cost of about ₹1,100 cr over five years — a structural fix that pays off in the 2030s, not this cycle.

7 · The business calendar

Two calendars overlaid

  • The tapping calendar: Indian rubber tapping peaks roughly from September to January and falls away in the hot months from February to May, when leaf fall and heat cut yields. Cheap domestic rubber is therefore a winter phenomenon.
  • The replacement calendar: owners change tyres before the monsoon and again before winter travel, so April–July and the festive quarter carry the aftermarket.
  • The negotiation calendar: original-equipment prices and annual contracts are settled in the March quarter, which is also when carmakers destock — the weakest window for these stocks.
  • The export calendar: European off-highway demand follows the northern-hemisphere farm season, which is why Balkrishna's cycle does not match the domestic one.

  • Strongest months vs the Nifty: Apr +4.6 pts (beat in 12 of 15 years); Sep +4.3 pts (beat in 11 of 15 years); Nov +3.0 pts (beat in 10 of 15 years).
  • Weakest months: Feb -2.0 pts (beat in 3 of 15 years); Jan -0.8 pts (beat in 5 of 15 years); Mar -0.8 pts (beat in 6 of 15 years).
  • Windows: Feb–Mar beat the Nifty in 4 of 15 years (median -5.6 pts); Apr–Jul beat the Nifty in 12 of 15 years (median +9.0 pts); Aug–Sep beat the Nifty in 9 of 14 years (median +2.8 pts); Oct–Dec beat the Nifty in 9 of 14 years (median +2.2 pts); Sep–Nov beat the Nifty in 10 of 14 years (median +4.5 pts); Jan–Mar beat the Nifty in 4 of 15 years (median -3.6 pts).

Control: small caps as a whole (Nifty Smallcap 100) beat the Nifty in April in 11 of 15 years, by an average of 3.0 points, and lag in January–February. Part of any April strength is this market-wide effect. After removing it, this industry's own April edge is about +1.6 points.

Reading it: the tyre calendar is the rubber calendar. April–July wins 12 of 15 years (median +9.0 points) because the replacement market wakes with the pre-monsoon and monsoon change of tyres, and because rubber tapping is at its seasonal low so cost pressure is least visible. April alone is 12 of 15 and September 11 of 15. The losing windows are the same two every time: February–March (4 of 15, median −5.6) and January–March (4 of 15, median −3.6, with 2025 at −22.9), when peak tapping supply, year-end OEM destocking and annual price negotiations land together.

A tendency, not a rule: small samples, survivorship bias (only names still listed) and equal weighting. Use it for timing context, not as a signal.

The exceptions, and why

  • April–July failed three times: 2016 (−10.9), 2018 (−12.8) and 2019 (−20.0). All three were years in which rubber or crude was rising into the window and demand was weakening — 2019 most severely, in the middle of the auto slowdown.
  • The best April–July runs were 2023 (+28.9) and 2014 (+28.7), the two great rubber-deflation years.
  • January–March is a trap. It has worked only 4 times in 15 years, with 2025 (−22.9), 2022 (−15.5) and 2013 (−13.6) the worst; peak tapping supply, OEM destocking and annual price talks all land in that window.
  • September is the second-best month (11 of 15), with two extraordinary readings in 2016 (+27.1) and 2014 (+26.5), both rubber-driven.
How to use this

The April–July window is real (12 of 15, median +9.0), but it is a proxy for the rubber cycle rather than an independent effect. Use it as a timing overlay only when the rubber price is flat or falling; in a year like 2026, with TSR20 up more than 50% from 2023, seasonality is the weaker signal and the cost line is the stronger one. The mirror image is more useful: avoid the January–March window almost regardless of the news.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Eight drivers

  1. Natural rubber prices: the single biggest driver of profit, and of these stocks.
  2. Crude: sets synthetic rubber, carbon black feedstock and freight costs simultaneously.
  3. Replacement demand: a function of the vehicle parc and how far it is driven, far steadier than new vehicle sales.
  4. Commercial-vehicle activity: truck tyres are the largest value pool, and freight demand decides them (see 019).
  5. Imports and trade policy: Chinese truck radials, the 2020 import restriction and anti-dumping duties shape domestic pricing.
  6. Export markets and tariffs: the US is the largest export destination at 16% of value; the tariff move from 25% to 50% in August 2025 and back to 18% in February 2026 swung the economics twice in a year.
  7. Radialisation and premiumisation: larger rim sizes and radial trucks lift realisation per tyre.
  8. Capacity cycles: the industry periodically over-builds, which turns a demand pause into a margin collapse — 2018–19 being the clearest case.

The anatomy of a tyre cycle

  1. Rubber falls for reasons that have nothing to do with vehicles — a good tapping season, a demand slump in China, a currency move.
  2. Margins expand violently, because selling prices are sticky downwards; this is the phase when the stocks double.
  3. Competition and customers claw it back through price cuts and OEM negotiation, and the industry uses the cash to build capacity.
  4. Rubber turns up into that new capacity; margins compress faster than prices can be raised, and the stocks de-rate — 2018–19, and 2025–26.

Patterns that repeat

  • The rubber column predicts the return column better than any demand statistic. The three best years (2014, 2023, 2012) were all years of falling rubber; the two worst (2018, 2019) followed a rubber and crude upturn into new capacity.
  • Volume growth is not protection. JK Tyre grew domestic volumes 25% in Q1 FY27 and lost 73% of its profit.
  • Balkrishna marches to a different drum — it was the only stock up in 2019 and the worst in 2022, because its cycle is European farm equipment, not Indian trucks.
  • Micro caps amplify without adding information: Indag and TVS Srichakra have the largest swings in both directions.

11 · The global picture

Three giants, a Chinese wave, and India's export niche

Globally the industry is led by Michelin, Bridgestone and Goodyear, followed by Continental, Pirelli and Sumitomo, with a large and rapidly growing Chinese tier selling on price. The strategic direction of the western majors is to shed capital-heavy, low-growth niches and concentrate on premium and services — which is precisely what created CEAT's opportunity to buy Camso from Michelin, and what Balkrishna has spent two decades exploiting in off-highway.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • Bias to radial (1990s–2010s): radial truck tyres required a different, much more capital-intensive plant; the companies that invested early, led by JK Tyre and Apollo, took share from those that did not.
  • Chinese import wave (2014–2020): cheap truck radials took share until import restrictions and duties closed the gap.
  • Western majors retreating from niches (2020s): Michelin's sale of Camso to CEAT is the clearest Indian example of a global portfolio decision becoming a domestic opportunity.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. EV-specific tyres: electric vehicles are heavier and deliver instant torque, so they need stronger construction and lower rolling resistance, and they wear tyres faster — structurally good for replacement volumes.
  2. Lower rolling resistance: silica-rich compounds that trade grip against fuel economy; the core compounding skill.
  3. Larger rim sizes and premium fitments: SUVs raise the value per tyre without raising the number of tyres.
  4. Smart and sensor-equipped tyres: pressure and wear monitoring, particularly for fleets, linking this industry to 024.
  5. Sustainable materials: recycled carbon black, bio-based oils, and natural rubber traceability requirements from European buyers.
  6. Retreading and circularity: end-of-life tyre rules and extended producer responsibility are formalising what was an informal recycling trade.

The honest read

This is a cost cycle, not a demand cycle, and that is the more tractable kind. The demand side of the industry is in good shape: volumes, exports and the vehicle parc are all growing, and the US tariff has already halved from its 2025 peak. What is missing is the rubber price, and the historical record is unambiguous that the stocks move when it falls, not when volumes rise. The discipline is to own the operating leverage — JK Tyre and Apollo are the most geared — only once the rubber price has actually turned, and to accept that the structural compounder in the group, Balkrishna, will never be cheap at that moment. Meanwhile treat Goodyear India's 45% fall as a question to answer from filings rather than a bargain to buy.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What is the raw-material basket cost per kg this quarter, and what is already locked in for next quarter?
  2. What proportion of natural rubber is imported versus bought domestically, and at what spread?
  3. What is the original-equipment versus replacement split by value, not volume?
  4. What price increase has been announced, how much has actually been realised, and in which segment?
  5. What is plant utilisation, and what capacity is being added over the next two years?
  6. What share of revenue is exported, and what is the tariff exposure by destination?
  7. (CEAT) What is the Camso integration cost run-rate, and what changes at each of the October 2026, April 2027 and September 2028 milestones?
  8. (Balkrishna) What is the capital cost and expected margin of the commercial-vehicle radial entry, against a domestic market already fighting on price?
  9. (JK Tyre) At what raw-material level does the current volume growth start producing profit?

Red flags

  • Volume growth celebrated while absolute profit falls.
  • Price increases "announced" repeatedly without a realised number.
  • Capacity expansion funded with debt at the top of a margin cycle.
  • A large share-price move with no corresponding disclosure — Goodyear India in 2026 is the live example.

18 · Myths and live debates

What you'll hear, and the evidence

ClaimEvidence
"Tyres are a play on rising vehicle sales."They are a play on the rubber spread. The best year in the record (2014, +166%) was a rubber and crude collapse; 2026 has strong volumes and negative returns.
"EVs will kill tyre demand."The opposite: EVs are heavier, deliver instant torque and wear tyres faster, which supports replacement volumes and premium fitments.
"Price increases will fix the margin."Only partly and only slowly. CEAT is pushing 5–6% against 15–20% cost inflation; the gap is the quarter's loss.
"Import restrictions permanently protect the industry."They raised the floor after 2020, but they do not touch the raw-material side, which is where 2026's damage came from.
"Balkrishna is just a better tyre company."It is a different business — off-highway, export-led, replacement-heavy — which is why it was up in 2019 when the rest fell, and down in 2022 when they rose.
"Retreading is a dying trade."It is counter-cyclical: when new tyres get expensive, fleet operators retread. Watch Indag and Eastern Treads as a sentiment read on truck operators' cash.

19 · Glossary

Every term you'll meet

The product

Bias (cross-ply) tyre
Construction with body plies laid diagonally; cheaper, tougher on bad roads, less fuel-efficient.
Radial tyre
Body plies laid at right angles to the direction of travel with a steel belt; longer life and better fuel economy, but needs a costlier plant.
TBR
Truck and bus radial, the industry's largest value pool.
PCR
Passenger car radial.
OHT
Off-highway tyre: agricultural, mining, construction and industrial tyres. Balkrishna's core market.
Radialisation
The share of a segment that has converted from bias to radial; India's truck segment converted late and is still converting.
Tread
The patterned rubber that contacts the road; the part that wears out.
Carcass / casing
The tyre's body beneath the tread; if it is sound, the tyre can be retreaded.
Bead wire
The steel hoop that clamps the tyre to the rim.
Steel cord
The belt reinforcing a radial tyre.
Green tyre
The assembled but uncured tyre, before baking.
Curing
Baking the green tyre in a heated mould, which vulcanises the rubber and stamps in the tread pattern.
Vulcanisation
Cross-linking rubber with sulphur so it becomes elastic and durable rather than sticky (see 012).
Compounding
Mixing rubber with carbon black, silica, oils and chemicals to a recipe; the core technical skill of a tyre maker.
Rolling resistance
The energy a tyre loses as it deforms; lower resistance means better mileage or range.
Retreading
Bonding a new tread onto a used casing at a fraction of a new tyre's price.
Precured tread rubber
Ready-made tread strips used in retreading; Indag's and Eastern Treads' product.
Rubber track
A continuous rubber belt replacing wheels on compact construction equipment; part of the Camso business.

Raw materials

Natural rubber
Latex tapped from rubber trees, dried into sheets or blocks.
Latex
The milky sap collected from a tapped tree (see 012).
Tapping
Cutting a groove in the bark to collect latex, usually before dawn (see 012).
RSS4
Ribbed smoked sheet grade 4, the benchmark Indian natural rubber grade quoted by the Rubber Board.
TSR20
Technically specified rubber grade 20, the block-rubber grade traded internationally and used as this primer's price benchmark.
Synthetic rubber
Rubber made from crude derivatives such as butadiene and styrene.
Carbon black
Fine carbon powder that reinforces rubber and makes tyres black; Balkrishna makes its own.
Silica
A reinforcing filler used to cut rolling resistance in fuel-efficient tyres.
Leaf fall disease
A fungal disease that strips rubber trees and cuts yields, a recurring cause of supply shortfalls.
Rubber Board
The statutory body that reports Indian rubber production, consumption and prices.
INROAD
The ATMA-led project to plant two lakh hectares of rubber in the north-east at about ₹1,100 cr over five years.

Market and trade

Replacement market
Tyres bought by vehicle owners rather than fitted new; roughly two-thirds of volume and most of the profit.
Original equipment (OE)
Tyres supplied to vehicle makers for new vehicles; thin margin, annual price negotiation (see 023).
Vehicle parc
The total population of vehicles on the road, which sets replacement demand.
ATMA
The Automotive Tyre Manufacturers' Association, the industry body and data source.
Anti-dumping duty
A duty on imports sold below normal value; applied to Chinese truck radials from 2017.
Restricted import list
A licensing regime; new pneumatic tyres were moved onto it in June 2020.
Reciprocal tariff
The US duty applied to Indian goods, which went from 25% to 50% in August 2025 and to 18% in February 2026.
Extended producer responsibility
The obligation to fund collection and recycling of end-of-life tyres.
Quality control order
A rule making BIS certification mandatory, which restricts low-grade imports.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. What decides tyre-company profits more than anything else?

The rubber price. Raw materials are roughly 60–65% of the selling price, and natural rubber is the largest single item.

2. Why did JK Tyre's profit fall 73% while its domestic volumes rose 25%?

Raw-material inflation outran price increases; its EBITDA margin fell to 6.54%. Volume cannot offset a cost shock in a business with this operating leverage.

3. Where does the industry actually make money?

The replacement market — roughly two-thirds of volume, and materially better margin than supplying vehicle makers.

4. Why can't rubber supply respond to a price spike?

A rubber tree takes about seven years from planting to first tapping, so new supply arrives a cycle late.

5. What was the best year in the record, and why?

2014: +166% against the Nifty's +31%, when rubber fell to 171¢ and crude halved, cutting both the natural and synthetic sides of the cost sheet at once.

6. What did CEAT buy from Michelin, and on what timeline?

The Camso compact-construction-equipment bias tyre and rubber-track business for ₹1,185.6 cr, completed September 2025; customer transition by October 2026, full value-chain control by April 2027, brand transfer by September 2028.

7. Why does Balkrishna's cycle differ from the rest?

It sells off-highway tyres mainly into European and American agriculture and mining, so it follows the northern farm cycle — up in 2019 when the domestic industry fell, down in 2022 when it rose.

8. What happened to US tariffs on Indian tyres?

They rose from 25% to 50% in August 2025, then fell to 18% in February 2026. The US is India's largest tyre export destination at 16% of export value.

9. Which seasonal window should be avoided?

January–March: it beat the Nifty only 4 times in 15 years, median −3.6 points, worst −22.9 in 2025.

10. What should be checked before treating Goodyear India's 45% fall as an opportunity?

The filings. Nothing in the verifiable public record explains a decline of that size, so the disclosure record needs reading before the chart does.
