Industry primer · 58 of 278 · Defence family, part 2

Defence Electronics

A modern fighter, warship or air-defence battery is increasingly a collection of electronics wrapped in metal: radars that see, electronic-warfare systems that jam and listen, radios that link, and computers that fuse it all together. This is the part of Indian defence where domestic industry has come furthest — and where margins are highest. This primer explains what the products are, who makes what, how BEL and a group of smaller listed suppliers earn their money, and why the sector's 2026 rally was a small-cap story while its largest company stood still.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

The most Indian, most profitable part of defence

Explain it to a 10-year-old

If a fighter jet were a person, the metal body would be the skeleton, the engine the muscles — and the electronics would be the eyes, ears, voice and brain. The radar is its eyes, seeing planes far away even at night. The jammer is like shouting loudly so the enemy cannot hear. The radio is its voice, and the computer is its brain, deciding what to do. India still buys many engines from abroad, but it now makes a lot of the eyes, ears and brains itself, and the companies that make them earn more on each rupee than the companies that bend the metal.

Say it to a client in one minute

Defence electronics is where Indian indigenisation and margins are highest, and where valuations have run furthest.

  • The leader: BEL — FY26 revenue ₹27,480 cr (+16%), profit ₹6,048 cr, EBITDA margin 30%, order book ₹73,882 cr. Guides to 15%+ growth, 28%+ margin and ₹55,000 cr+ of orders in FY27, including QRSAM.
  • The suppliers: Data Patterns (processors and radars), Astra Microwave (RF and radar modules), Apollo Micro Systems (embedded systems), Paras (optics and electronics) — fast-growing, lumpy, richly valued.
  • 2026: the basket is +26%; Paras +101%, Data Patterns +72%, Astra +66% on FII buying, while BEL is −2% as it waits for QRSAM.
  • The cracks: Data Patterns' Q1 margin fell to 27%, Astra's revenue fell 11.5%, and C2C's auditors raised going-concern doubts.
  • Calendar: January–March has beaten the Nifty in only 2 of 15 years; April in 12 of 15.

Most of these products are built from the same building blocks — antennas, RF (radio-frequency) modules, signal processors, power supplies and software — assembled into a system that sits on a platform: a fighter, a warship, a truck or a missile. The same company may therefore sell a single board to one customer and a complete radar to another. That is why the listed companies overlap and why their order books are often a mix of components, subsystems and whole systems.

The chain in one breath

  • Chip makers — mostly abroad — supply the atoms.
  • Module makers such as Astra and Data Patterns build the organs.
  • BEL assembles most of the senses into complete systems, often to DRDO designs.
  • HAL, BDL and the shipyards put them into platforms.
  • Everyone earns again from upgrades and spares for decades.
Fund-manager lens

The critical question for each company is where it sits between build-to-print and design ownership. A company that builds a DRDO design earns a manufacturing margin and must wait for DRDO's next project. A company that owns its design — Data Patterns' processors and radars, Astra's RF modules — can sell it across programmes and exports and earns more. That is the main reason Data Patterns guides to a 35–40% EBITDA margin while Astra earns about 19%, and why valuations in this section differ so widely.

BEL's economics rest on scale and on the government as a customer that pays advances. It carries large cash balances, plans over ₹2,200 cr of R&D and over ₹1,200 cr of capital spending this year, and earns a margin above most private manufacturers. Its order book of ₹73,882 cr is 2.7 years of revenue — much shorter than HAL's 7.7 years (see 057) — because electronics programmes are delivered faster than aircraft.

The smaller companies depend on winning a place in programmes led by BEL, HAL, BDL or DRDO. Their orders arrive in large, irregular pieces, and revenue can swing sharply when a customer delays acceptance. Data Patterns' Q1 FY27 illustrates it: revenue rose 16.8%, but delays in customer approvals shifted some revenue to later quarters and the margin fell to 27% from 32%. Astra's revenue fell 11.5% in the same quarter even as it booked a ₹2,205 cr order. For these companies, one quarter says little; the year and the order book say more.

Why margins are higher than in the rest of defence

  • Design content: much of the value is in software and design, not materials.
  • Less imported pass-through: unlike an aircraft maker passing through a foreign engine, electronics firms add more of the value themselves.
  • Upgrades: electronics are refreshed several times during a platform's life, creating repeat business.

5b · Air defence and QRSAM

Why one order decides BEL's year

BEL's FY27 guidance of ₹55,000 cr+ in new orders includes the Quick Reaction Surface-to-Air Missile (QRSAM) system, a DRDO-developed short-range air-defence system valued at about ₹30,000 cr, which BEL expects to receive by September 2026. Without it, BEL's inflow would be far below the prior year's ₹30,045 cr; with it, the order book would rise well above ₹90,000 cr. That single contract explains why BEL's stock has been flat while smaller peers rallied: its Q1 inflow fell 51% to ₹3,750 cr, and the market is waiting.

QRSAM matters for the small caps too. Apollo Micro Systems expects ₹2,500–3,000 cr of orders from QRSAM and other programmes by the end of FY27, and suppliers of radar modules, processors and power electronics will receive sub-orders once BEL's contract is signed. Air defence gained urgency after Operation Sindoor in May 2025, when Indian air-defence networks faced drones and missiles; since then, counter-drone systems and short-range air defence have moved up the procurement list.

The paradox of Indian defence electronics is that the systems are increasingly designed in India but the most important chips are not. Gallium and germanium — used in radar amplifiers and thermal optics — are materials China has restricted since 2023, and advanced processors come mainly from the US. Companies that secure multi-year supply, or qualify alternative sources, reduce a risk that does not appear in their order books.

7 · The business calendar

A back-ended year

  • April–May: BEL announces order-inflow and revenue guidance with fourth-quarter results; the market resets expectations for the year.
  • April–June: the smallest quarter for most companies; margins often look weak.
  • July–December: orders accumulate; large contracts (such as QRSAM) may be signed.
  • 1 February: the budget. Expectations are usually priced in beforehand.
  • January–March: the biggest quarter for deliveries and billing as the Ministry spends its budget.

For analysis, compare full years rather than quarters, and look at order inflow over rolling twelve months. A weak first quarter — like Data Patterns' or Astra's in FY27 — is common in this industry and should be judged against the year's guidance.

Control: small caps as a whole (Nifty Smallcap 100) beat the Nifty in April in 11 of 15 years, by an average of 3.0 points, and lag in January–February. Part of any April strength is this market-wide effect. After removing it, this industry's own April edge is about +5.9 points.

Reading it: before 2019 the basket is BEL and Astra Microwave only; Apollo joins in 2019, Data Patterns and Paras in 2022, C2C in 2025. The calendar is the strongest in the Defence family so far. January–March has beaten the Nifty in only 2 of 15 years (median −4.5 points) and February–March in 5 of 15. April has worked in 12 of 15 (average +8.9) and April–July in 10 of 15 (median +19.9). August–September has worked in only 3 of 14. The pattern matches the business: budget expectations are priced in before 1 February, while April and May bring BEL's order-inflow guidance and fourth-quarter results, when BEL and Data Patterns book their largest quarters.

A tendency, not a rule: small samples, survivorship bias (only names still listed) and equal weighting. Use it for timing context, not as a signal.

The exceptions, and why

  • January–March worked only in 2015 (+16.8) and 2026 (+3.1). Its worst years were 2018 (−25.6) and 2025 (−18.6).
  • April–July failed in 2018 (−21.4), 2013 (−9.7), 2012 (−4.7), 2019 (−2.8) and 2016 (−1.3); its best runs were 2014 (+86.4) and 2020 (+61.6).
  • August–September worked only in 2022 (+20.7), 2025 (+16.0) and 2023; it lost in 11 of 14 years.
How to use this

This is the cleanest calendar in the Defence family: two of fifteen January–March wins is hard to dismiss, and the business logic — budget hopes priced in, then April guidance and fourth-quarter results — is sound. The caveat is that 2012–2018 rests on two stocks (BEL and Astra). Use it for timing entries and exits around a view, not as a view in itself.

9 · What moves margins

Eight drivers

  1. Large orders. A single contract such as QRSAM can change a year's inflow.
  2. Design ownership. Own designs earn more than build-to-print.
  3. Product mix. Systems and upgrades earn more than components.
  4. Customer acceptance timing. Delays shift revenue between quarters.
  5. Engineer costs. Wage inflation squeezes margins when revenue slips.
  6. Imported chips. Prices, licences and Chinese controls on gallium and germanium.
  7. Exports. Radars, EW and optics are among India's growing defence exports.
  8. Valuation. FII flows and index inclusion have driven 2026's moves more than earnings.

Basket: BEL, Data Patterns, Astra Microwave, Apollo Micro Systems, Paras Defence and C2C, equal-weighted monthly; BEL and Astra only before 2019. Sigma, Precision Electronics and Uravi are excluded (see section 4). Monthly returns capped at ±50%, price only; 2026 to 18 September.

The anatomy of the cycles

  1. The quiet PSU years (2012–2013): BEL traded as a cash-rich, slow-growing government company.
  2. Make in India, first wave (2014–2017): BEL +185% in 2014; steady gains after.
  3. The margin scare (2018–2019): BEL −52% in 2018 on worries about pricing of government-nominated orders and the small-cap crash.
  4. The long re-rating (2020–2025): indigenisation lists, new listings, the Ukraine war and Operation Sindoor. The basket beat the Nifty in every year from 2020 to 2025.
  5. The split (2026): small caps re-rated on FII buying; BEL paused; the weakest small caps (C2C) broke.

Patterns that repeat

  • Six straight years of outperformance (2020–2025), the longest run in the Defence family, reflect real earnings growth at BEL as well as re-rating.
  • Small caps move two to three times as much as BEL, in both directions: Apollo +290% in 2023, C2C −58% in 2026.
  • Listing surges fade. Paras fell 19% in 2022 after its listing surge; C2C and Uravi fell 78–81% from their early highs.

The wars in Ukraine and the Middle East, and India's own Operation Sindoor, showed that drones, jamming and air defence decide outcomes as much as aircraft and tanks. That has shifted procurement budgets toward sensors, electronic warfare and counter-drone systems worldwide. Indian companies are small by global standards — BEL's revenue is roughly an eighth of Thales's — but India's domestic reservation policy gives them a protected home market in which to build scale before competing abroad.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. AESA radars. Thousands of small transmit–receive modules replace one large transmitter; Astra's Uttam order shows the demand for these modules.
  2. Gallium nitride. GaN amplifiers give more power and range; they depend on a material China controls.
  3. Counter-drone systems. Cheap drones need cheap defences: detectors, jammers, guns and lasers.
  4. Software-defined everything. Radios, radars and EW systems that change function through software updates.
  5. Sensor fusion and AI. Combining radar, optical and electronic data into one picture for faster decisions.
  6. Space. Satellite payloads and ground systems; Astra and Paras already sell to the space sector (see 061).

Each shift increases the share of software and design in the product, which favours companies that own their intellectual property and invest in R&D. BEL's R&D budget of over ₹2,200 cr and Data Patterns' in-house designs are their main defences against becoming build-to-print contractors.

What is priced in

BEL at about 47 times FY26 profit is priced for its guidance — 15%+ growth at a 28%+ margin — and for QRSAM arriving on time. A delay would disappoint; a signing would lift the order book to more than three years of revenue. The small caps are priced far higher on current earnings: Paras, for example, trades at over 120 times its Q1 profit annualised. Their prices assume that 2026's order wins become revenue quickly and that margins recover — assumptions that Data Patterns' and Astra's Q1 results have already tested.

The honest read

The industry is excellent and the policy support durable; the valuations of the small caps are the risk. The stocks rallied on flows while two of the four largest small caps reported disappointing quarters. BEL is the lower-risk way to own the theme and has lagged; the small caps offer more growth but will react sharply to any slip in orders or margins, as C2C showed at the extreme.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What share of revenue comes from your own designs, and what from build-to-print?
  2. How much of the order book is signed, and how much is "negotiated" or expected?
  3. Which programmes (QRSAM, Uttam, others) will drive revenue this year, and when?
  4. What is the cause of the quarter's margin change — mix, wages or timing?
  5. How dependent are you on imported processors, GaN chips or germanium, and how much stock is secured?
  6. What are receivable days, and how much is overdue from government customers?
  7. What share of revenue is exports, and in which products?
  8. How much do you spend on R&D, and what new products will it deliver?
  9. For BEL: what inflow is expected without QRSAM?
  10. For acquirers (Apollo, Sigma): how will acquisitions be integrated and financed?

19 · Glossary

Every term you'll meet

Systems

Radar
A sensor that sends radio waves and listens for echoes to find objects.
AESA
Active electronically scanned array — a radar made of many small transmit–receive modules that steer the beam electronically.
Uttam
India's AESA radar for the Tejas fighter.
Electronic warfare (EW)
Using the electromagnetic spectrum to detect, deceive or jam an enemy.
ESM / ELINT
