Coal & Coke

The fuel underneath three-quarters of India's electricity. One state-owned company digs most of it, sells it at prices the government influences, and auctions a slice at whatever the market will pay — and that slice decides the profit. Around it sits a second industry nobody notices: the contractors who move the coal, and who cart away the ash left when it burns.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

A monopoly with an administered price and an auction window

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Most of India's electricity comes from burning coal, and almost all of that coal is dug up by one government-owned company. It sells most of it at a fixed, low price to power stations, because cheap electricity matters to everybody. But it also puts a small share — about one tonne in eight — up for auction, where anyone can bid. Those auction tonnes sell for far more, and that is where most of the company's profit comes from. So the interesting question is never how much coal is dug; it is how much is auctioned, and how badly people want it.

Say it to a client in one minute

This is a regulated monopoly whose earnings turn on a small marginal market.

  • The structure: Coal India supplies power producers under long-term fuel agreements at notified prices, and sells roughly 13% by e-auction at ₹3,085 a tonne against a blended ₹2,338. The spread between the two is the earnings engine.
  • 2026 so far: production fell 7% to 169.63 million tonnes in the June quarter while offtake rose 4% to 197.86 million tonnes — the difference came out of pithead stocks. Revenue rose 8% to ₹46,255 cr and profit was flat at ₹8,852 cr.
  • Competition is finally arriving. Captive and commercial mines produced 210.46 million tonnes to March 2026, up 10.2%, and the 16th auction round opened in September 2026.
  • Two new listings: Bharat Coking Coal in January, up 96% on debut, and Caliber Mining in July.
  • Most of this section is not mining. Of twelve names, one is a large miner, one a coking-coal subsidiary, and the rest are coal traders, transporters and ash handlers.

Analogy

Imagine a government dairy that must sell most of its milk to schools at a fixed low price, but is allowed to auction one crate in eight at the gate. The schools take the volume; the auction sets the profit. And because milk cannot be stored, a third business exists purely to move it — and, here, to cart away what is left once it is used.

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Blocks: the government decides who is allowed to mine which patch of ground.
  • Mining: most Indian coal is near the surface, so earth is stripped off the top and the coal scooped out.
  • Washing: Indian coal has a lot of stone and ash in it, which has to be cleaned out for steel-making.
  • Moving: coal is heavy and cheap, so transport costs a lot relative to the coal itself — the railway is the bottleneck.
  • Selling: most goes to power stations at a set price; a little is auctioned to the highest bidder.
  • Ash: after burning, a huge amount of fine grey powder is left, and somebody has to take it away.
Fund-manager lens

Three things decide the miner's profit, and volume is the least of them. E-auction share and premium, because that is the only market-priced tonne — 13% of offtake at ₹3,085 against a ₹2,338 blend. Notified price revisions, which are political and infrequent. And cost per tonne, dominated by wages under long-term agreements and by the stripping ratio as mines deepen. A quarter in which production falls 7%, offtake rises 4% and profit is flat tells you the company sold stock it had already paid to mine — good for cash, and not repeatable.

Reading the June quarter properly

Coal India produced 169.63 million tonnes and sold 197.86 million — it sold 28 million tonnes more than it dug. That is a stockpile drawdown, and it flatters both revenue and cash flow because the cost of mining those tonnes was incurred in an earlier period. Revenue rose 8%, blended realisation improved by about ₹90 a tonne, and profit still finished flat at ₹8,852 cr, which tells you costs rose to absorb the gain. For a business whose costs are dominated by a large unionised workforce on long-term wage agreements and by the rising expense of stripping deeper seams, flat profit on higher realisation is the normal state of affairs unless e-auction premiums widen.

Analogy

A shopkeeper who sells more than he buys for a quarter looks busier and richer. He has simply emptied the storeroom, and next quarter he must either dig faster or sell less. Coal India's stockpile has been the shock absorber for the Indian power system for years — which is a public good and a private cost.

7 · The business calendar

Stockpile in, stockpile out

  • April–June: peak power demand as summer cooling load climbs; stockpiles are drawn down and e-auction premiums typically firm.
  • July–September: the monsoon floods opencast mines and production falls sharply — August is the worst month in the record for these stocks.
  • October–December: production recovers hard as the industry races to rebuild stocks before winter; the largest output quarter.
  • January–March: the year-end production push to meet annual targets, followed historically by weak share prices.
  • Auction rounds and price notifications: not seasonal — the 16th commercial auction round opened in September 2026, and notified price revisions arrive when the government decides.

  • Strongest months vs the Nifty: Apr +4.8 pts (beat in 11 of 15 years); Sep +4.6 pts (beat in 11 of 15 years); Jun +3.8 pts (beat in 7 of 15 years).
  • Weakest months: Aug -4.1 pts (beat in 3 of 16 years); Oct -3.3 pts (beat in 3 of 15 years); Jan -2.4 pts (beat in 5 of 15 years).
  • Windows: Feb–Mar beat the Nifty in 9 of 15 years (median +0.7 pts); Apr–Jul beat the Nifty in 10 of 15 years (median +7.8 pts); Aug–Sep beat the Nifty in 6 of 14 years (median -2.4 pts); Oct–Dec beat the Nifty in 6 of 14 years (median -0.9 pts); Sep–Nov beat the Nifty in 9 of 14 years (median +4.9 pts); Jan–Mar beat the Nifty in 5 of 15 years (median -4.4 pts).

Control: small caps as a whole (Nifty Smallcap 100) beat the Nifty in April in 11 of 15 years, by an average of 3.0 points, and lag in January–February. Part of any April strength is this market-wide effect. After removing it, this industry's own April edge is about +1.8 points.

Reading it: the pattern follows the power-demand year rather than the mining year. April is the best month (11 of 15, +4.8 points) and September equal-best (11 of 15, +4.6), matching peak summer power demand and the post-monsoon restocking that follows flooded mines. April–July works 10 of 15 with a median of +7.8, and September–November 9 of 14. The weak stretch is August (3 of 16, −4.1 points), when the monsoon floods pits and production collapses, and January–March (5 of 15, median −4.4).

A tendency, not a rule: small samples, survivorship bias (only names still listed) and equal weighting. Use it for timing context, not as a signal.

The exceptions, and why

  • April–July worked in 10 of 15 years (median +7.8), with failures in 2017 (−27.8), 2018 (−20.2), 2020 (−14.6), 2014 (−13.8) and 2012 (−3.2) — all years when either the power cycle or the state-enterprise mood was against it.
  • The best readings were 2023 (+74.8) and 2019 (+56.1), the first a genuine coal squeeze, the second a micro-cap run in Refex.
  • August is the clearest negative in the series: 3 of 16 years, −4.1 points on average, with 2019 at −24.2 and 2015 at −17.0. The monsoon is visible in the share prices.
  • January–March has failed in ten of fifteen years, with 2012's −41.6 the worst reading in this entire primer series.
How to use this

The April-to-July strength and the August weakness both have physical causes — summer power demand and monsoon flooding — which makes this one of the more trustworthy seasonal patterns in the family. The caution is the basket: three stocks, one of which tripled in 2024 on an unrelated ash-handling story. For a view on coal itself, track Coal India's monthly production and offtake releases, which are published well before the quarterly results and tell you most of what the results will say.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Eight drivers

  1. Power demand: roughly three-quarters of India's electricity is coal-fired, so peak-season demand drives offtake (see 037).
  2. E-auction premium: the only market-priced tonne, and the swing factor in profit.
  3. Notified price revisions: infrequent, political, and large when they happen.
  4. Production and evacuation: rakes, not reserves, are usually the constraint.
  5. Imported coal prices: when seaborne coal is dear, domestic e-auction premiums widen — the 2022 pattern.
  6. Commercial mining supply: captive and commercial output reached 210.46 mt, up 10.2%, and the 16th auction round is under way.
  7. Wage agreements and stripping ratios: the cost side, which rises structurally as mines deepen.
  8. Renewable displacement: solar and wind take the marginal unit of daytime generation first (see 038).

The anatomy of a coal cycle

  1. Power demand outruns supply — a hot summer, a weak monsoon for hydro, or an import price spike.
  2. Stockpiles fall and e-auction premiums widen, because the marginal industrial buyer has nowhere else to go. Profit rises fast.
  3. Production is pushed up and imports become affordable again; premiums narrow.
  4. Earnings normalise while costs — wages, stripping — carry on rising, so the next flat year arrives quickly. 2025 and 2026 are that phase.

Patterns that repeat

  • Coal India underperformed for most of the 2010s — negative in 2016, 2017, 2018, 2019 and 2020 — because price control capped the upside of a monopoly.
  • The 2022–24 run was an energy-shock re-rating, not a volume story: +54%, +67%, +2%.
  • Small caps in this section move on their own stories: Refex +300% in 2024 on ash handling, then −46% in 2025.
  • The stockpile is the buffer. In 2020 it built up and the stock fell 36%; in 2026 it is being drawn down and profits are flat.

11 · The global picture

The one large economy still adding coal

While most rich countries are retiring coal plants, India is still building them, because electricity demand is growing faster than renewables can be added and because coal is the only fuel the country has in abundance. That makes India, with China, the centre of the remaining global coal trade. The country still imports substantial volumes — chiefly from Indonesia for thermal coal and Australia for coking coal — because domestic coal is high in ash and because coastal power plants were designed for imported grades. The strategic direction is explicit: expand domestic production to displace imports, which is what commercial mining auctions are for, with captive and commercial output reaching 210.46 million tonnes and the 16th auction round offering 25 more blocks.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • Nationalisation to monopoly (1970s): private coal mining was nationalised, creating Coal India and the structure that still defines the industry.
  • Captive blocks, cancellation and re-auction (2014): the Supreme Court cancelled earlier allocations, and a transparent auction regime replaced them.
  • Commercial mining (2020 onwards): for the first time since nationalisation, any company may mine and sell coal freely — the slow unwinding of the monopoly, now visible at 210 million tonnes.

12 · Where the industry is going

Six shifts

  1. Commercial and captive mining scale-up: 12 blocks operationalised in FY26 adding over 86 million tonnes of annual capacity.
  2. First-mile connectivity: conveyors and silos replacing road haulage from pit to railhead, cutting cost and dust.
  3. Coal gasification: converting coal to syngas for chemicals and fertiliser, with policy support but unproven economics.
  4. Washing and beneficiation: reducing ash content to raise plant efficiency and cut transport of waste.
  5. Ash utilisation: a regulated obligation turning a waste problem into a services industry — road embankments, cement, mine backfill.
  6. Diversification by the miner itself: Coal India investing in renewables and critical minerals as its core business faces a long-term ceiling.

The honest read

Coal India is doing something quietly important: it is meeting rising demand out of inventory while its own production falls, which is sustainable for a few quarters and not for a few years. The bull case has always been the e-auction premium, and at ₹3,085 against a ₹2,338 blend it is still healthy but no longer widening. The structural question is the one the market has been slow to price: commercial mining is now producing 210 million tonnes and growing at 10% a year, which is the first real competition the company has faced since nationalisation, and it arrives at the same time as renewables take the daytime margin (see 038). Against that, coal still fuels most of India's electricity and demand is growing. The result is a business that should be valued as a mature cash generator with a long, slow ceiling — which is roughly where a 24% drawdown from the 2024 peak and a ₹5.50 quarterly dividend leave it. The two things to avoid are reading the basket as a mining sector, and assuming the ash-handling contractor's economics have anything to do with the miner's.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What is the production plan by subsidiary, and what caused the shortfall against target?
  2. What is the current pithead stock, and how many more quarters can offtake exceed production?
  3. What share of volume went to e-auction, and at what premium to notified price?
  4. When is the next notified price revision expected, and what is the cost inflation it must cover?
  5. What is cost per tonne, split between wages, contractual mining and stripping?
  6. What is the stripping ratio trend as existing mines deepen?
  7. What is the capital expenditure on first-mile connectivity and washeries?
  8. How much capital is being allocated away from coal, and at what expected return?
  9. (Services companies) What is the order book, and who is the counterparty?

Red flags

  • Offtake exceeding production for several consecutive quarters.
  • Flat profit on rising realisations — cost inflation absorbing the gain.
  • Diversification capital into unrelated businesses at undisclosed returns.
  • Receivables from state distribution companies stretching (see 037).

18 · Myths and live debates

What you'll hear, and the evidence

ClaimEvidence
"Coal India is a monopoly, so it must be a great business."It was negative in five consecutive years from 2016 to 2020, because a monopoly whose prices are set by its owner is not a price-setter.
"Coal is in terminal decline in India."Not yet. It still fuels around three-quarters of generation, offtake rose 4% this quarter, and commercial mining is adding capacity, not retiring it.
"Record offtake means the business is growing."Offtake exceeded production by 28 million tonnes. That is a stockpile drawdown, and it reverses.
"Commercial mining will not dent the monopoly."Captive and commercial mines produced 210.46 mt to March 2026, growing 10.2% — already a meaningful share, and 12 more blocks were operationalised in FY26.
"The coal sector is up 14% this year."The median stock is up 5%, and two of the three basket members are logistics and ash-handling companies rather than miners.
"Ash handling is a coal business."It is a regulated services business with contracts, order books and receivables — Refex tripled in 2024 and halved thereafter for reasons unconnected to the coal price.

19 · Glossary

Every term you'll meet

Mining

Opencast mining
Removing surface earth to reach a shallow coal seam; most Indian coal is mined this way.
Underground mining
Shafts and galleries to reach deeper seams; costlier and a smaller share of Indian output.
Overburden
The earth and rock above a coal seam that must be stripped away.
Stripping ratio
Tonnes of overburden moved per tonne of coal produced; it rises as a mine deepens, and with it the cost.
Seam
The layer of coal within the rock.
Coal block
A defined area allocated or auctioned for mining.
Captive mine
A block mined for the owner's own plant rather than for sale.
Commercial mining
Mining coal for open sale, permitted since 2020 — the end of the pure monopoly.
Mine opening permission (MOP)
The final clearance to begin production; 12 blocks received it in FY26.
Washery
A plant that removes ash and stone from raw coal.
Beneficiation
Any process that raises coal quality before use.
Grade and gross calorific value
The energy content per tonne, which determines the notified price.
Dragline
The very large excavator used to strip overburden in opencast mines; its availability sets production capacity.
Mine development operator (MDO)
A contractor engaged to develop and run a mine on the block owner's behalf; how most new captive mines are built.
Ash content
The non-combustible share of a coal by weight. Indian coal is high-ash, which is why washing and ash disposal matter so much here.
Sponge iron
Direct-reduced iron made using coal rather than coke; a significant non-power buyer of e-auction coal.

Products and market

Thermal coal
Coal burned to generate electricity or process heat.
Coking coal
Coal with the properties needed to make coke for steelmaking; India is largely import-dependent.
Washed coal
Coal cleaned to lower ash content.
Coke
Coal baked in the absence of air, used as a reductant in blast furnaces.
Fly ash
The fine residue from burning coal, which plants are obliged to have removed and utilised.
Fuel supply agreement (FSA)
A long-term contract supplying a power plant at notified prices.
Notified price
The administered price for FSA coal, set by grade.
E-auction
The competitive sale of a slice of output — about 13% of offtake, at ₹3,085 a tonne in Q1 FY27.
E-auction premium
The gap between auction and notified prices; the main swing factor in profit.
Blended realisation
The weighted average price across all channels — ₹2,338 a tonne this quarter.
Offtake
Coal actually despatched to customers, as distinct from coal produced.
Pithead stock
Coal mined but not yet despatched; the industry's buffer.
Evacuation
Moving coal from mine to customer, usually by rail.
First-mile connectivity
Conveyors and silos replacing road haulage between pit and railhead.
Rake
A full trainload of wagons; rake availability is the usual bottleneck.

Policy and commercial

Nationalisation
The 1970s takeover of private coal mines that created Coal India.
Auction round

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. Where does Coal India's profit actually come from?

The e-auction slice — about 13% of offtake at ₹3,085 a tonne against a blended ₹2,338. Most volume goes out at notified prices under fuel supply agreements.

2. How did production fall 7% while offtake rose 4%?

The company sold 28 million tonnes more than it mined, drawing down pithead stocks. That flatters cash and revenue, and cannot continue indefinitely.

3. Why did Coal India underperform for most of the 2010s?

A monopoly whose prices are set by its owner is not a price-setter. It fell in five consecutive years from 2016 to 2020.

4. What is commercial mining, and how big is it now?

Mining coal for open sale, allowed since 2020. Captive and commercial mines produced 210.46 million tonnes to March 2026, up 10.2%.

5. What happened in January 2026?

Bharat Coking Coal, Coal India's coking-coal subsidiary and 58.5% of India's domestic coking coal output, listed at ₹45 against a ₹23 issue price — a 96% premium on a ₹1,071 cr offer for sale.

6. Why is August the worst month for these stocks?

The monsoon floods opencast pits and production collapses. August has beaten the Nifty in only 3 of 16 years.

7. What does Refex Industries actually do?

Ash and coal handling — moving over 70,000 tonnes of ash a day for 40 power plants, on a ₹1,635 cr order book. It is a services company, not a miner.

8. What made 2022 and 2023 such strong years?

Global energy prices spiked, imported coal became unaffordable, and domestic coal was the cheapest energy available — widening e-auction premiums.

9. What is the structural ceiling on this industry?

Renewables taking the marginal daytime unit of generation, plus commercial mining competing away the monopoly — while coal demand itself still grows.

10. Why should the basket return be treated with care?

It contains three companies — a miner, a coal logistics firm and an ash contractor. The median stock is up 5% against a basket up 14%.
