1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Sun, sea and a lot of patience

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Salt farmers in Gujarat dig shallow, flat ponds called salt pans. They fill them with seawater or salty water pumped from underground and let the sun do the work. Over weeks the water evaporates, and white salt crystals are left behind, which workers rake into big heaps. Most of this salt isn't eaten. It goes to factories that split it into caustic soda and chlorine, or make washing soda and glass. The salt you eat is cleaned, has iodine added (so your thyroid gland works properly) and is packed with a brand name. Raw salt costs a rupee or two a kilo at the pan; a packet of branded salt costs over ₹25.

Say it to a client in one minute

Indian salt is a solar-evaporation industry concentrated in Gujarat (coastal pans and the Rann of Kutch), with Rajasthan (Sambhar, Nawa) and Tamil Nadu (Tuticorin) behind it. About two-thirds of domestic demand is industrial: the chlor-alkali and soda-ash plants in 003. Edible salt must be iodised. The branded market is led by Tata Salt (Tata Consumer; 40%+ of branded salt), with Aashirvaad, Annapurna, Catch and Nirma behind. Raw salt is worth ₹2–3 per kg at the works; refining, iodising and branding multiply that about ten times.

The two listed salt companies (Goyal Salt, Saboo Sodium Chloro) are small refiners and packers, both 57–65% below their peaks. The real salt plays sit elsewhere:

  • Archean: industrial salt exports plus bromine from the same brine.
  • Tata Chemicals and GHCL: salt turned into soda ash.
  • Tata Consumer: Tata Salt.

Analogy

Salt is like water: free from the sky, cheap from a tap, expensive in a branded bottle. The pan is the sky, the refinery is the tap, and Tata Salt is the branded bottle. Most of the price you pay is for the bottle and the name.

Everyday examples

The chlorine that disinfects city water and the caustic soda in your soap both started as Gujarat salt (see 003). So did the soda ash in window glass. The salt in a packet of chips is vacuum salt from a food-grade refinery. The coarse salt poured on icy roads in Korea or Japan may have been shipped from Kandla.

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Brine: seawater has about 3.5% salt. Underground water in the Rann of Kutch is much saltier, because an ancient sea dried up there.
  • Evaporation: the water moves from pond to pond, getting saltier each time. First, chalky gypsum settles out. Then, when the water is about 8 times saltier than the sea, clean salt crystals form. The bitter water left at the end (bittern) holds bromine, magnesium and potash.
  • Refining: the heaps are washed to remove mud, spun dry, crushed, mixed with a tiny amount of iodine and packed.
  • Industrial use: a chlor-alkali plant dissolves the salt again and splits it with electricity into chlorine and caustic soda (see 003).
  • Brands: the same salt, sealed in a bright packet with a trusted name, sells for ten times more.
Fund-manager lens

Salt is a textbook case of value sitting away from the raw material. Owning pans earns little unless you use the salt yourself (soda ash, chlor-alkali, bromine from the bittern) or put a trusted brand on it. That's why the best "salt stocks" aren't in this watchlist, and why Archean, which mines both salt and bromine from the same brine, is the closest listed pure play.

4 · How a salt pan works

Fractional crystallisation in the open air

StageBrine strength (°Bé)What happensWhy it matters
Reservoir~3–10Seawater or subsoil brine is stored and starts concentratingCheap evaporation area
Condensers~10–25Calcium sulphate (gypsum) and iron and calcium impurities drop outClean brine means purer salt
Crystallisers~25–29Sodium chloride crystallises; workers harvest itThe product
Bittern>29Magnesium, potassium and bromide salts concentrateRaw material for bromine, magnesium chloride and potash (see 004)

A well-run works harvests only in the 25–29 °Bé window, because salt harvested beyond it picks up bitter magnesium salts. Large integrated works (Archean, Tata Chemicals) turn the bittern into bromine and sulphate of potash; small works discard it. That is the difference between a salt business and a marine-chemicals business.

Analogy

It's like boiling down sugarcane juice for jaggery. Different things come out at different stages, and the skill is knowing exactly when to scoop. The leftover syrup (bittern) is valuable if you have a use for it.

The cost structure of a salt works

  • Land: mostly government land leased for long periods; lease renewal is a recurring risk.
  • Pumping: subsoil brine in the Rann is pumped with diesel or, increasingly, solar pumps; diesel costs matter.
  • Labour: harvesting and heaping are still labour-intensive in small works; large works mechanise.
  • Freight: salt is cheap and heavy, so rail and road freight to chemical plants or ports can exceed its value.

Why chemical plants care about salt quality

A chlor-alkali or soda-ash plant uses roughly 1.5–1.7 tonnes of salt for every tonne of caustic soda or soda ash it makes, so salt is a large share of its raw-material bill. Price is not the only thing it buys on. Modern membrane cells (see 003) need brine almost free of calcium and magnesium, because traces of them clog the membranes. So buyers pay more for washed salt with high sodium chloride content (typically 98.5% or more) and low calcium, magnesium and sulphate. That is why large users run their own salt works and washeries (Tata Chemicals at Mithapur, GHCL near Sutrapada, Nirma in Kutch), and why good-quality export salt from Gujarat commands a premium over crude salt.

The people behind the pans

Much of India's inland salt still comes from Agariya families in the Little Rann of Kutch. They move into the desert after the monsoon, pump subsoil brine into hand-built pans for about eight months, and leave before the Rann floods again. Their costs are diesel (increasingly replaced by state-subsidised solar pumps), labour and transport; their selling price is set by traders and large buyers. Land rights inside the Wild Ass Sanctuary, health and education are recurring policy issues. For an investor, this fragmented base is why crude-salt prices stay low: thousands of small producers with little bargaining power sell into a few big buyers.

Analogy

A salt pan is like a solar farm that produces a product instead of electricity. The sun is free, the land and pumps cost money, and the profit depends on how far you have to carry the product to someone who needs it.

8 · The business calendar

A dry-season industry

  • October–June: the production season. Pans are prepared after the monsoon and harvested through the dry months, with peak output in March–May.
  • June–September (monsoon): production stops, and rain can wash away unsold heaps. Cyclones on the Gujarat coast (Tauktae 2021, Biparjoy 2023) can damage pans and stocks.
  • The Little Rann of Kutch: Agariya families move into the Rann after the monsoon and leave before it floods again.
  • Demand: edible demand is steady all year; industrial demand follows chlor-alkali and soda-ash operating rates; exports peak in the dry season.

Reading it: treat this as noise. Before 2024 the basket is a single tiny, thinly traded stock (Saboo Sodium Chloro, ~₹49 cr); Goyal Salt listed only in October 2023. A tendency, not a rule: small samples, survivorship bias (only names still listed) and equal weighting. Use it for timing context, not as a signal.

The exceptions, and why

  • March beat the Nifty in only 3 of 15 years (−26.7 in 2022, −23.4 in 2020, −18.8 in 2018). These are micro-cap sell-offs, not salt events.
  • April–July beat the Nifty in 9 of 15 years; the misses (2018 −34.6, 2019 −24.9) were the micro-cap crash years.
  • The largest single months (October 2017 +44.4, October 2021 +36.1) came during micro-cap manias.
How to use this

Don't. A basket of one or two micro caps has no reliable seasonality. Salt's economic season (dry-season output, cyclone risk) shows up in Archean's, Tata Chemicals' and GHCL's results, not in these two stocks.

10 · What moves demand and margins

Seven drivers

  1. Weather: a long dry season means more salt; unseasonal rain and cyclones cut output and destroy heaps.
  2. Chlor-alkali and soda-ash operating rates: the biggest demand source (see 003). New caustic and soda-ash capacity in Gujarat raises industrial salt demand.
  3. Export demand and freight: Chinese, Japanese, Korean and Southeast Asian chemical demand; ocean and inland freight.
  4. Logistics: rail rake availability, road condition and diesel prices to ports.
  5. Land and labour: lease renewals, wildlife-sanctuary rules in the Little Rann, wages and worker welfare.
  6. Branded competition: distribution reach and pricing of the big brands for edible salt; growth in value-added salts (low-sodium, rock salt).
  7. Regulation: iodisation rules (FSSAI), and any action on microplastics or sodium reduction.

A longer history

  • 1930: Gandhi's Dandi March against the British salt tax (he made salt at Dandi on 6 April) made salt a symbol of independence.
  • 1983: Tata Chemicals launched Tata Salt, India's first packaged iodised salt; branded salt became an FMCG category.
  • 1998–2006: the ban on non-iodised salt for eating was imposed, lifted in 2000 and restored in 2005. Iodised salt now reaches ~94% of households (NFHS-5).
  • 1990s–2000s: salt de-licensed; large works mechanised and turned bittern into bromine.
  • 2017: the small central salt cess was abolished with other minor cesses.
  • 2022–23: record Indian output, driven by strong soda-ash and caustic demand; Archean listed (November 2022).

Patterns that repeat

  • The listed salt stocks follow micro-cap sentiment, not salt output: +285% in 2021 and −61% in 2018 had nothing to do with pans.
  • Industrial salt follows chemicals: soda-ash booms (2021–22) lift salt demand and prices.
  • Weather is the supply shock: cyclones and unseasonal rain, not demand, cause shortages.
  • Value accrues downstream: bromine and brands earn the margins.

12 · The global picture

China, the US and India make most of the world's salt

World salt comes from three methods: solar evaporation (India, Australia, Mexico, China's coast), rock-salt mining (the US, Germany, Canada, Pakistan) and solution mining or vacuum evaporation (Europe, the US, China). Uses differ too. In India and China, salt mostly feeds chemicals; in North America and Europe, a big share goes to de-icing roads in winter.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • Salt as tax to salt as commodity: for centuries governments taxed and monopolised salt (the British in India, the gabelle in France, Chinese dynasties); de-regulation made it a low-value bulk commodity.
  • Asia's chemical boom made solar salt a trade good: Indian, Australian and Mexican works supply Chinese, Japanese and Korean chemical plants.
  • India's export rise: cheap Gujarat solar salt and port access lifted India into the top rank of exporters.

13 · Where technology is going

Five shifts

  1. Solar pumps replacing diesel pumps for subsoil brine in the Rann: lower cost and cleaner, and state-supported.
  2. Mechanised harvesting and washeries: higher purity for chlor-alkali membrane cells (see 003), which need very pure brine.
  3. Bittern valorisation: extracting bromine, magnesium and potash (Archean's model); lithium from brines is a future possibility.
  4. Value-added edible salts: low-sodium, iron-fortified, rock and pink salts, and flavoured salts; premiumisation in a staple.
  5. Microplastic testing: a 2024 Toxics Link study reported microplastics in all Indian salt samples it tested, pushing brands towards better washing and filtration.

18 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What share of volume is industrial vs edible vs export? What are realisations for each?
  2. Do you own your pans or buy crude salt? What is the lease tenure?
  3. How much of the season was lost to rain or cyclones, and what stock is carried into the monsoon?
  4. What is your freight cost per tonne, and how dependent are you on rail rakes?
  5. (Goyal) Why did margins fall as revenue grew 45%? Is growth from lower-margin trading?
  6. (Saboo) How much of profit comes from salt vs the hotel, FMCG and power businesses?

Red flags

  • Revenue growth with falling margins (trading-led growth).
  • Diversification into unrelated businesses at tiny scale.
  • Thin trading and large price moves without news.

19 · Myths and live debates

What you'll hear, and the evidence

ClaimEvidence
"Salt is a defensive staple, so salt stocks are defensive."Demand is steady, but the listed salt stocks fell 61% (2018) and rose 285% (2021): micro-cap behaviour, not staples.
"India's salt dominance is a moat."Salt is abundant and cheap everywhere on a coast; the moat is in brands (Tata) and downstream chemistry (Archean, Tata Chemicals).

21 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. Where does most of India's salt go?

About two-thirds to industry, mainly chlor-alkali and soda-ash plants, plus large exports; edible salt is the smaller share.

2. At what brine strength does salt crystallise, and why stop harvesting beyond it?

About 25–29 °Bé; beyond that, bitter magnesium salts contaminate the product.

3. What is bittern, and who uses it?

The leftover brine rich in bromide, magnesium and potassium; Archean and Tata Chemicals turn it into bromine and potash.

4. Why are the best "salt stocks" outside this watchlist?

Value sits downstream: Archean (salt plus bromine), Tata Chemicals and GHCL (salt plus soda ash) and Tata Consumer (the Tata Salt brand).

5. What must edible salt contain in India?

Iodine: at least 30 ppm at manufacture and 15 ppm at consumer level.

6. What hurts salt supply?

Unseasonal rain and cyclones on the Gujarat coast (Tauktae 2021, Biparjoy 2023), which shorten the season and wash away heaps.

7. Why did Goyal Salt fall 38% in 2026 despite 45% revenue growth?

Its FY26 operating and net profit fell 12–14% as margins thinned.
