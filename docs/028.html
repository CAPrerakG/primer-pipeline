Oil & Gas Exploration

The business of finding hydrocarbons underground and lifting them to the surface. It is the most capital-intensive, most politically exposed and least controllable business in the market: a producer decides how much it spends and roughly how much it lifts, and a global price it cannot influence decides what that is worth. India's particular problem is that it imports close to 90% of its crude and its own output has been shrinking by about 1.1% a year for a decade.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

A price-taker with a depletion problem

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Oil is not a lake underground. It sits inside rock, the way water sits inside a sponge, hundreds of metres below the surface. To get it you first guess where the sponge is — by bouncing sound waves off the rock and reading the echoes — then you drill an expensive hole to find out if you guessed right. Most holes are disappointing. If you do find oil, you can sell every drop, but at a price decided in London and New York, not by you. And the sponge slowly empties: an old field gives less every year, so you have to keep drilling just to stand still. India's sponges are mostly old, which is why the country has to buy nine of every ten barrels it uses from abroad.

Say it to a client in one minute

Upstream is the purest commodity exposure in the Indian market, wrapped in policy.

  • The price is global, the politics are local. Producers keep their realisation only to the extent the government lets them: through royalty and cess, through the old subsidy-sharing regime, and briefly through the windfall levy of 2022–24.
  • 2026 has been a windfall year for earnings. Brent has averaged $88 and traded above $100 in September; ONGC's profit more than doubled to ₹17,034 cr and Oil India's standalone profit more than tripled to a record ₹2,870 cr.
  • Volumes are the problem. ONGC's crude output fell 5.5% and gas about 2% in the June quarter. The market has noticed: ONGC is down 3% this year despite those earnings.
  • The section has changed shape in 2026: Vedanta Oil and Gas — the old Cairn business, anchored on the Barmer field in Rajasthan — listed on 15 June, and Gujarat Natural Resources in April.
  • The policy direction is genuinely more open: the Oilfields Amendment Act of 2025, successive Open Acreage rounds, a deep-water exploration mission and international majors returning to Indian acreage.

Own it for the price, but underwrite the reserves. A producer with falling output in a high-price year is liquidating, not compounding.

Analogy

Think of acreage as a rented orchard. Under nomination, you were given the orchard free but must hand over a share of the fruit and, in bad years, feed the village. Under production sharing, you recover the cost of the ladder before splitting the fruit — which gives you an incentive to buy a very expensive ladder, and gives the government an incentive to audit it. Under revenue sharing, you simply promise a percentage of everything you pick and nobody argues about the ladder. That shift, in 2016, is the single most important structural change in Indian upstream.

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Acreage: the government auctions the right to search a patch of land or seabed.
  • Seismic: you thump the ground or fire air guns at sea and listen to the echoes to draw a picture of the rock.
  • Exploration drilling: you drill to find out. This costs a fortune and usually finds nothing.
  • Development: if you do find something, you spend several years and much more money building the wells and pipes to get it out.
  • Production: oil flows, but less each year, so you keep drilling and pushing water into the rock to keep the pressure up.
  • Sale: you sell at the world price, minus what the government takes in royalty and cess.
Fund-manager lens

Three numbers decide an upstream company, and none of them is profit. Production growth, because a high price on a falling volume is a melting ice cube. Reserve replacement, meaning whether this year's discoveries at least match this year's production. And the government take — royalty, cess, profit petroleum and any levy of the moment — because that is what stands between the realisation and the shareholder. ONGC in Q1 FY27 is the case study: a record pre-tax profit of ₹22,848 cr, and a share price down for the year, because crude oil output fell 5.5%.

Read this before using the section

This section is two industries in one basket. ONGC and Oil India are ₹80,000 cr-plus state enterprises whose profits are a function of crude, gas formulas and government take. Antelopus Selan, Hindustan Oil Exploration and Gujarat Natural Resources are small independents whose share prices are driven by individual wells, block awards and, frequently, liquidity. The 2026 basket return of +40% against a median of +14% is exactly that distortion: one ₹4,100 cr stock up 169% pulls the average away from what the industry actually did. Use the median, and read the two groups separately.

The subsidy years, and why they still matter

For most of the decade before 2014, Indian upstream did not enjoy high crude prices; it paid for them. Petrol, diesel, kerosene and cooking gas were sold below cost by the state refiners, and the resulting under-recovery was shared between the government and the upstream producers through discounts that were decided after the fact. That is why the basket rose just 15% in 2012 with Brent at $112, and why deregulation — petrol in 2010, diesel in October 2014 — mattered more to these shares than the oil price did. The windfall levy of July 2022, withdrawn in December 2024, was a smaller echo of the same idea: when crude spikes, the state has repeatedly found a way to take a share of it. Any model of an Indian producer must therefore include a political variable, not just a price deck.

Analogy

Owning an Indian producer is like owning a farm where the crop price is set globally but the government may declare, in a bumper year, that a share of the harvest belongs to the nation's kitchen. The farm is still worth owning; it is simply worth less at the very top of the price cycle than the arithmetic suggests.

7 · The business calendar

Weather, formulas and auctions

  • The monsoon (June–September): offshore activity in the Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal is constrained; ONGC cited adverse offshore conditions in the June quarter.
  • Gas price resets: the administered formula is revised half-yearly, on 1 April and 1 October, which moves gas revenue in steps rather than continuously.
  • Bid rounds: OALP and DSF rounds run on their own calendar and have repeatedly been extended — Round X was deferred four times.
  • OPEC+ meetings and geopolitics: the single biggest source of price news, and entirely outside the industry's control.
  • Budget day: cess, royalty and levy changes are announced here, which is why February can be an anxious month for the sector.

8 · What the stocks do

Seasonality test on TradingView prices

Basket: 4 names (Oil & Natural Gas Corp. Ltd., Oil India Limited, Antelopus Selan Energy Ltd, Hindustan Oil Exploration Co., Ltd.), equal-weighted, monthly from Jul 2011, compared with the Nifty 50.

  • Strongest months vs the Nifty: Apr +4.5 pts (beat in 11 of 15 years); Jan +2.7 pts (beat in 9 of 15 years); Jun +1.3 pts (beat in 9 of 15 years).
  • Weakest months: Nov -3.4 pts (beat in 5 of 15 years); Jul -1.5 pts (beat in 5 of 16 years); Feb -1.4 pts (beat in 7 of 15 years).
  • Windows: Feb–Mar beat the Nifty in 6 of 15 years (median -1.4 pts); Apr–Jul beat the Nifty in 9 of 15 years (median +3.8 pts); Aug–Sep beat the Nifty in 7 of 14 years (median +0.5 pts); Oct–Dec beat the Nifty in 5 of 14 years (median -2.7 pts); Sep–Nov beat the Nifty in 5 of 14 years (median -4.6 pts); Jan–Mar beat the Nifty in 7 of 15 years (median -3.2 pts).

Control: small caps as a whole (Nifty Smallcap 100) beat the Nifty in April in 11 of 15 years, by an average of 3.0 points, and lag in January–February. Part of any April strength is this market-wide effect. After removing it, this industry's own April edge is about +1.5 points.

Reading it: weaker and noisier than the industrial sections, because the real driver is a global price nobody in India sets. April is the one dependable month (11 of 15 years, +4.5 points) and January the second (9 of 15). The negatives cluster in the last quarter: November is the worst month (5 of 15, −3.4), October–December beat the Nifty in 5 of 14 years (median −2.7) and September–November in 5 of 14 (median −4.6). April–July works 9 of 15 with a median of +3.8, but its failures are severe (2017 −17.7, 2013 −15.4, 2022 −13.6). Treat all of it as secondary to the crude price and the policy calendar.

A tendency, not a rule: small samples, survivorship bias (only names still listed) and equal weighting. Use it for timing context, not as a signal.

The exceptions, and why

  • April–July failed in six of fifteen years, and the failures are large: 2017 (−17.7), 2013 (−15.4), 2022 (−13.6), 2019 (−9.3), 2018 (−7.9). This is a weaker seasonal than anything in the Auto family, because a single OPEC decision can overwhelm it.
  • The best April–July runs were 2024 (+38.0) and 2023 (+26.7), both years of state-enterprise re-rating rather than crude strength.
  • The fourth quarter is consistently poor: November alone has beaten the Nifty in 5 of 15 years, with 2014 (−18.0), 2018 (−15.4) and 2025 (−13.4) the worst — all three were quarters in which crude fell sharply.
  • January is unexpectedly good (9 of 15, +2.7 points), with 2026 (+19.6), 2022 (+19.2) and 2024 (+11.6) the standouts — in each case a crude or policy story that ran into the new year.
How to use this

Lightly. With four names in the basket and two of them state enterprises whose re-ratings dominate whole years, the monthly pattern here is far more fragile than in an industry with twenty independent companies. The honest use is as a tiebreaker: if you like the crude view and the valuation, April–July is a better entry window than October–December. Nothing in this table should override the crude price, the production trend or the policy calendar.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Eight drivers

  1. Crude prices: Brent averaged $88 in 2026 against $68 in 2025; every dollar drops almost straight to pre-tax profit.
  2. The rupee: revenue is dollar-linked and much of the cost base is not, so a weak rupee helps producers.
  3. Production volumes: the discipline that separates a compounder from a liquidation.
  4. Administered gas pricing: the formula, its floor and ceiling, and the separate freedom given to new-well gas.
  5. Government take: royalty, cess, profit petroleum and the ever-present possibility of a levy.
  6. Exploration success and reserve replacement: the only source of long-term value.
  7. Capital-expenditure cycles: ONGC's ₹40,000 cr-plus Western Offshore programme lifts output only from FY28.
  8. Policy openness: bid rounds, the 2025 Oilfields Amendment Act and the return of international majors to Indian acreage.

The anatomy of an upstream cycle

  1. Crude rises for reasons outside India — a supply cut, a war, a demand surprise.
  2. Earnings explode, because costs are largely fixed and realisation is not.
  3. The state's share is revisited — historically through subsidy sharing, in 2022 through a windfall levy — which caps the re-rating.
  4. Crude falls back, earnings deflate and the stocks give up most of the gain, unless production has genuinely grown in the meantime. It rarely has.

Patterns that repeat

  • The correlation with Brent is strong but asymmetric. Compare 2012 (Brent $112, basket +15% against a Nifty +28%) with 2021 (Brent $71, basket +63%): what mattered was not the level but who was allowed to keep it.
  • The best years were policy years, not price years: 2023 and 2024 were driven by the gas pricing reform and the re-rating of state enterprises.
  • Peaks cluster: ONGC, Oil India and Hindustan Oil Exploration all peaked in August 2024 and are 32–37% below those levels, even with crude above $100 in September 2026.
  • The small independents swing four to five times as hard in both directions — Antelopus Selan: +104% (2023), −50% (2025), +169% (2026).

11 · The global picture

India is a buyer in a market set elsewhere

World oil prices are set by the balance between OPEC+ supply decisions, American shale's response to price, and demand. India, the third-largest consumer, is a price-taker on all of it: it imports close to 90% of its crude, and its import bill reached a record of about 4.9 million barrels a day. Domestic production has been falling roughly 1.1% a year for a decade, on mature fields, delayed monetisation of discoveries and fewer new finds. The policy response has been to make the acreage terms progressively more attractive, and it is beginning to draw international interest back — including BP taking exploration acreage in India.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • From state monopoly to licensing rounds (1999 onwards): NELP opened acreage, and Cairn's Barmer discovery in 2004 became the country's largest onshore find.
  • From profit sharing to revenue sharing (2016): HELP and OALP removed the cost-recovery audit that had made the earlier regime litigious.
  • From offshore shallow water to deep water (2020s): the National Deep Water Exploration Mission and the 2025 Oilfields Amendment Act are aimed squarely at the acreage India has never properly drilled.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Enhanced and improved oil recovery: polymer, gas and chemical injection to raise the fraction of oil recovered from mature fields — the highest-value technology in a country of old reservoirs.
  2. Deep-water and ultra-deep-water drilling: the acreage where India's remaining large prizes probably sit, and where it lacks domestic capability.
  3. Advanced seismic and reservoir modelling: better images and simulation to cut the dry-hole rate.
  4. Horizontal and multi-lateral wells: more reservoir contact per well, the standard answer to declining fields.
  5. Digital field management: continuous monitoring and predictive maintenance to reduce downtime, which is where a 5% output decline is often lost.
  6. Gas-focused development: the transition fuel, and the reason new-well gas pricing freedom matters — it is what makes a marginal gas well worth drilling.

The honest read

This is what a commodity top usually looks like from the inside: the best earnings in the sector's history, and share prices that refuse to follow. The market is making a defensible judgement — that $100 crude in a West Asia conflict is not a base case, that a 5.5% production decline is, and that when crude is this high the government's willingness to take a share of it has historical form. The interesting subset is therefore not the majors but the companies where volume is actually growing: Oil India, lifting 11% more crude with a refinery expanding from 3 to 9 MMTPA, and the small independents adding fields through the Discovered Small Fields route. Antelopus Selan's 169% is not a sector signal, and the section's +40% headline should never be quoted without the +14% median beside it.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What was production by field this quarter, and what is the natural decline rate being fought?
  2. What is the reserve replacement ratio for the year, and how much came from revisions rather than new discoveries?
  3. What is the operating cost per barrel, and how has water cut changed it?
  4. What is the realisation net of royalty, cess and profit petroleum — the number that actually reaches shareholders?
  5. What share of gas is sold under the administered formula and what share at new-well prices?
  6. What is the capital expenditure plan by project, and when does each add barrels?
  7. (ONGC) What specifically is being done about the 5.5% crude decline before the Daman project arrives in FY28?
  8. (Oil India) What is a sustainable gross refining margin at Numaligarh once the current cycle normalises?
  9. (Independents) What is the work programme commitment on new blocks, and how is it funded?

Red flags

  • Profit growth celebrated while production falls.
  • Reserve additions that come from re-estimating old fields rather than drilling new ones.
  • Capital expenditure cut in a high-price year to protect dividends.
  • A small producer whose share price has moved far ahead of any announced reserve addition.

18 · Myths and live debates

What you'll hear, and the evidence

ClaimEvidence
"High crude means these stocks go up."Not reliably. Brent averaged $112 in 2012 and the basket underperformed by 13 points; 2026 has $88 crude, record profits and ONGC down 3%.
"The sector is cheap on earnings."It is cheap on peak-cycle earnings, with declining volumes and a history of the state taking a share when prices spike. Those are reasons for a low multiple, not an anomaly.
"India can drill its way to energy independence."Output has fallen about 1.1% a year for a decade and imports are at a record 4.9 million barrels a day. New acreage and deep-water exploration change the trajectory over a decade, not a cycle.
"The energy transition makes upstream uninvestable."India's oil demand is still growing, and gas is explicitly a transition fuel. The nearer risk is a government take on high prices, not demand disappearing.
"The sector is up 40% this year."The equal-weighted basket is. The median stock is up 14%, and one ₹4,100 cr company up 169% accounts for the difference.
"Vedanta Oil and Gas is a cheap way to buy Cairn."It is the same asset base with three months of listed history and a demerger overhang. The decline profile of Barmer and the capital plan are what decide it, and neither is visible in a share price yet.

Exploration well
A well drilled to test whether hydrocarbons are present. Most are unsuccessful.
Dry hole
A well that finds nothing commercial; its cost is written off.
Appraisal well
A well drilled after a discovery to size it.
Development well
A well drilled to produce from a proven field.
Workover
Maintenance intervention on an existing well to restore or raise its output.
Decline rate
The annual percentage fall in a field's output without intervention.
Water cut
The share of water in what a well lifts; it rises as a field ages and raises cost per barrel.
Enhanced oil recovery (EOR)
Injecting polymer, gas or chemicals to recover more of the oil in place.
Water injection / gas lift
Techniques to maintain reservoir pressure or help fluids to surface.
Deep water / ultra-deep water
Offshore acreage in great water depths; technically demanding and largely undrilled in India.
MMTOE
Million metric tonnes of oil equivalent, the common unit for combined oil and gas output. ONGC targets 42.5 for FY27.
mmbtu
Million British thermal units, the unit in which gas is priced.
Condensate
Light liquid hydrocarbons that separate out of produced gas.
Reserves and resources
Quantities judged commercially recoverable, versus those merely believed present.
Reserve replacement ratio
New reserves added divided by production in the same year; below one means the business is shrinking.

Contracts and policy

Nomination block
Acreage granted to the state producers before liberalisation.
Production sharing contract (PSC)
Costs recovered first, then profit petroleum shared with the government.
Cost recovery
The mechanism under a PSC allowing a contractor to recoup spending before sharing profit — the source of most historical disputes.
Profit petroleum
The government's share of production after cost recovery.
HELP
The Hydrocarbon Exploration and Licensing Policy of 2016: revenue sharing, a uniform licence and marketing freedom.
OALP
Open Acreage Licensing Programme, under which companies can nominate blocks they want auctioned; Round XI opened in March 2026.
DSF
Discovered Small Fields rounds, auctioning already-discovered small fields on simple terms.
DGH
The Directorate General of Hydrocarbons, the upstream regulator.
Royalty
A percentage of production value paid to the government regardless of profitability.
Cess
A per-tonne levy on nomination crude.
Windfall levy
The special additional excise duty on crude, introduced in July 2022 and withdrawn in December 2024. It returned in March 2026 on fuel exports only, not on crude (see 031).
Subsidy sharing / under-recovery
The pre-2014 system in which upstream producers funded part of the loss state refiners made selling fuel below cost.
Administered price mechanism (APM)
The formula pricing gas from nomination fields, with a floor and a ceiling.
New-well gas pricing
The pricing freedom given to gas from newly drilled wells; ONGC realised $13.31/mmbtu on it.
Oilfields (Regulation and Development) Act
The governing statute of 1948, substantially amended in 2025.
Demerger
Splitting a business into separately listed entities; Vedanta Oil and Gas listed this way on 15 June 2026 (see 021 for the reverse case).

Market terms

Brent
The North Sea benchmark against which most Indian crude is referenced.
Indian crude basket
The weighted average price of the crude India actually imports.
Realisation
What a producer actually receives per barrel after adjustments and discounts.
OPEC+
The producer group whose supply decisions set the marginal price.
Price-taker
A seller with no influence on the price it receives — the defining condition of this industry.
Gross refining margin
The difference between the value of products and the cost of crude, per barrel; relevant here through Oil India's Numaligarh subsidiary (see 031).
DD&A
Depletion, depreciation and amortisation — how capitalised exploration and development costs are charged against production.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. Why did high crude prices not help Indian producers before 2014?

Subsidy sharing: upstream producers funded part of the under-recovery on fuels sold below cost. In 2012, with Brent at $112, the basket underperformed the Nifty by 13 points.

2. What is the single most important number in an upstream result?

Production, not profit. ONGC posted a record pre-tax profit of ₹22,848 cr while crude output fell 5.5%, and the stock is down for the year.

3. What changed in 2016 with HELP and OALP?

The move from profit sharing with cost recovery to straightforward revenue sharing, with a uniform licence for all hydrocarbons and the ability to nominate acreage for auction.

4. What did ONGC and Oil India realise per barrel in Q1 FY27?

$99.45 for ONGC's nomination crude (from $66.13) and $98.73 for Oil India.

5. Why is Oil India partly a refining stock?

Numaligarh Refinery is a large subsidiary; its profit rose 167% to ₹1,305 cr on a gross refining margin of $35.95 a barrel, and it is expanding from 3 to 9 MMTPA.

6. What is the section's 2026 return, properly stated?

The basket is +40% against a Nifty −11%, but the median stock is +14%; Antelopus Selan's +169% accounts for the gap.

7. What arrived on the exchanges on 15 June 2026?

Vedanta Oil and Gas — the old Cairn business, anchored on the Barmer block — demerged from Vedanta, one share for each Vedanta share, debuting at ₹39.

8. What is the windfall levy, and why does it still matter?

A special additional excise duty on crude introduced in July 2022 and withdrawn in December 2024. It returned in March 2026 on exports of petrol, diesel and ATF, but not on crude. It is the precedent showing the state will take a share when prices spike.

9. How dependent is India on imported crude?

About 90% of requirements, with imports at a record of roughly 4.9 million barrels a day, while domestic output has fallen about 1.1% a year for a decade.

10. Which seasonal window is worst for this basket?

The fourth quarter: October–December has beaten the Nifty in 5 of 14 years (median −2.7) and September–November in 5 of 14 (median −4.6).
