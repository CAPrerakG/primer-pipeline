Iron Ore & Manganese Mining

Steel begins with iron ore, and almost every tonne of steel needs a little manganese to make it strong. India digs about 310 million tonnes of iron ore a year — the fourth largest output in the world — and is one of the few countries with a listed, state-owned manganese miner. These are businesses with no marketing, no brands and one product whose price is set in China: when ore prices rise, profits soar; when they fall, volume records count for nothing. This primer explains how ore is mined, graded and priced, what royalties and auctions take out of the price, how the four listed companies differ, and why 2026 has been a bad year for them despite record production.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Record volumes, falling prices

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Steel is made by melting rock that has iron in it. That rock is called iron ore, and digging it out of a hill is the first step in making everything from cars to bridges. A pinch of another rock, manganese, is added in the furnace, like salt in cooking — without it the steel would be brittle. The companies here own the hills. They cannot choose their price: it is set by what steel makers around the world, especially in China, are willing to pay. So they can dig more rock than ever and still earn less, if the price has fallen.

Say it to a client in one minute

This is a price-taking business at the bottom of the steel chain, and the price is falling while volumes rise.

  • Volumes: NMDC produced a record 53.15 mt in FY26, targets 60 mt in FY27 and 100 mt by 2030. India's output rose 7% to 310 mt.
  • Prices: iron ore is about $97 a tonne against $158 at end-2020; consensus is roughly $95 for 2026 as Guinea's Simandou ramps up.
  • Manganese: MOIL's Q1 FY27 profit rose 70% on a 10% higher realisation; it targets 3.5 mt by FY30.
  • Integration: Sandur has moved from mining into ferro alloys, coke and specialty steel — FY26 income ₹5,163 cr, up 61%.
  • Caution: OMDC, valued at ₹2,294 cr, has had no mining operations since 2020; it is a lease-value play, not a producer.
  • Valuation: NMDC trades at about 9 times annualised Q1 profit, MOIL 14 times, Sandur 13 times FY26 profit.

Grade is everything. Indian ore is generally high in iron (many Odisha and Chhattisgarh deposits run above 62% Fe) but often high in alumina, which raises costs in the furnace. That is why India both exports low-grade fines and imports some high-grade ore for coastal mills. In manganese, India's ore is mostly medium grade and the country imports a large share of its needs from South Africa, Australia and Gabon.

The chain in one breath

  • Governments auction the right to mine.
  • Miners blast and dig the ore.
  • Plants crush and wash it to raise its grade.
  • Railways and ports move it, often thousands of kilometres.
  • Steel mills melt it; the government takes its share at every tonne.
Fund-manager lens

The critical variable is the gap between the landed price a steel mill will pay and the miner's all-in cost, including royalty, district mineral foundation contributions and any auction premium. In the auctions since 2015, some bidders offered premiums above 100% of the reference price, which leaves them earning almost nothing when ore prices fall. NMDC, MOIL and Sandur hold older, low-premium leases, which is why they remain profitable at prices that would close a newly auctioned mine. That advantage is finite: leases expire, and renewals come at auction.

The rest of the industry

Most Indian iron ore is mined by steel makers for their own use — Tata Steel, JSW, AM/NS and SAIL all hold captive mines — so the listed "miners" are only part of the picture. Vedanta and Rungta are large merchant miners; Odisha's OMC is state-owned and unlisted. Pellet makers such as those inside steel groups sit in the steel sections, and ferro alloy producers, the main buyers of manganese ore, are the subject of primer 065.

Because royalties and levies are charged on sale value, the government's share is fixed as a proportion of revenue, not of profit. When prices fall, the miner absorbs the whole decline. That is the central feature of the business model, and it explains why the stocks track the ore price more closely than their own production.

Volumes versus price, in one quarter

NMDC's Q1 FY27 shows the tension exactly. Production rose 26% to 15.12 mt — a record — yet sales rose only 2% to 11.73 mt and revenue only 2% to ₹6,795 cr. Output ran ahead of despatches, and prices offered no help. MOIL's quarter was the opposite: production and sales grew only slightly, but a 10% better realisation lifted profit 70%. In this industry, price does the work.

What integration changes

Sandur's answer has been to stop selling all its ore. By adding ferro alloys, coke and, through Arjas Steel, specialty steel, it captures the margin further along the chain and smooths the ore cycle — FY26 income rose 61% to ₹5,163 cr with profit of ₹658 cr. The trade-off is that it now carries the risks of a steel maker too, and should increasingly be judged as one.

5b · How ore is priced

China sets the price; India sets the discount

The world benchmark is 62% iron content fines delivered to China, quoted daily and traded as futures in Singapore. Everything else is a differential: higher grade earns a premium, high alumina a penalty, lumps a premium over fines, and inland Indian mines a discount for the cost of getting ore to a port or a mill. NMDC publishes its own prices for lumps and fines and revises them often, which is why Indian realisations can move even when the global benchmark does not.

The structural question for the next five years is Simandou in Guinea — one of the world's largest untapped high-grade deposits, expected to ship about 20 mt in 2026 and to reach roughly 120 mt a year by 2030. That is new supply equal to a third of India's entire output, aimed at the same Chinese buyers. Consensus forecasts of about $95 a tonne for 2026, with a $80–100 range, already reflect it. For Indian miners, the offset is domestic: steel consumption grew 7–8% in FY26 to 164 mt, and India's mills need more ore every year.

A mining company's real asset is not the ore in the ground but the permission to take it out. Indian mining has repeatedly shown how fragile that is: the Karnataka ban of 2011, the Goa cancellations, Odisha's suspensions, and the Supreme Court's cancellation of leases in 2014. OMDC is the extreme case — a listed company valued at more than ₹2,000 cr whose mines have produced nothing for years.

7 · The business calendar

Monsoon, restocking and the March rush

  • January–March: the fiscal year-end push; miners maximise despatches and steel mills build stocks before the monsoon.
  • February: Chinese New Year slows Chinese buying, and global prices often drift.
  • April–June: pre-monsoon stocking; NMDC often revises prices; production runs hard before the rains.
  • July–September: the monsoon floods pits and slows haulage in Odisha, Chhattisgarh, Karnataka and Goa; output and prices usually dip.
  • October–December: post-monsoon restocking; mills rebuild inventory and prices often firm.

That calendar matches the stock pattern: April and October have been the best months, and February–March the worst. It is a cleaner seasonal signal than in most industries because the physical constraint — rain — is real and repeats every year.

  • Windows: Feb–Mar beat the Nifty in 3 of 15 years (median -8.5 pts); Apr–Jul beat the Nifty in 8 of 15 years (median +1.3 pts); Aug–Sep beat the Nifty in 5 of 14 years (median -1.8 pts); Oct–Dec beat the Nifty in 8 of 14 years (median +5.8 pts); Sep–Nov beat the Nifty in 5 of 14 years (median -1.3 pts); Jan–Mar beat the Nifty in 4 of 15 years (median -9.2 pts).

Control: small caps as a whole (Nifty Smallcap 100) beat the Nifty in April in 11 of 15 years, by an average of 3.0 points, and lag in January–February. Part of any April strength is this market-wide effect. After removing it, this industry's own April edge is about +2.4 points.

Reading it: four companies — NMDC, MOIL and OMDC through the period, Sandur from September 2023. February–March has beaten the Nifty in only 3 of 15 years (median −8.5 points) and February alone in 3 of 15 (average −4.2). April has worked in 10 of 15 (+5.4) and October in 9 of 15 (+3.2). The shape follows the mining year: dispatches and price revisions cluster before the monsoon and after it, while February–March combines the Chinese New Year lull with the end of the Indian fiscal year, when miners have already pushed volumes out.

A tendency, not a rule: small samples, survivorship bias (only names still listed) and equal weighting. Use it for timing context, not as a signal.

The exceptions, and why

  • February–March worked only in 2016 (+10.3), 2021 (+7.3) and 2022 — each a moment when ore prices were rising fast. Its worst years were 2024 (−22.0), 2017 (−15.8) and 2013 (−14.8).
  • April–July failed in 2018 (−31.1), 2013 (−21.7), 2022 (−15.3) and 2019 (−8.3); it worked best in 2024 (+34.6) and 2012 (+23.3).
  • August–September has lost in 9 of 14 years, the monsoon quarter.
How to use this

The seasonal pattern is real but second-order. The first-order driver is the ore price: in the five years when iron ore fell (2015, 2021, 2024 and 2026 among them), no calendar helped. Use the season for timing within a view on prices, not instead of one.

9 · What moves margins

Eight drivers

  1. The global ore price. Chinese steel output and seaborne supply set it.
  2. Domestic steel demand. India's mills now consume 164 mt of finished steel a year.
  3. NMDC's price revisions. They set the domestic reference for merchant ore.
  4. Royalty, DMF and auction premium. A fixed share of revenue, whatever the price.
  5. Volume growth and clearances. Higher approved capacity lifts output.
  6. Logistics. Rakes, pipelines and port access decide how much can be sold.
  7. Export duty. Applied and removed repeatedly; it redirects ore to or from the domestic market.
  8. Manganese-specific supply. Cyclones, mine outages and restarts abroad swing manganese prices sharply.

10 · A decade of cycles

YearIron ore ($/t)What happenedBasketNiftyDiff (pts)

Basket: NMDC, MOIL and OMDC throughout, Sandur from September 2023, equal-weighted monthly. Iron ore is the SGX 62% Fe futures price at each year-end (2026 to 18 September). Monthly returns capped at ±50%, price only.

A global mirror: Vale

Vale (NYSE:VALE), the world's second-largest iron ore producer, shows the same cycle without the Indian regulatory layer: −46% in 2014 and −60% in 2015 as prices collapsed, +132% in 2016 and +60% in 2017 on the rebound, −44% in 2024 as China's property slump bit, then +47% in 2025 and +9% so far in 2026. Its share price today is roughly a third below its 2011 level, fifteen years on — the same lesson the Indian miners teach: in commodities, the cycle can eat a decade.

The anatomy of the cycles

  1. Bans and cancellations (2011–2015): Karnataka, Goa and Odisha suspensions, then the Supreme Court's cancellation of leases and the shift to auctions in 2015.
  2. The rebound (2016–2020): prices recovered from $41 to $158; supply shocks at Vale helped.
  3. The round trip (2021–2022): a record spike above $200, a halving by December, and an export duty that came and went.
  4. The PSU re-rating (2023): government-owned miners were re-rated with the rest of the PSU complex; the basket rose 114%.
  5. Volume growth, price decline (2024–2026): record Indian output against a falling global price, now with Simandou arriving.

Patterns that repeat

  • Records do not pay. NMDC's best-ever production year came with a 5% share-price fall.
  • Prices turn faster than mines. Capacity added at high prices arrives after they have fallen.
  • Policy is a second commodity. Export duties, auction premiums and clearances move earnings as much as the ore price.

India is unusual: it is a large producer that consumes almost everything it mines. That makes its miners less exposed to the seaborne market than Vale or Rio Tinto, but not immune — the domestic price follows the import parity price, and when global prices fall, Indian mills demand discounts. The hedge is domestic growth. India's finished steel consumption of 164 mt in FY26 is growing at 7–8% a year, while China's is shrinking.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Beneficiation. Upgrading low-grade and dump ore into usable fines as high-grade reserves deplete.
  2. Slurry pipelines and conveyors. Cheaper, cleaner transport than road; NMDC and Sandur are both building them.
  3. Pelletisation. More ore is pelletised before use, adding a value-added step in India.
  4. Green steel. Direct-reduction and hydrogen routes need high-grade, low-alumina ore and pellets — a premium India must earn.
  5. Automation and digital mining. Fleet management, drones for surveys, remote drilling.
  6. Deeper mines. MOIL's shaft-sinking programme reflects manganese deposits that are no longer shallow.

What is priced in

At roughly 9 times annualised profit, NMDC is priced for lower ore prices, not for its volume target — the market is assuming that 60 mt of sales at weaker realisations earns little more than 50 mt did. MOIL at about 14 times, after a 34% fall, prices in the return of Australian manganese supply. Sandur, at about 13 times, is increasingly a steel company and will be valued as one. OMDC's ₹2,294 cr value rests on leases, not production, and will move on clearance and lease news rather than on earnings.

The honest read

These are cash-generating, low-cost assets with a domestic demand story that is genuinely strong. But they cannot escape the global price, and the next few years bring the largest new iron ore supply in a generation. The way to own the theme is to buy the low-cost producer when the cycle is against it and to avoid confusing production records with earnings growth. Manganese is a separate, tighter market with its own supply shocks, and MOIL is the only listed way to own it.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What realisation per tonne did you achieve, and how does it compare with the import parity price?
  2. Why did sales lag production this quarter, and where is the inventory?
  3. What is the all-in cost per tonne, including royalty, DMF and premium?
  4. Which clearances or lease approvals are pending, and what volume depends on them?
  5. How much of the expansion plan is funded and approved?
  6. What is the logistics mix — rail, road, pipeline — and how is it changing?
  7. How will the arrival of Simandou supply affect your pricing assumptions?
  8. For MOIL: how do domestic manganese prices compare with imported ore?
  9. For integrated miners: what share of ore is sold and what share consumed internally?
  10. What is the capital-allocation plan — dividends, buybacks or expansion?

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. What is the difference between lumps and fines?

Lumps can go straight into a furnace; fines must be sintered or pelletised first.

2. What did NMDC produce in FY26, and what does it target?

53.15 mt, the first Indian miner past 50 mt; it targets 60 mt in FY27 and 100 mt by 2030.

3. Why did NMDC's profit rise only 2% in Q1 FY27 when production rose 26%?

Sales rose only 2% and prices did not help; output ran ahead of despatches.

4. What drove MOIL's 70% profit growth?

A 10% higher realisation, at ₹9,749 a tonne.

5. How much of a miner's revenue goes to the government?

Roughly a fifth through royalty, DMF and NMET, plus any auction premium.

6. What is Simandou, and why does it matter?

A Guinean iron ore project shipping about 20 mt in 2026 and ramping toward 120 mt by 2030 — a major new source of supply.

7. Why is OMDC unusual?

It is valued above ₹2,000 cr but has not mined since 2020, pending statutory clearances.

8. Which months have been best and worst for these stocks?

April and October best; February–March worst, beating the Nifty in only 3 of 15 years.

9. What happened to iron ore prices in 2021?

They peaked above $200 in May and fell to about $123 by December.

10. How has Sandur changed its business?

It has integrated forward into ferro alloys, coke and specialty steel through Arjas Steel.
