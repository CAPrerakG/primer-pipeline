Industry primer · 78 of 278 · Engineering family, part 1

Castings & Foundries

A casting is the oldest way of making a metal object: melt the metal, pour it into a cavity shaped like the thing you want, let it cool. Almost every machine you have ever used contains one — an engine block, a pump housing, a brake disc, a railway bogie, the grinding balls that pulverise ore inside a mining mill. It is also the first industry in this Engineering family and the natural sequel to the Metals family, because a foundry is where metal stops being a commodity and starts being a component. What this section demonstrates, more sharply than any so far, is that how a casting is sold matters far more than how it is made: one company here earns a 36% EBITDA margin and another 9.5%, and the difference has nothing to do with metallurgy.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Two businesses wearing the same overalls

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Think of making a sandcastle, but instead of wet sand you pour melted metal into the mould, and when it goes hard you knock the mould away. That is a casting. Now here is the interesting part: if you make engine parts for a car company, they hand you the exact drawing and ask for the cheapest price, so you earn very little. But if you make the heavy metal balls that grind rock inside a mine, and yours last longer than anyone else's, the mine will pay a lot more — because what they are really buying is fewer breakdowns.

Say it to a client in one minute

One section, two completely different economics.

  • The consumable model — AIA Engineering (~₹35,374 cr): high-chrome mill internals and grinding media sold to miners and cement plants on wear performance. Q1 FY27 volumes 64,644 t (+7.5%), revenue ₹1,153 cr, profit ₹301 cr, EBITDA margin 36.35% (from 40.46%). Realisations above ₹180/kg against 18-month-old guidance of ₹160–165.
  • The tier-one model — Alicon Castalloy (~₹1,122 cr): aluminium castings to a customer's drawing — cylinder heads, motor housings, battery trays. Q1 FY27 income ₹579.1 cr (+37%), first quarter above ₹500 cr, profit ₹11.4 cr (+23%), EBITDA margin 9.5% (from 11.4%). Order book ₹8,450 cr to 2031.
  • The gap is the lesson, and it is the same lesson as refractories (070) and electrodes (071): a consumable sold on performance beats a component sold on a drawing.
  • The calendar: April has beaten the Nifty in 14 of 15 years — the strongest monthly signal found in 78 primers.
  • The audit: 28 names, of which six are usable. One is AIA's own 74.85%-owned subsidiary, and one is classified by the data provider as a confectionery company.

Read that table from the bottom up and a pattern emerges that will recur through the whole Engineering family. The further a product is from a drawing supplied by a powerful customer, the better it earns. An aluminium cylinder head is made to a car maker's specification, competitively tendered, and re-tendered; a grinding ball is sold on a promise about how much ore it will grind before it wears out, which is a claim only the supplier can substantiate and only experience can verify.

The chain in one breath

  • Metal is melted to a precise chemistry.
  • A mould is formed in sand or steel.
  • The metal is poured and allowed to solidify without defects.
  • Heat treatment gives it the hardness or toughness required.
  • Machining turns a rough casting into a component — and roughly doubles its value.
Fund-manager lens

Three numbers describe any foundry, and revenue is not one of them. Rejection rate decides whether the metal and energy poured into a bad casting is recovered or wasted. Machined share decides how much of the component's value the foundry captures rather than handing to a machining shop. And realisation per kilogram tells you whether the company is selling a product or a commodity — AIA's ₹180 a kg against a grey-iron caster's typical realisation is the whole story of this section in one ratio. Ask for all three; most managements will give you two.

The audit

Welcast Steels is AIA Engineering's subsidiary, not a peer. AIA holds 74.85% of it and it exists to manufacture grinding media for AIA; its board has approved delisting and it is shutting its Bengaluru plant. Putting both in one basket would count the same business twice. This is the second same-group trap in three primers — the first was Foseco India and its newly acquired crucible subsidiary, which caused an error in 070 that we have since corrected and republished. The practical rule: when two names in a roster share a group, check the shareholding and the ISIN before writing anything.

La Tim Metal's series is unusable. It falls 97.8% in a single day on 3 August 2012, from ₹39.8 to ₹0.9, immediately after its series begins. Whatever the cause, no return can be computed across it.

Two classification errors worth correcting in the source file. Shri Gang Industries is tagged by the data provider as Food: Specialty/Candy while sitting in a castings section, and Foundry Fuel Products as Coal. More substantively, Foseco India is a consumables supplier to foundries, not a foundry, and its natural home is with refractories and foundry consumables (070) rather than here.

The basket is therefore six names: AIA Engineering, Nelcast, Uni-Abex, Magna Electro, Nitin Castings (all from 2005–2007) and Alicon Castalloy (from 2011). Why AIA earns four times Alicon's margin

It is worth being precise about this, because it is the most transferable idea in the primer. AIA Engineering sells grinding media and mill liners into mining and cement. Those parts are consumed — ground away against rock — so the customer buys them repeatedly and measures them on cost per tonne of ore processed rather than cost per kilogram of steel. A supplier whose alloy lasts 15% longer can charge substantially more and still save the customer money. The result is a 36% EBITDA margin and a realisation above ₹180 a kg.

Alicon supplies aluminium castings to vehicle manufacturers. Those parts are made to the customer's drawing, competitively tendered, and the customer has an engineering department perfectly capable of qualifying a second source. The result is 9.5%, and the way to grow is to add machining and win more programmes — which Alicon is doing, with an order book of ₹8,450 cr running to 2031 and a new leased plant.

The test to apply

Ask of any casting company: does the customer re-order because the part wore out, or because a new vehicle programme started? The first is a consumable business with pricing power; the second is a capital-goods supplier subject to a tender. It is the same distinction that made refractories (070) and graphite electrodes (071) interesting, and it will recur in bearings, pumps and valves later in this family.

Read AIA's capex carefully

One flag on the quality name. AIA has raised FY27 capex to ₹350–400 cr, but on its own breakdown ₹170–200 cr of that is a new corporate headquarters and ₹50–100 cr is land for future expansion, leaving comparatively little for capacity. At the same time it has withheld tonnage guidance pending trials of its new discharge system. A company spending roughly half its capital programme on an office while declining to forecast volumes is not necessarily doing anything wrong — but it is a combination worth asking about directly.

5b · The industry behind the six names

Five thousand foundries, 90% of them small

The six investable names in this section are the visible tip of something much larger, and the shape of the whole industry explains why so few of its participants are listed. India's foundry industry produced 15.86 million tonnes of castings in 2024–25 from roughly 5,000 units, about 90% of them micro, small or medium enterprises, employing more than two million people. It is the world's second-largest foundry industry after China.

Three investment implications follow from that structure, and they hold across the Engineering family.

  1. Fragmentation caps pricing, and creates an opening. With five thousand suppliers, a vehicle maker can always find a cheaper quote for a grey-iron part — which is why 68% of the industry's tonnage sits at the thin end. The opportunity is not to be the cheapest of five thousand; it is to exit that comparison entirely, through specification, certification or a consumable model.
  2. Exports are the quality signal. $4.31 bn of casting exports is evidence that Indian metallurgy competes on merit rather than on protection. A foundry with a meaningful export share has passed an audit that no domestic tender requires, and the demand pull across automotive, railways, agriculture and power is broad rather than dependent on one customer.
  3. Consolidation will be slow, and environmental rules will drive it. An industry employing two million people across mostly tiny units does not consolidate on economics alone. What does move it is compliance — emission norms on melting and sand handling that a small cupola operator cannot afford. That is the same mechanism formalising metal recycling in 076, and it favours the organised players here for exactly the same reason.

The long-range projections — the industry reaching $42.5 bn by 2029 and a claimed $169 bn by 2047 — should be treated as industry-body ambition rather than forecast. The checkable facts are the tonnage, the export number and the MSME share, and those alone make the case that the market is large, growing and structurally incapable of rewarding an undifferentiated participant.

7 · The business calendar

The customer's schedule year

  • January–March: vehicle makers and equipment buyers run down inventory before the fiscal year end and defer schedules; the weakest window at 5 wins in 15 years.
  • April: new annual schedules, new price settlements and fresh budgets; 14 wins in 15 years, the strongest month in this project.
  • April–July: foundries run flat out to clear schedules before the monsoon; 10 of 15, median +7.8.
  • August–September: the monsoon raises humidity, which genuinely affects sand moulding and rejection rates; still 9 of 14.
  • October–December: the festive vehicle season and post-monsoon mining activity; 8 of 14.

Reading it: six companies with long histories — AIA Engineering, Nelcast, Alicon Castalloy (from 2011), Uni-Abex, Magna Electro Castings and Nitin Castings. April has beaten the Nifty in 14 of 15 years, averaging +5.4 points — the single strongest monthly record anywhere in this project, and its only loss was 2013. April–July has worked in 10 of 15 (median +7.8) and August–September in 9 of 14. February–March has worked 5 times in 15 (median −4.7) and January–March 5 of 15. The mechanism is the customer's year: automotive and mining buyers place annual schedules from April, and foundries run flat out to clear them before the monsoon.

A tendency, not a rule: small samples, survivorship bias (only names still listed) and equal weighting. Use it for timing context, not as a signal.

The exception, and why it matters

  • April's single failure in fifteen years was 2013 (−7.8), in the depths of the commercial vehicle downturn; its weakest wins were 2019 (+0.8) and 2021 (+1.6), and its best 2022 (+14.0), 2015 (+13.1) and 2023 (+10.9).
  • April–July failed in 2019 (−14.6) and 2020 (−14.1) — the commercial vehicle collapse and the lockdown, both demand shocks rather than seasonal effects.
  • February–March's two good years were 2026 (+16.7) and 2014 (+7.3), both moments when the market was anticipating a capex upturn.
How to use this

Fourteen of fifteen is the strongest monthly record in seventy-eight primers, and unusually it has a mechanism you can state and check: Indian vehicle and equipment makers set annual schedules and settle prices from April, so a foundry's order visibility and its realisation both step up at the start of the financial year. Two cautions all the same. The basket contains four small caps, so part of the effect is small-cap seasonality — the same April strength appears in recycling (076) and specialty metals (075). And a one-month signal is a poor basis for a position; its value is in timing an entry you had already decided on, and in knowing that February–March is when this industry's news tends to disappoint.

9 · What moves margins

Eight drivers

  1. Commercial vehicle and tractor volumes. The iron foundries' demand base.
  2. Mining and cement output. AIA's consumable demand, and global.
  3. Metal prices. Pass-through with a lag; aluminium squeezed Alicon this quarter.
  4. Realisation per kilogram. Whether the company sells a product or a commodity.
  5. Rejection rates. The main controllable cost.
  6. Machined share. How much of the component's value is captured.
  7. Power tariffs. Melting is electricity-intensive.
  8. Export orders and freight. AIA's margin fell partly on freight.

The anatomy of the cycles

  1. The capex upturn (2013–2015): a collapsed sector re-rated violently as the vehicle cycle turned — 2014 alone returned 161%, with Alicon up 372%.
  2. The credit squeeze and the CV collapse (2018–2020): IL&FS drained small-cap liquidity, commercial vehicle volumes fell sharply, and then lockdowns shut foundries; three years of underperformance.
  3. The second re-rating (2021–2023): auto recovery, import substitution and a capex theme lifted the basket 48%, 34% and 98%.
  4. Dispersion (2024–2026): the small iron foundries kept running while the two largest names went sideways — a market discriminating between order books.

Patterns that repeat

  • The commercial vehicle cycle is the dominant driver for everything except AIA, and it is violent in both directions.
  • Small foundries outperform in recoveries and collapse in credit events, because they are working-capital businesses.
  • AIA behaves differently from the rest of its own section — it barely participated in 2014 or 2023, because it is a mining consumable, not an auto casting.

India's structural advantage in castings is real and unusually durable: energy and labour costs are competitive, the metallurgical skill base is deep, and the industry is close to both a large domestic vehicle market and to the mining economies that buy wear parts. Its structural disadvantage is equally real — foundries are fragmented, working-capital-hungry and dependent on customers with far more bargaining power. That combination explains why the sector produces occasional outstanding businesses and a great many mediocre ones, and why twenty-two of the twenty-eight names in this section could not be included in a basket.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Electric vehicles change the parts list. Cylinder heads and engine blocks disappear; motor housings, battery trays and structural castings arrive. Alicon already supplies all three.
  2. Giga-casting. Casting large sections of a vehicle body in one piece, which favours very large machines and threatens many small stampings.
  3. Lightweighting. Aluminium and magnesium replacing iron, which shrinks tonnes while raising value per kilogram.
  4. Simulation. Solidification modelling to cut rejection rates before any metal is poured — the cheapest margin gain available.
  5. Sand reclamation and cleaner melting. Environmental compliance becoming a barrier to entry that favours organised foundries.
  6. Longer-wearing alloys. AIA's discharge system and grade development, where the product is measured in wear life rather than weight.

What is priced in

For AIA, a margin that keeps normalising downward. The stock is flat for the year and 22% below its June high despite record realisations, because the market is watching 36.35% fall from 40.46% and drawing the obvious line — and because management has declined to guide on tonnage while directing a large part of its capital programme into a building. The bull case is unchanged and quite strong: a global duopoly-ish position in a genuine consumable, with a new discharge system in trials that could expand the addressable market. But it has to be bought on the consumable franchise, not on the current margin.

For Alicon, a re-rating that has already been taken away. At 52% below its 2024 high, on 37% revenue growth and a ₹8,450 cr order book, the market is pricing the 9.5% margin rather than the growth — and it is not wrong to, because a tier-one caster's order book is only as valuable as the margin at which it converts. The Shikrapur plant and the machining mix are the things to watch; the order book number, spread to 2031, is less impressive than it first appears.

The honest read

This section is a good place to state the Engineering family's opening principle, because it will apply for the next twenty primers. In engineering, the question is never what a company makes — it is who writes the specification. When the customer writes it and tenders it, you own a converter with a 9.5% margin and a long order book of thin business. When the supplier writes it, guarantees a performance outcome, and the part wears out, you own something much better. Both are in this section, under one heading, at four times the margin difference.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What were tonnes sold, and what was realisation per kilogram?
  2. What is the rejection rate, and how has it moved over two years?
  3. What share of revenue is machined rather than rough castings?
  4. How are metal costs passed through, and with what lag?
  5. What is capacity utilisation, and what is the debottlenecked ceiling?
  6. Of the announced capex, how much adds capacity and how much is infrastructure?
  7. What is the order book, and over how many years does it execute?
  8. What proportion of sales goes to electric-vehicle platforms, and at what margin?
  9. What is the export share, and how is freight recovered?
  10. For consumable products: what performance guarantee is given, and how is it measured?

19 · Glossary

Every term you'll meet

The process

Casting
A metal object made by pouring liquid metal into a mould.
Foundry
The factory where metal is melted and poured.
Pattern
The master shape used to form a mould.
Sand casting
Moulding in bonded sand — the most common route for iron.
Die casting
Injecting metal into a steel die under pressure; used for aluminium.
Gravity die casting
Pouring into a steel mould without pressure.
Investment casting
The lost-wax route, for high-precision parts.
Centrifugal casting
Spinning the mould so metal is thrown outward — used for tubes, as at Uni-Abex.
Cupola furnace
A traditional coke-fired iron melting furnace.
Induction furnace
Electric melting; now the dominant route.
Fettling
Cleaning and dressing a casting after it leaves the mould.
Heat treatment
Controlled heating and cooling to set hardness and toughness.
Machining
Cutting the casting to final tolerance; roughly doubles its value.
Solidification simulation
Modelling the pour on a computer to predict and prevent defects.
Giga-casting
Casting a very large single vehicle structure in one piece.
Sand reclamation
Recovering and reusing foundry sand.

Products and metallurgy

Grey iron
Cast iron with flake graphite; cheap, good at damping vibration.
Ductile iron
Cast iron with spheroidal graphite; far tougher, used in axles and suspensions.
Steel casting
A casting in steel rather than iron; stronger, harder to pour.
High-chrome alloy
A very hard, wear-resistant grade used for grinding media and mill liners.
Mill internals
The liners and grinding media inside a mining or cement mill — AIA's product.
Grinding media
The balls or rods that pulverise ore; consumed in use.
Heat-resistant alloy
Grades that hold strength in furnaces and petrochemical plants.
Battery tray
The structural aluminium casting that holds an EV battery pack.
Motor housing
The casting enclosing an electric traction motor.
Cylinder head
The aluminium casting atop an engine block — the part EVs remove.
Porosity
Gas voids in a casting; a common cause of rejection.
Shrinkage defect
A cavity formed as metal contracts while solidifying.

Business terms

Rejection rate
The share of castings scrapped for defects — the main controllable cost.
Conversion charge
What a foundry earns per kilogram above the metal cost.
Realisation per kg
Revenue divided by tonnes sold; the test of whether a product or a commodity is being sold.
Machined share
The proportion of revenue from finished rather than rough castings.
Tier one
A supplier selling directly to a vehicle manufacturer, to its drawing.
Consumable model
Selling a part that wears out, priced on performance rather than weight.
Executable order book
Contracted business, with the years over which it will be delivered.
Debottlenecking
Small investments that raise output without a new plant.
Pass-through
Indexing metal cost changes to the customer, usually with a lag.
Tonnage guidance
A volume forecast; AIA has withheld its FY27 figure.
NGDS
AIA's new generation discharge system, in mill trials.
Delisting
Removing a company's shares from an exchange — approved for Welcast Steels.
MSME
Micro, small and medium enterprise — about 90% of India's 5,000 foundries.
Specific consumption
Grinding media consumed per tonne of ore ground; AIA's demand metric.
Wear life
How long a consumable part lasts before replacement — what AIA actually sells.
Second source
An alternative supplier a customer qualifies to keep prices down.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. What is a casting?

A metal object made by pouring liquid metal into a mould shaped like the finished part.

2. Why does AIA earn 36% and Alicon 9.5%?

AIA sells a consumable — grinding media priced on wear life and cost per tonne of ore. Alicon sells components to a customer's drawing, competitively tendered.

3. What is the single best test of a foundry's product quality?

Realisation per kilogram. It reveals whether the company is selling a product or a commodity.

4. What is the main controllable cost?

The rejection rate — metal, energy and labour wasted on defective castings.

5. Why is Welcast Steels excluded?

AIA Engineering owns 74.85% of it and it manufactures for AIA; including both would double-count the same business.

6. What should you notice about AIA's FY27 capex?

Of ₹350–400 cr, ₹170–200 cr is a new corporate headquarters and ₹50–100 cr is land — comparatively little adds capacity.

7. Do electric vehicles reduce casting demand?

They change the parts list — out go cylinder heads and blocks, in come motor housings, battery trays and structural castings.

8. What is the strongest calendar pattern in this project?

April in this sector: 14 wins in 15 years, because vehicle and equipment buyers set annual schedules and prices from April.

9. How valuable is Alicon's ₹8,450 cr order book?

Less than it looks: it executes through 2031, so it is a long-dated book converting at a 9.5% margin.

10. What is the Engineering family's opening principle?

The question is never what a company makes but who writes the specification — and whether the part wears out.
