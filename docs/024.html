Auto Electricals & Electronics

The nervous system of a vehicle: the wiring that carries power, the lamps that light the road, the cluster that shows speed, and the controllers, sensors and software that increasingly decide how the vehicle behaves. Electronics were about a third of a car's cost in 2020 and are heading for half by 2030, which makes this the fastest-growing part of the supply chain.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

The part of the vehicle that keeps growing

Explain it to a 10-year-old

A vehicle is not just metal. Inside it there are wires like veins, lamps like eyes, and small computers that decide when to spray fuel, how bright the lights should be, and whether to warn the driver. Twenty years ago a scooter had almost none of this. Today even a cheap bike has electronics, and a new car has dozens of little computers. So the companies that make wires, lamps, dials and controllers sell more with every new model, even if the number of vehicles sold barely changes.

Say it to a client in one minute

This is the content-growth corner of the auto supply chain. Electronics were roughly 30–35% of a car's cost in 2020 and are forecast at 45–55% by 2030, so these suppliers grow faster than vehicle volumes.

  • Bosch is the technology leader, with fuel-injection, powertrain and aftermarket businesses; Q1 FY27 revenue +22% with a 14% EBITDA margin.
  • Motherson Sumi Wiring is the pure wiring-harness play, sensitive to copper and wages.
  • Varroc and Lumax Industries are lighting and electronics suppliers riding the shift to LED and EV content.
  • Pricol makes instrument clusters and sensors, Fiem two-wheeler lighting, India Nippon ignition systems, Danlaw automotive test and telematics electronics.
  • Two large listings arrived in 2026: SEDEMAC (control electronics, March) and Dhoot Transmission (electrical components, August, listing 37.8% above issue).

The catch is that most of the silicon is imported, which is what the India Semiconductor Mission's ₹1.64 lakh cr of approved projects is meant to change.

Analogy

Think of a vehicle as a house. The mechanical suppliers (023) build the walls and plumbing. This industry does the wiring, the lights, the meters and the smart switches. As houses get smarter, the electrician's share of the bill keeps rising even if the house is the same size. That is exactly what is happening to cars.

Everyday examples

The LED headlamp on a new Creta is probably Lumax's or Varroc's. The digital speedometer on a Splendor may be Pricol's. The fuel-injection system on almost any BS-VI two-wheeler is likely Bosch's. The starter-generator that makes an idling scooter restart silently could be SEDEMAC's. The harness bundling it all together is Motherson Sumi Wiring's or Dhoot's.

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Raw inputs: copper for wires, tiny silicon chips for the computers, and LEDs for the lamps. India buys most of the chips from abroad.
  • Components: circuit boards and sensors are built, often to a design the vehicle maker has approved.
  • Systems: engineers write the software and build the controller that decides what the vehicle does.
  • Harnessing: hundreds of wires are cut, joined and taped into one bundle shaped exactly for that model.
  • Vehicle makers: pick these suppliers years before the vehicle launches, which makes the business sticky.
Fund-manager lens

Two things separate winners here. First, engineering content: a harness maker sells copper and labour, while a controller maker sells software and know-how, which is why margins differ so much (Motherson Sumi Wiring's profit was flat on 37% revenue growth, while Bosch's margin reached 14%). Second, the technology transition: LED lighting, digital clusters and EV controllers all multiply content per vehicle. Buy the content shift, not the volume.

Why wiring is different from electronics

A wiring harness is copper, connectors and a lot of skilled hand assembly. Copper is passed through to customers, but with a lag, and wage increases cannot be passed on at all. That is why Motherson Sumi Wiring grew revenue 37% in Q1 FY27 and still made no more profit than a year earlier. A controller, by contrast, embeds years of software development: the bill of materials is smaller, but the engineering is the product, so margins are higher and stickier. Between them sits lighting, where the shift from halogen bulbs to LED modules multiplies the value per vehicle and rewards tooling and design skill.

Analogy

Harness making is tailoring: skilled, labour-intensive, priced by the cloth. Control electronics is writing the pattern everyone else must cut to. Both are needed; only one scales without adding people.

7 · The business calendar

Built around the customer's model year

  • April–June: new programmes start and annual prices are settled; suppliers book tooling revenue for upcoming launches.
  • July–September: festive production build; the strongest volume stretch, and historically the strongest months for these stocks.
  • October–December: festive sales, then a slowdown; model-year changes bring new lighting and cluster specifications.
  • January–March: year-end inventory control and the annual price-down; the weakest window.
  • Auto Expo and launch cycles: new model launches decide which supplier's content is designed in for the next five years.

  • Windows: Feb–Mar beat the Nifty in 4 of 15 years (median -3.1 pts); Apr–Jul beat the Nifty in 12 of 15 years (median +11.6 pts); Aug–Sep beat the Nifty in 8 of 14 years (median +2.4 pts); Oct–Dec beat the Nifty in 8 of 14 years (median +3.1 pts); Sep–Nov beat the Nifty in 9 of 14 years (median +3.5 pts); Jan–Mar beat the Nifty in 5 of 15 years (median -5.1 pts).

Control: small caps as a whole (Nifty Smallcap 100) beat the Nifty in April in 11 of 15 years, by an average of 3.0 points, and lag in January–February. Part of any April strength is this market-wide effect. After removing it, this industry's own April edge is about +1.4 points.

Reading it: the same production calendar as 023, slightly stronger. April beat the Nifty in 12 of 15 years and July in 12 of 16, with April–July winning 12 of 15 (median +11.6 points). September (11 of 15) and the September–November window (9 of 14) also work, matching the festive build. February–March is the weak spot (4 of 15) and January–March worse (5 of 15, median −5.1), when carmakers manage year-end inventory and the annual price-down lands.

A tendency, not a rule: small samples, survivorship bias (only names still listed) and equal weighting. Use it for timing context, not as a signal.

The exceptions, and why

  • April–July failed only three times: 2013 (−3.0), 2017 (−1.4) and 2019 (−26.1). Only 2019, the auto slowdown, was a serious loss. The biggest runs were 2022 (+30.8) and 2026 (+28.1).
  • April misses were small (2025 −2.9, 2013 −1.8, 2019 −1.2); the best Aprils were 2020 (+14.5), 2026 (+11.9) and 2022 (+9.6).
  • July is unusually reliable (12 of 16), which fits the pre-festive production ramp; the exception was July 2019 (−14.7) at the depth of the slowdown.
  • February–March beat the Nifty only 4 times in 15 years, with the worst in 2022 (−17.0) and 2020 (−16.5).
How to use this

April–July, the same window as 023, but with a slightly better hit rate and a shorter tail of failures. The reason is structural: content-driven suppliers keep growing even when volumes are flat, so the seasonal pattern is less dependent on the vehicle cycle. Still check copper and chip costs, which is where the margin surprises come from.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Eight drivers

  1. Electronics content per vehicle: from roughly 30–35% of car cost in 2020 towards 45–55% by 2030; the single most important driver.
  2. LED and display penetration: lighting and cluster value multiplies as halogen and analogue give way.
  3. Emission and safety norms: BS-VI, OBD, ABS for two-wheelers (see 018), ADAS features on mid-range cars.
  4. Electrification: motor controllers, battery-management electronics and high-voltage harnesses; Varroc's EV revenue is 16% of sales, Motherson Sumi Wiring's 8.5%.
  5. Copper and chip prices: the two input swings that decide harness and electronics margins.
  6. Currency: imported chips and displays make a weak rupee a cost problem, though it helps exporters.
  7. Vehicle volumes and mix: two-wheeler-heavy suppliers (Fiem, Pricol, India Nippon) follow 018; car-linked ones follow 017.
  8. Localisation policy: the India Semiconductor Mission and component PLI schemes shift value onshore over time.

The anatomy of a content cycle

  1. A rule or feature arrives (fuel injection, LED lamps, digital clusters, ABS, EV drivetrains), raising the value of electronics per vehicle.
  2. Suppliers win design-ins on new models, which lock in revenue for five to seven years.
  3. Revenue outgrows volumes for several years; margins expand with scale and localisation.
  4. Input or currency shocks interrupt it (copper in 2026, chips in 2021–22), compressing margins until pass-through catches up.

Patterns that repeat

  • This basket beats 023 in most upcycles (2023: +78% vs +58%), because content growth compounds on top of volumes.
  • Copper and wages hit harness makers specifically: Motherson Sumi Wiring in 2024 and 2026.
  • Micro caps swing hardest: Danlaw +445% (2023), −48% (2025), +114% (2026).
  • New listings cluster near peaks: two large IPOs arrived in 2026 after three strong years.

11 · The global picture

Where the value is moving

Globally, vehicle electronics is dominated by Bosch, Continental, Denso, Aptiv, ZF and Hyundai Mobis, with chips from NXP, Infineon, Texas Instruments, Renesas and STMicro. The industry's direction is the "software-defined vehicle": fewer, more powerful computers running software that can be updated, replacing dozens of small controllers. Hyundai Mobis and Qualcomm announced a joint effort in this direction at CES 2026 aimed at emerging markets including India.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • Mechanical to electronic control (2000s–2020s): carburettors gave way to fuel injection, cables to sensors, bulbs to LEDs.
  • Many small controllers to few big computers: the software-defined vehicle changes who owns the value, favouring software and chip firms.
  • India from assembler to designer: SEDEMAC's listing shows Indian control-electronics design reaching public markets; the Semiconductor Mission is the supply-side counterpart.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Software-defined vehicles: centralised computing, over-the-air updates and features sold after purchase.
  2. ADAS moving down-market: cameras, radar and driver-assist features appearing on mid-priced Indian cars, with India-specific tuning for local road conditions.
  3. LED and adaptive lighting: matrix headlamps, signature lighting and LED for two-wheelers.
  4. Digital clusters and connected dashboards: TFT displays replacing analogue dials even on motorcycles.
  5. High-voltage architecture: EV harnesses, inverters, battery-management systems and DC-DC converters.
  6. Domestic semiconductors: 12 India Semiconductor Mission projects worth about ₹1.64 lakh cr, including packaging and fabrication, aimed at automotive-grade silicon.

The honest read

This is the structurally best-placed part of the auto chain: content per vehicle rises whether or not volumes do. The discipline is to pay for engineering content rather than assembly. Bosch and SEDEMAC sell know-how; Lumax and Varroc sell a technology transition; harness makers sell copper and labour, and their margins show it. Watch copper, the rupee and the pace at which domestic chips actually arrive.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What is content per vehicle today, and what does the next-generation platform add?
  2. What share of revenue is electronics versus electrical assembly?
  3. How much of copper and chip cost is contractually passed through, and with what lag?
  4. What is the LED or digital-cluster share of lighting and cluster revenue?
  5. What is the EV share of revenue and of the order book?
  6. How much of the bill of materials is imported, and what is the localisation plan?
  7. (Harness makers) How are minimum-wage revisions being absorbed?
  8. (SEDEMAC, Danlaw) How much revenue is design and software versus hardware?

Red flags

  • Revenue growth with flat absolute profit for several quarters.
  • Dependence on a single customer's model cycle.
  • Tooling revenue flattering growth without margin.
  • Newly listed names with short disclosure histories priced for perfection.

19 · Glossary

Every term you'll meet

Products and technology

Wiring harness
The bundled wires and connectors carrying power and signals (see 023).
ECU (electronic control unit)
A small computer controlling one vehicle function; a modern car has dozens.
Domain controller
A more powerful computer replacing several ECUs; the basis of software-defined vehicles.
Fuel injection system
Electronically metered fuel delivery; required from BS-VI onwards.
Ignition system / CDI
The electronics that fire the spark plug; India Nippon's core product.
Instrument cluster
The display behind the steering wheel or handlebar; analogue, hybrid or full TFT.
TFT display
A colour screen used in digital clusters and infotainment.
LED lighting
Light-emitting diode lamps replacing halogen bulbs, at two to four times the value per vehicle.
Matrix / adaptive headlamp
Lighting that changes its beam pattern automatically.
Starter-generator
A combined starter motor and generator enabling start-stop and mild hybrids; SEDEMAC's speciality.
Motor controller / inverter
The electronics that drive an EV motor.
BMS
Battery management system (see 022).
ADAS
Advanced driver-assistance systems (see 017).
Telematics unit
The connected box transmitting vehicle data.
OBD
On-board diagnostics for emissions and fault monitoring (see 018).
Software-defined vehicle
A vehicle whose functions are controlled and updated by software (see 017).
Automotive-grade chip
A semiconductor qualified for vehicle temperature, vibration and reliability standards.
CAN bus
The internal network over which a vehicle's controllers talk to each other; the reason a single wire pair can carry dozens of signals.
Connector
The plug that joins one part of a harness to another; a large share of harness cost and the most common source of electrical faults.
Crimping
Pressing a metal terminal onto a wire end to make a gas-tight joint without solder; the core skilled step in harness assembly.
DC-DC converter
Steps an EV's high-voltage battery down to the 12-volt supply the lights, horn and electronics still run on.
Functional safety (ISO 26262)
The standard governing electronics whose failure could injure someone; compliance work is a barrier to entry in controllers.
EMI / EMC
Electromagnetic interference and compatibility: making sure one electronic part does not disturb another. Testing for it is part of Danlaw's business.
Over-the-air (OTA) update
Changing vehicle software remotely, without a workshop visit; the feature that makes software-defined vehicles possible.
Potting
Encasing a circuit board in resin to survive heat, vibration and water; standard for two-wheeler electronics.
IP rating
A two-digit measure of how well a part resists dust and water, as in IP67; quoted on lamps, connectors and controllers.
Sensor fusion
Combining camera, radar and other inputs into one picture of the road; the computing layer underneath ADAS.

Business terms

Design-in
Being specified into a model's architecture before production (see 023).
Content per vehicle
The value of a supplier's parts per vehicle (see 023).
Tooling revenue
One-time income for making moulds and dies for a new model; lumpy and lower margin.
Localisation
Making imported content domestically (see 017).
Technology licence / JV partner
A foreign partner providing designs, as Stanley does for Lumax.
Aftermarket
Replacement parts sold after sale; Bosch's large, steady business (see 023).
Listing premium
The gain on an IPO's first trading day; Dhoot's was 37.8%.
Exceptional item
A one-off gain or loss that distorts year-on-year profit comparisons (see 021).
Minimum-wage revision
A state-mandated wage increase; a direct cost for labour-intensive assembly.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. Why does this industry grow faster than vehicle sales?

Electronics content per vehicle is rising from roughly 30–35% of car cost in 2020 towards 45–55% by 2030.

2. Why did Motherson Sumi Wiring's profit stay flat on 37% revenue growth?

3. What is the difference between a harness maker and a controller maker?
