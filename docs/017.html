Passenger Vehicles

Cars, SUVs and vans. India is now the world's third-largest car market, dominated by a Japanese-owned champion (Maruti Suzuki), a home-grown SUV specialist (Mahindra), a Korean (Hyundai) and a Tata company that also owns Jaguar Land Rover. The product has moved from small hatchbacks to SUVs, and the powertrain is starting to move from petrol to CNG, hybrids and batteries.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Selling India its cars

Explain it to a 10-year-old

A car company is like a very big Lego builder. It designs the car, buys about 70% of the pieces from other companies (seats, tyres, lights, engines' small parts, electronics), and puts them together on a long assembly line: a car rolls off roughly every minute. Then it sends the cars to showrooms owned by dealers, who sell them to families, often with a bank loan. Indian families used to buy small, cheap cars. Now most want tall SUVs that look strong. Petrol is getting expensive and the air in cities is dirty, so the government is pushing companies to make cars that use less fuel or run on batteries.

Say it to a client in one minute

India sold a record 4.64 million passenger vehicles in FY26, helped by GST 2.0 (September 2025), which cut the tax on small cars from 28%+ to 18%. Four listed makers matter:

  • Maruti Suzuki: ~40% share, the small-car and CNG leader, owned 58% by Suzuki.
  • Mahindra: India's #1 SUV maker by revenue share (25%).
  • Hyundai India: listed October 2024.
  • Tata Motors Passenger Vehicles: Indian cars and EVs plus Jaguar Land Rover.

The industry's economics are volume times margin. Volumes are fine in 2026 (Maruti +29%), but margins fell as aluminium, steel and gas costs rose after the Hormuz shock and fuel prices went up ~8%. The stocks are 19–57% off their peaks. The long-term issues are the SUV mix (good for Mahindra and Tata, harder for Maruti), CAFE III from April 2027, and the EV transition, where Tata and Mahindra lead and Maruti is late but has a plan.

Fuel types

  • Petrol: still the majority.
  • Diesel: now mostly in SUVs (Mahindra, Tata, Hyundai); Maruti exited diesel in 2020 with BS-VI.
  • CNG: factory-fitted CNG for cost-conscious buyers and taxis; Maruti dominates.
  • Strong hybrids: Toyota and Maruti (Grand Vitara, Hyryder, Innova Hycross).
  • Battery electric (BEV): ~6% of cars in 2026; Tata leads, with Mahindra (BE 6, XEV 9e), MG, Hyundai and Maruti (e-Vitara) behind.
Analogy

The car market is like a clothes market where fashion shifted from jeans to jackets. Hatchbacks are jeans (useful, cheap, everyone had one); SUVs are jackets (bought for how they look and how safe they feel). Makers who sell jackets (Mahindra, Tata, Hyundai) did well; the jeans champion (Maruti) had to learn to make jackets fast.

Everyday examples

A WagonR CNG is a Delhi taxi driver's workhorse. A Creta is the default family SUV in a metro. A Thar is a lifestyle purchase. A Nexon EV might be a Bengaluru software engineer's first electric car. A Range Rover (JLR, owned by Tata) is a status symbol in London, Dubai and Mumbai.

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Materials: a car is mostly steel (the body), aluminium (engine and wheels), plastics (the dashboard), rubber (tyres) and glass, plus tiny computer chips.
  • Components: hundreds of other companies make parts; the car maker buys most of them.
  • Design: a new model takes three to five years and hundreds of crores to design; companies reuse the same "skeleton" (platform) for many models to save money.
  • Assembly: big machines stamp steel sheets into doors and roofs, robots weld them, the body is painted, and workers fit the engine, seats and wheels.
  • Dealers: the car goes to a showroom. The company counts a sale when it ships the car to the dealer (a "dispatch"); the family's purchase shows up later as a "registration".
Fund-manager lens

Car makers are assemblers with big fixed costs: plants, design and brand. Profit comes from filling plants (utilisation), selling richer models (mix) and controlling what you pay suppliers (commodity pass-through). A 10% volume swing moves profit far more, which is why margins, not volumes, explained 2026's fall. Watch the gap between dispatches and retail sales: when dealers are stuffed with inventory, discounts and a stock correction follow.

Typical EBITDA margins: Maruti 10–13% in good years, Mahindra auto 12–15%, Hyundai India 12–14%, Tata India PV 6–8%, and JLR 12–15% EBITDA (EBIT 5–9%). Capital intensity is moderate. A new plant costs ₹10,000–20,000 cr, and each new model hundreds of crores.

Analogy

A car plant is like a cinema hall. The rent and staff cost the same whether it's full or empty, and the popcorn (top trims, accessories, service) is where the margin is. A hit film (a hit SUV) fills the hall for years; a flop leaves it empty.

7 · The business calendar

Festivals, price rises and monthly numbers

  • The 1st of every month: makers publish dispatch (wholesale) numbers, and the stocks react. Vahan registrations give daily retail data.
  • January and April: annual price increases (and a "buy before the hike" pull-forward in December and March).
  • March: the financial year-end push to hit targets.
  • Monsoon (June–August): slower retail; plant maintenance shutdowns.
  • Navratri to Diwali (September–November): the biggest retail season; dealers stock up from August.
  • December: year-end discounts to clear stock before the model year changes.

8 · What the stocks do

Seasonality test on TradingView prices

Basket: 4 names (Maruti Suzuki India Limited, Mahindra & Mahindra Ltd., Hyundai Motor India Limited, Tata Motors Passenger Vehicles Ltd), equal-weighted, monthly from Jul 2011, compared with the Nifty 50.

  • Strongest months vs the Nifty: Apr +4.1 pts (beat in 13 of 15 years); Jan +2.1 pts (beat in 9 of 15 years); Oct +2.0 pts (beat in 10 of 15 years).
  • Weakest months: Mar -3.0 pts (beat in 5 of 15 years); Feb -0.7 pts (beat in 5 of 15 years); Jul -0.5 pts (beat in 8 of 16 years).
  • Windows: Feb–Mar beat the Nifty in 4 of 15 years (median -4.1 pts); Apr–Jul beat the Nifty in 10 of 15 years (median +6.2 pts); Aug–Sep beat the Nifty in 8 of 14 years (median +1.3 pts); Oct–Dec beat the Nifty in 6 of 14 years (median -0.3 pts); Sep–Nov beat the Nifty in 8 of 14 years (median +3.7 pts); Jan–Mar beat the Nifty in 6 of 15 years (median -4.5 pts).

Control: small caps as a whole (Nifty Smallcap 100) beat the Nifty in April in 11 of 15 years, by an average of 3.0 points, and lag in January–February. Part of any April strength is this market-wide effect. After removing it, this industry's own April edge is about +1.1 points.

Reading it: April is the most reliable month (13 of 15 years), and April–July beat the Nifty in 10 of 15 years (median +6.2 points). February–March is weak (4 of 15). October–December beat the Nifty in only 6 of 14 years even though it contains the festive season: strong festive sales are usually priced in by September, and the stocks fade into year-end discounting. These are large caps, so the small-cap April effect barely applies here.

A tendency, not a rule: small samples, survivorship bias (only names still listed) and equal weighting. Use it for timing context, not as a signal.

The exceptions, and why

  • April misses: 2021 (−4.2), when India's second COVID wave hit showrooms, and 2025 (−0.5). The best Aprils were 2020 (+13.6, the rebound from the crash), 2013 (+12.0) and 2022 (+7.8).
  • April–July failures: 2012 (−9.8, the Manesar plant violence), 2015 (−1.4), 2018 (−8.1, the NBFC squeeze and JLR), 2019 (−14.2, the slowdown), 2021 (−9.6, the second wave and chip shortage). The best were 2020 (+38.5) and 2022 (+22.5).
  • October–December beat the Nifty in only 6 of 14 years. The biggest misses were 2022 (−9.2), 2018 (−8.8), 2025 (−8.5, after the GST-cut rally) and 2024 (−6.7). The exceptions were 2021 (+18.0, easing chips) and 2019 (+14.0, the corporate tax cut and festive recovery).
  • February–March beat the Nifty in only 4 of 15 years (2020 −21.4 as COVID began).
How to use this

The pattern is "buy the spring, sell the festival". Car stocks tend to do best from April to July, when the new financial year's volumes and price increases come through, and fade in October–December once festive demand is known. It works better for large caps than the small-cap patterns in chemicals, but margins and commodity costs override it (2026).

9 · What moves demand and margins

Nine drivers

  1. Income and credit: about 80% of cars are bought on loans, so interest rates and bank and NBFC lending matter (the 2018–19 NBFC crisis hurt).
  2. Taxes: GST 2.0 cut small cars to 18% (from 28% plus cess) and set 40% for larger cars in September 2025, a big affordability boost.
  3. Fuel prices: petrol and diesel rose ~8% in May 2026; that pushes buyers to CNG, hybrids and EVs.
  4. Commodity costs: steel, aluminium and precious metals; a 300 bp margin hit for Maruti in Q1 FY27.
  5. Mix and launches: hit SUVs (Scorpio-N, XUV700, Creta, Nexon) and product cycles.
  6. Regulation costs: BS-VI (2020), RDE (2023), six-airbag and safety rules, CAFE III (April 2027), each adding cost per car.
  7. Supply shocks: chips (2021–22), rare-earth magnets (2025), supplier fires (2026).
  8. Currency: the yen (Maruti), won (Hyundai) and pound (JLR); exports.
  9. JLR: China, the US (tariffs), Europe and the EV transition drive Tata Motors PV's profit.

The anatomy of a car cycle

  1. Recovery: lower rates, a tax cut (2014 excise, 2025 GST) or pent-up demand (2020–21) lifts volumes, and operating leverage lifts margins.
  2. Boom: waiting periods, few discounts and record margins (2022–23 SUVs).
  3. Peak signs: rising dealer inventory, discounts, commodity inflation, regulatory cost steps.
  4. Downturn: a credit squeeze (2018–19), commodity squeeze (2021, 2026) or demand payback after a stimulus; margins fall faster than volumes.

Patterns that repeat

  • Margins, not volumes, set the stock: in 2021 and 2026 volumes held up but commodity costs cut margins and the stocks fell.
  • Tax cuts create booms and paybacks: the 2014 excise cut and the 2025 GST cut both lifted sales; the year after is harder.
  • Product leadership moves share: Mahindra's SUV run (2022–24) and Tata's EVs (2021) re-rated them; Maruti's late SUV shift cost it share.
  • JLR decides Tata's stock: 2012 +75%, 2018 −60% and 2023 +101% were all about JLR.

11 · The global picture

India is the third-largest car market

China sells about 30 million vehicles a year and the US about 16 million. India passed Japan in 2022 to become the third-largest vehicle market. Its cars are cheaper and smaller than in the West, and it is one of the few big markets still growing. Global makers treat India as both a market and an export base for small cars.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • Ambassador and Premier → Maruti (1983 onwards): the Maruti 800 made the car a middle-class product.
  • Maruti's dominance → the SUV shift (2016–25): Mahindra, Hyundai, Tata and Kia won share with SUVs; Maruti fell from ~50% to ~40%.
  • US makers exit: GM (2017) and Ford (2021) quit Indian manufacturing, and their plants went to Hyundai (Talegaon) and Tata (Sanand).
  • The EV shift: China leads globally; in India Tata took an early lead, with Mahindra, MG and now Maruti and Hyundai following.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Electrification: BEVs at ~6% of cars in 2026, heading for ~13% by FY30 (Nomura), with cheaper LFP batteries and local cell plants.
  2. Hybrids and CNG: the bridge technologies. Maruti and Toyota push strong hybrids; Maruti leads CNG. Both help meet CAFE III.
  3. Ethanol and flex-fuel: E20 petrol is now standard; flex-fuel engines (E85, E100) are in development (see 005 for ethanol).
  4. Safety and ADAS: Bharat NCAP star ratings (from 2023), six airbags and driver-assist systems in mid-range cars.
  5. Software-defined vehicles: connected cars, over-the-air updates, digital cockpits.
  6. Platforms and modularity: fewer, more flexible platforms (Maruti's Heartect, Mahindra's INGLO EV platform).

The honest read

This looks like a margin cycle, not a demand cycle. Management guides to margin recovery as commodity contracts reset, and the key question is whether prices can be raised into a post-GST-boom market. CAFE III (2027) adds cost for everyone, and most for makers without EVs. Tata PV is a JLR recovery bet; Mahindra has the best product momentum; Maruti has the cheapest route to recovery if commodities ease.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. How much margin was lost to commodities this quarter, and how much will come back as contracts reset?
  2. What is dealer inventory in days, and how have discounts moved?
  3. What share of volumes is SUV, CNG, hybrid and EV, and how will you meet CAFE III?
  4. What is the launch pipeline for the next 18 months?
  5. What is export growth by region?
  6. (Maruti) What are the royalty rate and yen exposure? When does Kharkhoda reach full utilisation?
  7. (Tata PV) What are JLR's order book, US tariff impact and EV launch timing?

Red flags

  • Dispatches running ahead of registrations (channel stuffing).
  • Rising discounts during a supposed boom.
  • Margins falling with volumes rising, beyond commodity effects.
  • Heavy capex into segments losing share.

18 · Myths and live debates

What you'll hear, and the evidence

ClaimEvidence
"India's car market will grow like China's did."Car ownership is still very low per 1,000 people, but growth has been 5–8% a year, not China's 2000s pace. Affordability and roads limit it.
"Maruti has lost its way."Its share fell as SUVs grew, but it added SUVs (Brezza, Grand Vitara, Fronx), leads CNG and hybrids, and grew volumes 29% in Q1 FY27. The 2026 problem is margin, not demand.
"EVs will kill Maruti."EVs are 6% of cars. CNG and hybrids will carry most of the CAFE burden this decade, and Maruti leads both; its e-Vitara is also exported.
"Tata Motors PV is an Indian car stock."JLR is most of its profit; its stock moves with China, the US and UK luxury demand.
"The GST cut is permanent growth."It lowered prices once; the growth boost fades after a year (the high-base effect in FY27).

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. Why did car stocks fall in 2026 even though volumes grew?

Margins fell: aluminium, steel and gas costs rose after the Hormuz shock (Maruti's margin −380 bp), and the market expects slower growth after the GST boost.

2. What did GST 2.0 change for cars?

From 22 September 2025, small cars moved to 18% (from 28% plus cess), larger cars to a flat 40%, and EVs stay at 5%.

3. What is the difference between dispatches and registrations?

Dispatches are factory shipments to dealers (reported monthly by makers); registrations (Vahan) are actual retail sales.

4. Why has Maruti's market share fallen from ~50% to ~40%?

Buyers shifted to SUVs, where Mahindra, Hyundai, Tata and Kia were stronger, and Maruti's hatchback and sedan base shrank.

5. What decides Tata Motors PV's profit?

Mostly Jaguar Land Rover: its volumes, margins and markets (China, the US, the UK).

6. What is CAFE III, and who is most exposed?

Fleet fuel-efficiency norms from April 2027; makers without EVs, hybrids or CNG face penalties.

7. Which seasonal window works best, and which fails?

April–July (10 of 15 years) works; October–December beat the Nifty in only 6 of 14 years because festive demand is priced in.

8. What caused the 2019 slump?

The NBFC credit crisis, rising ownership costs and the BS-VI transition; FY20 car sales fell 18%.

9. Why was 2021 bad for Maruti but great for Tata?

Chips and commodities hurt Maruti's small-car margins, while Tata's new SUVs, the Nexon EV and TPG's EV investment re-rated it.

10. What share of car sales is electric in 2026?

About 6% (January–July), with forecasts of ~13% by FY30.
