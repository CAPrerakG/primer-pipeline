Power Transmission & Distribution Utilities

The wires. Generation gets the attention, but electricity is worthless where it is made — it has to travel from the deserts of Rajasthan and the coalfields of Odisha to the cities that consume it. The companies that own those lines earn a regulated return on every rupee of steel and conductor they put in the ground, which makes this the most predictable business in the Power family and, right now, the one with the largest building programme.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

A toll road for electrons, with a building boom

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Electricity is made in big power stations far from where people live — in deserts where the sun is strong, or next to coal mines. To get it to your house, it travels along huge steel towers carrying thick cables, sometimes for a thousand kilometres. The companies that build and own those towers do not make electricity and do not sell it; they charge a fee for letting it pass, like a toll bridge. The government decides the fee, and it is set so the company earns a fixed profit on the money it spent building the line. So the only way to earn more is to build more lines — and India needs an enormous number of new ones, because the new power stations are in places the old wires do not reach.

Say it to a client in one minute

This is the least cyclical business in the power chain, with the clearest multi-year capital-expenditure runway.

  • The programme: the National Electricity Plan envisages about ₹9.15 lakh cr of transmission investment through 2032, with roughly 1,37,500 circuit kilometres of new lines; ICRA expects ₹5–6 trillion of capex by FY32.
  • Power Grid is the incumbent: capex ₹7,765 cr in the quarter (+11%), capitalisation ₹5,277 cr (more than tripled), and FY27 guidance of ₹37,000 cr capex and ₹30,000 cr capitalisation on a ₹3.25 lakh cr asset base.
  • Adani Energy Solutions is the challenger: profit +130% to ₹1,237 cr, EBITDA above ₹3,000 cr for the first time, a ₹71,779 cr transmission order book and 24.6 million smart meters ordered.
  • The risk is execution, not demand. Only about 12% of competitively bid projects commissioned by March 2026 finished on schedule; delays run from two months to three years on land and right-of-way issues.
  • Read the basket carefully: its −18% is dominated by Reliance Infrastructure, down 70% and now tradeable only once a week under a 5% band.

Analogy

Transmission is the national highway and distribution is the lane to your gate. The highway operator is paid a toll fixed by a regulator to give it a set return on construction cost — it does not care who is driving or what they are carrying. The lane operator has a harder job: it must collect from every household, and in India a substantial share of what it delivers is never paid for at all.

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Planning: engineers work out which new lines the country will need in ten years.
  • Award: companies compete by offering the lowest toll, and the winner gets to build and own the line for 35 years.
  • Right of way: the line must cross thousands of people's land, and each one must agree. This is what takes years.
  • Construction: steel towers go up and cables are strung between them.
  • Capitalisation: the moment the line is switched on, the company starts earning its fee.
  • Operation: as long as the line is available to carry power, the fee is paid — whether or not power actually flows.
Fund-manager lens

This is the purest asset-growth business in the market: earnings are a regulated return on capitalised assets, so the only meaningful forecast is how much gets commissioned and when. Watch capitalisation, not capex — Power Grid spent ₹7,765 cr but capitalised ₹5,277 cr, and only the second number starts earning. Watch the order book against the delivery record, because a ₹71,779 cr book is worth what the company can actually commission, and the industry's on-time record is 12%. And watch the regulated return itself, which is periodically reviewed and is the one thing that can reprice the entire sector overnight.

Why capitalisation matters more than capex

A transmission utility spends money for years before earning anything from it. Capital expenditure is cash going out; capitalisation is the moment a completed line enters the regulated base and begins earning its allowed return. The two can diverge sharply, and Power Grid's June quarter shows why it matters: capex rose 11% to ₹7,765 cr, but capitalisation more than tripled to ₹5,277 cr, meaning a large tranche of previously funded work finally came on stream. For the competitively bid projects that now dominate new awards, the arithmetic is harsher still — the tariff is fixed for 35 years at the bid price, so every month of delay and every rupee of cost overrun comes directly out of the return. With only about 12% of such projects commissioned on schedule and delays running to three years, the gap between an order book and an earning asset is the central analytical problem in this industry.

Analogy

Winning a transmission bid is like agreeing to build a bridge for a fixed toll set today, collectible for 35 years starting whenever the bridge opens. If the land takes three extra years, you still get the same toll — you simply get it later, having paid interest throughout. That is why the on-time record, not the order book, is the number that decides returns.

7 · The business calendar

Build in the dry, capitalise before March

  • October–June: the construction season. Foundations and stringing proceed in dry weather, which is why the two positive windows sit either side of the monsoon.
  • July–September: the monsoon halts tower erection and stringing; the weakest stretch operationally and for the shares.
  • January–March: the capitalisation push before the financial year ends — good for the accounts, historically poor for the share prices.
  • Tariff and regulatory cycles: multi-year tariff periods reset the allowed return; these are step changes, not seasonal ones.
  • Bid calendar: competitively bid schemes are tendered irregularly; 41 were under bidding at last count.

  • Strongest months vs the Nifty: Apr +3.6 pts (beat in 9 of 15 years); Jun +3.4 pts (beat in 11 of 15 years); Mar +2.4 pts (beat in 10 of 15 years).
  • Weakest months: Feb -2.5 pts (beat in 5 of 15 years); Sep -1.5 pts (beat in 7 of 15 years); Jul -1.3 pts (beat in 6 of 16 years).
  • Windows: Feb–Mar beat the Nifty in 8 of 15 years (median +0.8 pts); Apr–Jul beat the Nifty in 10 of 15 years (median +3.1 pts); Aug–Sep beat the Nifty in 6 of 14 years (median -1.3 pts); Oct–Dec beat the Nifty in 9 of 14 years (median +4.1 pts); Sep–Nov beat the Nifty in 7 of 14 years (median -1.4 pts); Jan–Mar beat the Nifty in 5 of 15 years (median -5.0 pts).

Control: small caps as a whole (Nifty Smallcap 100) beat the Nifty in April in 11 of 15 years, by an average of 3.0 points, and lag in January–February. Part of any April strength is this market-wide effect. After removing it, this industry's own April edge is about +0.6 points.

Reading it: two usable windows and one clear trap. April–July works in 10 of 15 years (median +3.1) and October–December in 9 of 14 (median +4.1) — the construction season either side of the monsoon, when lines are strung and assets are capitalised. January–March is the trap: 5 of 15 years and a median of −5.0, with failures in ten separate years. August–September is also weak (6 of 14), which is when transmission construction stops for the rains.

A tendency, not a rule: small samples, survivorship bias (only names still listed) and equal weighting. Use it for timing context, not as a signal.

The exceptions, and why

  • April–July worked in 10 of 15 years (median +3.1), failing in 2018 (−17.7) and 2019 (−14.9) — both years dominated by Reliance Infrastructure's collapse rather than by the transmission cycle.
  • October–December works 9 of 14 (median +4.1), the post-monsoon construction restart.
  • January–March has failed in ten of fifteen years, median −5.0, despite being the quarter in which most capitalisation happens — the accounts and the share prices point in opposite directions.
  • The 2021 readings are extreme in every window because two of the four names roughly quadrupled that year, one on a genuine re-rating and one on a distressed bounce.
How to use this

Use the construction calendar, not the seasonality table: this basket has four names, one of which has been a distressed vehicle for most of the period, so the statistics carry a lot of noise. The mechanism is nonetheless sound — lines are built in the dry months, capitalised at year-end, and the regulated return follows. For a regulated utility, the more useful cadence is quarterly capitalisation against annual guidance, which for Power Grid means tracking ₹30,000 cr of FY27 capitalisation against ₹5,277 cr achieved in the first quarter.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Eight drivers

  1. Capitalisation rate: the pace at which projects enter the earning asset base.
  2. The allowed return on equity: under periodic regulatory review, and the one variable that reprices the whole sector.
  3. Order book and bid wins: 92 competitively bid schemes worth ₹1.24 lakh cr awarded, 41 more under bidding.
  4. Execution timeliness: only about 12% on time; delays of two months to three years directly erode bid returns.
  5. Renewable build-out: new generation in Rajasthan and Gujarat creates the need for new corridors (see 038).
  6. Right-of-way and clearance processes: the practical determinant of delivery.
  7. Smart metering roll-out: an annuity business layered on the wires — 24.6 million meters ordered.
  8. Distribution loss reduction: the efficiency lever in the retail half of the business.

The anatomy of a transmission cycle

  1. Generation is built somewhere new — coalfields in the 2010s, renewable zones now — and the existing grid cannot carry it.
  2. A national plan is drawn up and corridors are awarded, increasingly by competitive bidding.
  3. Capital is deployed for years before anything earns, and the delivery record determines who actually makes the bid return.
  4. Assets capitalise and earnings step up, steadily and with little cyclicality — which is why this section's problems are almost always financial distress at one company rather than an industry downturn.

Patterns that repeat

  • The defensive characteristic is real: 2015 (+3.0% against a Nifty −4.1%) and 2012 (+42.3%) both came in difficult years for generation.
  • One distressed name has dominated the aggregate for a decade. Reliance Infrastructure fell 91% in 2019, rose 258% in 2021, and is down 70% now.
  • Group events can overwhelm fundamentals: Adani Energy Solutions fell 60% in 2023 while its transmission business kept growing, and is still 65% below its 2022 peak.
  • The capital-expenditure story peaks with sentiment, not with capitalisation: most names topped in late 2024 while capitalisation accelerated into 2026.

11 · The global picture

Everyone is short of wires

The global energy transition has run into the same constraint everywhere: renewable generation can be built in eighteen months, and the transmission to evacuate it takes five to ten years. Europe, the United States and India all now have queues of generation waiting for grid connection — India's version is the roughly 45 GW of connectivity granted or applied for that has no contract behind it (see 038). India's response is unusually structured: a national plan of about ₹9.15 lakh cr through 2032, tariff-based competitive bidding as the default award route, and a single national grid already operating synchronously. The weakness is delivery — only about 12% of competitively bid projects have been commissioned on schedule — which is a land and clearance problem rather than an engineering or financing one.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • From a single state monopoly to competitive bidding: transmission awards moved from cost-plus allocation to tariff-based bidding, which is now the default and has let private developers take a meaningful share.
  • From coal corridors to renewable corridors: the destination of new lines has moved from the eastern coalfields to the western desert states.
  • From wires to wires-plus-metering: smart metering has turned distribution into an annuity contracting business, which is why Adani Energy Solutions holds an order book of 24.6 million meters.

12 · Where the business is going

Six shifts

  1. High-voltage direct current: long-distance links with lower losses, essential for moving desert renewables to load centres.
  2. Renewable energy zones and pooling substations: grid built ahead of generation, to shorten the queue.
  3. Smart metering at scale: prepaid meters that fix the distribution revenue leak — 13.4 million installed by one company alone.
  4. Grid-scale storage on the network: batteries owned by the grid rather than the generator, to manage the evening ramp (see 046).
  5. Digital substations and monitoring: reducing outage time and manpower.
  6. Distribution privatisation and franchises: the slow, politically difficult reform that would fix the sector's credit problem (see 037).

The honest read

Transmission has the clearest multi-year visibility of anything in the Power family: a national plan of roughly ₹9.15 lakh cr, a pipeline that grows every time a renewable zone is announced, and an incumbent whose capitalisation just tripled. What has changed is who takes the risk. Competitive bidding transfers cost and delay onto the developer, and with only about 12% of such projects finishing on time, the gap between winning work and earning on it is wide and widening. That favours operators who can actually execute — which is the strongest argument for Adani Energy Solutions' 130% profit growth being more than a group story, and the reason Power Grid's tripled capitalisation matters more than its flat profit. The two things to hold in mind are the regulatory return review, which can reprice the whole asset base, and the fact that this section's headline return is currently being written by a distressed company that trades once a week.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What was capitalised this quarter, and what is the full-year target?
  2. What is the gap between capex incurred and capitalisation, and why?
  3. How many projects are behind their scheduled commissioning date, and by how long?
  4. What return was assumed in recent competitive bids, and what is the sensitivity to a one-year delay?
  5. What is the status of the regulated return review, and what would a change mean for earnings?
  6. What is the right-of-way status on the largest projects?
  7. (Smart metering) What is the annuity per meter, and the working capital cycle on installation?
  8. (Distribution) What are aggregate technical and commercial losses, and the trajectory?

Red flags

  • Order book growing while capitalisation stalls.
  • Bids won at tariffs that assume flawless execution in an industry with a 12% on-time record.
  • Capex guidance met but capitalisation repeatedly deferred.
  • Group-level financing events overshadowing operating disclosure.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. How does a transmission utility earn?

A regulated return on capitalised assets, paid for making the line available rather than for the power that flows through it.

2. Why is capitalisation more important than capex?

Capex is cash spent; capitalisation is the moment an asset starts earning. Power Grid's capex rose 11% while capitalisation more than tripled to ₹5,277 cr.

3. How large is the building programme?

About ₹9.15 lakh cr through 2032 under the National Electricity Plan, including roughly 1,37,500 ckm of lines; ICRA expects ₹5–6 trillion of capex by FY32.

4. What is TBCB, and what does it change?

Tariff-based competitive bidding: the lowest 35-year tariff wins. It transfers land, cost and delay risk to the developer at a fixed price.

5. What is the industry's central execution problem?

Only about 12% of competitively bid projects commissioned by March 2026 were on schedule, with delays of two months to three years, mostly from land and right-of-way issues.

6. Why is the basket's −18% misleading?

Reliance Infrastructure is down 70% on distress and trades once a week under a 5% band. The median stock is down 7%.

7. What did Adani Energy Solutions report?

Income ₹9,852 cr (+40%), EBITDA ₹3,178 cr (+58%) and profit ₹1,237 cr (+130%), on a ₹71,779 cr transmission order book.

8. What is the smart metering opportunity?

An annuity per meter over the contract life: 24.6 million meters ordered and 13.4 million installed at one company, plus the proposed IntelliSmart acquisition.

9. What is the single biggest risk to the sector's earnings?

A change in the allowed return on equity, which reprices the entire regulated asset base at once.

10. Why does transmission matter to the renewable story?

Generation can be built in eighteen months and corridors take five to ten years — which is why roughly 45 GW of renewable connectivity is waiting (see 038).
