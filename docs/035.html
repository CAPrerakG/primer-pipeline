Lubricants & Specialty Oils

The last few litres of the barrel, and the most profitable. Engine oil in a branded bottle sells for many times the price of the base oil inside it; transformer fluid, white oil and rubber process oil sell on specification rather than brand. This is where the Oil & Gas family stops being a commodity chain and becomes a consumer and specification business — and where 2026's spectacular profits deserve the most careful reading.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Two businesses wearing the same label

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Every engine has moving metal parts rubbing against each other, and without a slippery liquid between them they would wear out and seize. That liquid is mostly ordinary refined oil — but a small amount of special chemicals is stirred in to stop it burning, foaming or going gummy, and the result is put in a branded bottle and sold in a shop. The oil inside might cost a hundred rupees; the bottle on the shelf sells for four hundred. That gap is the brand. Some companies instead sell tanker-loads of similar oil to factories, to electricity transformers, even to the makers of face cream and cough syrup — no bottle, no brand, just a specification. Both are good businesses, but only one of them keeps its price when raw oil gets cheap.

Say it to a client in one minute

Split the section in two before doing anything else.

  • Branded marketers — Castrol, Gulf Oil, Veedol — sell into a fragmented retail and workshop network. Castrol's Q2 revenue rose 25% with a 26.4% EBITDA margin, and 160,000 outlets is the moat. Gulf Oil grew lubricant volumes 17% to a record 48,000 kilolitres.
  • Base-oil converters — Savita, Gandhar, GP Petroleums — sell specification products: transformer oil, white oil, process oils. Their 2026 profits exploded: Savita's profit up 396% with a 24.76% margin, Gandhar's up 689%.
  • That explosion is mostly a spread event. Base oil prices moved, stock bought earlier was sold dearer, and margins tripled. It is real cash, and it is not a run-rate. Castrol says as much about its own quarter.
  • The volume story is modest: Indian lubricant volumes are forecast to grow around 3% a year. Value growth comes from premiumisation, not litres.
  • The genuinely interesting structural change is Savita's synthetic ester transformer fluid plant, commissioned in February 2026, aimed at grid, data-centre and energy-storage demand.

Analogy

Base oil is flour. A branded lubricant company is a bakery selling packaged bread under a name people trust, at a price that barely moves when flour does. A base-oil converter is a mill selling sacks of specialised flour to other manufacturers — it earns whatever the spread between wheat and flour happens to be that month. When wheat prices swing, the mill's profits swing violently and the bakery's barely move. In 2026 the mills made fortunes.

Everyday example

The ₹450 litre of engine oil your mechanic pours into a motorcycle contains perhaps ₹120 of base oil and ₹40 of additives. What you are paying for is the brand, the assurance it is not counterfeit, and the mechanic's margin. That is why Castrol can hold a 21–24% structural EBITDA margin in a business whose raw material is a commodity.

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Base oil: cleaned-up oil from a refinery, usually shipped in from abroad.
  • Additives: a small dose of chemicals that decides whether the oil protects an engine for 10,000 km or 3,000.
  • Blending: the two are mixed in big tanks and tested — not very difficult, which is why anybody can make lubricant.
  • Distribution: getting the bottle into a hundred thousand small shops and workshops is the difficult part, and the profitable one.
  • Use: the oil is replaced every few thousand kilometres, so demand depends on how much people drive, not on how many cars are sold.
Fund-manager lens

Blending is not a barrier to entry; distribution and approvals are. That single fact explains the whole section. A branded marketer with 160,000 outlets earns a stable 21–24% margin through the cycle because the shelf, not the plant, is scarce. A converter selling bulk specification oil earns the spread between base oil and product, which in a stable year is modest and in a year like 2026 is enormous. The analytical error to avoid is annualising a converter's windfall quarter: Gandhar's profit rose 689% on an 8% increase in manufacturing volume, which tells you precisely how much of it was price and inventory rather than business growth.

How to read this section

The useful division is not by size but by where the margin comes from. Brand-led: Castrol, Gulf Oil, Veedol — stable margins, distribution moats, modest volume growth, and share prices that did nothing in 2026 despite good results. Spread-led: Savita, Gandhar, GP Petroleums — margins that tripled on a base-oil move, and share prices up 75–85%. One name, Aztec Fluids, is not in this industry at all. Judged over a full cycle, the brand businesses have been the better compounders; judged over 2026, the converters won by a distance. Knowing which you own matters more here than in any other section of this family.

The 2026 arithmetic, done honestly

Gandhar Oil's profit rose 689% while its manufacturing volume rose 8%. Savita's profit rose 396% and its EBITDA over 500%. No business grows its economics sevenfold on 8% more output through operational improvement; what happened is that base oil prices moved sharply and converters sold inventory bought at lower cost into a higher-priced market, at the same time as the spread between base oil and finished specification products widened. This is exactly the mechanism described for Panama Petrochem in 032, and the same caution applies — it is genuine cash profit, and it reverses when feedstock prices fall. The most useful corroboration comes from the most disciplined operator in the section: Castrol reported a 26.4% margin and told investors its structural range remains 21–24%, naming inventory as the reason for the difference. When the market leader labels its own beat as partly inventory, the 689% should be read the same way.

Analogy

Two shops sell the same cooking oil. One has a famous name and charges a steady premium; the other buys and sells in bulk at whatever the market allows. When the wholesale price jumps, the bulk trader makes a fortune on the stock in his godown and the branded shop makes a little extra. When it falls back, the trader gives it all back and the branded shop carries on. Both are in the oil business; only one is in the oil price business.

7 · The business calendar

Driven by kilometres, not by car sales

  • April–June: the pre-monsoon service season — vehicles are serviced before the rains and industrial plants schedule maintenance. The strongest stretch, and it shows in the April–July record.
  • July–September: the monsoon reduces vehicle usage and construction activity, softening demand.
  • October–December: the festive and post-harvest quarter lifts freight movement and rural demand.
  • January–March: channel destocking before the financial year ends; historically the weakest window for these stocks.
  • Base oil contract cycles: imported base oil is bought on monthly or quarterly terms, which is why margins move in steps rather than continuously.

Seasonality test on TradingView prices

  • Strongest months vs the Nifty: Apr +3.2 pts (beat in 8 of 15 years); Jun +2.0 pts (beat in 7 of 15 years); Jul +1.9 pts (beat in 9 of 16 years).
  • Weakest months: Mar -2.9 pts (beat in 5 of 15 years); Nov -1.3 pts (beat in 4 of 15 years); Jan -1.2 pts (beat in 5 of 15 years).
  • Windows: Feb–Mar beat the Nifty in 4 of 15 years (median -3.5 pts); Apr–Jul beat the Nifty in 9 of 15 years (median +7.1 pts); Aug–Sep beat the Nifty in 8 of 14 years (median +0.2 pts); Oct–Dec beat the Nifty in 6 of 14 years (median -2.8 pts); Sep–Nov beat the Nifty in 5 of 14 years (median -6.5 pts); Jan–Mar beat the Nifty in 5 of 15 years (median -4.4 pts).

Control: small caps as a whole (Nifty Smallcap 100) beat the Nifty in April in 11 of 15 years, by an average of 3.0 points, and lag in January–February. Part of any April strength is this market-wide effect. After removing it, this industry's own April edge is about +0.2 points.

Reading it: one clear pattern and one clear warning. April–July has beaten the Nifty in 9 of 15 years with a median of +7.1 points, and April is the best single month (8 of 15). Against that, February–March has worked in only 4 of 15 years (median −3.5), January–March in 5 of 15 (median −4.4) and September–November in 5 of 14 (median −6.5). The April–July strength lines up with the pre-monsoon service season, when vehicles are serviced before the rains and industrial maintenance is scheduled; the weakness runs through the destocking months at the end of the financial year.

A tendency, not a rule: small samples, survivorship bias (only names still listed) and equal weighting. Use it for timing context, not as a signal.

The exceptions, and why

  • April–July failed six times in fifteen years, with 2019 (−17.4) and 2018 (−13.6) the worst — the auto slowdown and the crude spike respectively, both of which hit lubricant demand and margins together.
  • The best readings were 2026 (+44.4) and 2021 (+42.2), and both were base-oil spread events rather than demand events.
  • February–March has failed in eleven of fifteen years, a remarkably consistent pattern that matches year-end channel destocking.
  • September–November is the other weak window (5 of 14, median −6.5), with 2021 (−16.1) and 2024 (−15.3) the worst.
How to use this

April–July is a genuine, physically grounded window here — the pre-monsoon service season is real, and a nine-in-fifteen record with a +7.1 median is respectable. But note what the two biggest readings have in common: they were spread windfalls, not service seasons, which means the tail of this distribution is driven by base oil rather than by demand. The practical rule is to use April–July as an entry window for the branded names, whose seasonality actually reflects volume, and to treat the converters as positions in the base-oil spread that happen to be listed.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Eight drivers

  1. Base oil prices: the input, the inventory effect and the spread — the dominant variable for the converters.
  2. Kilometres driven and industrial activity: lubricant demand follows usage, not vehicle sales.
  3. Premiumisation: synthetic and semi-synthetic grades at higher prices; the main source of value growth in a 3% volume market.
  4. Distribution reach: outlets added, especially rural — Castrol's 45,000 rural outlets.
  5. Drain intervals: better oils and engines lengthen the gap between oil changes, a quiet structural headwind to volume.
  6. OEM tie-ups and factory fill: the channel that seeds the aftermarket.
  7. Electrical infrastructure: transformer oil demand follows grid and data-centre investment — Savita's ester plant is a direct play on it.
  8. Electric vehicles: fewer engine oils, but new coolants, greases and thermal fluids.

The anatomy of a lubricant cycle

  1. Base oil falls, usually with crude. Selling prices are sticky, so margins expand for everyone and the converters expand most — 2014's +115%.
  2. Competition passes it on through lower prices or higher trade margins, and the windfall fades — 2015 and 2016.
  3. Base oil rises, and the same mechanism runs in reverse: converters are squeezed hardest, brands defend with price increases — 2018 and 2025.
  4. Demand growth continues quietly underneath at around 3% a year in volume, with the value growth coming from premiumisation.

Patterns that repeat

  • The biggest years are input-cost years, not demand years: 2014 (+115%), 2026 (+32% with a +39% median).
  • Brands lag in windfall years and protect in bad ones. Castrol is +2% in 2026 and has never had a −40% year.
  • Volume growth is not the constraint or the opportunity — a 3% market means value comes from mix.
  • The sector falls when vehicles stop moving, not when they stop selling: 2019 and 2020 are the clearest cases.

11 · The global picture

A global brand contest fought in Indian workshops

India is among the largest lubricant markets in the world by volume, and unusually competitive: the global majors — bp's Castrol, Shell, ExxonMobil, TotalEnergies and Valvoline — compete against the state oil marketers' own brands, which reach every corner of the country through fuel-station networks (see 031), and against a long tail of regional blenders. The market is worth roughly US$2.8 bn a year in automotive lubricants alone and is growing about 3% a year in volume. Base oil, however, is not made competitively in India: most of it is imported, which means every blender in the country shares the same input risk and the same currency exposure, and differentiates only on brand, specification and distribution.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • From mineral to synthetic: higher-value synthetic and semi-synthetic oils raised realisations while lengthening drain intervals — more value per litre, fewer litres.
  • From bazaar to organised retail and workshops: branded distribution and OEM tie-ups displaced loose oil, which is the foundation of Castrol's and Gulf's economics.
  • From engine oils to electrical and thermal fluids: transformer oils, ester fluids and data-centre cooling are where growth is moving, and Savita's February 2026 ester plant is the clearest Indian expression of it.

12 · Where the business is going

Six shifts

  1. Synthetic ester transformer fluids: biodegradable, fire-safer alternatives to mineral transformer oil, now made at commercial scale in India.
  2. Data-centre and immersion cooling fluids: a genuinely new demand pool for specialty oil makers, and one Savita names explicitly.
  3. EV fluids: battery coolants, e-axle oils and greases — smaller volumes per vehicle than engine oil, but higher specification and value.
  4. Longer drain intervals: better oils reduce the number of litres sold per vehicle, forcing the industry to grow on value.
  5. AdBlue and emission fluids: a bulk, distribution-led adjacency; Gulf Oil moved 40,000 kilolitres in a quarter.
  6. Anti-counterfeiting technology: tamper-evident packs and digital authentication, protecting the brand premium that is the whole business.

The honest read

The most important thing to say about this section in 2026 is that the best-performing stocks are not the best businesses — they are the businesses with the most inventory leverage to a base-oil move. Savita and Gandhar have earned real cash and deserve credit for volume growth of 8–18% and, in Savita's case, for a genuinely strategic ester plant. But a 689% profit increase on 8% more output is a price event, and when base oil normalises the comparison base will be brutal. The branded names are the mirror image: Gulf Oil grew volumes 17% and its stock fell 11%; Castrol grew profit 43% and its stock is flat. For a fund with a multi-year horizon, that gap is the opportunity — a 21–24% margin business with 160,000 outlets, priced as though nothing happened, against converters priced on peak spreads. And the structural question to underwrite is not electric vehicles, which arrive slowly and bring their own fluids, but drain intervals: every improvement in oil quality sells fewer litres.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. How much of this quarter's gross margin was inventory gain, and what is the margin at replacement cost?
  2. What was volume growth in kilolitres, by segment?
  3. What is the premium and synthetic share of volume, and where is it going?
  4. How many outlets were added, and what is the rural share?
  5. What is the base oil sourcing strategy — contract tenure, hedging, and share by group?
  6. What is the assumed drain-interval trend, and how does it affect volume forecasts?
  7. (Converters) What is the sustainable margin once base oil stabilises?
  8. (Savita) What is the order pipeline and capacity utilisation for synthetic ester fluids?
  9. (All) What is the EV fluid revenue today, and what does it replace?

Red flags

  • Profit growth far exceeding volume growth, presented as operational improvement.
  • Capacity expansion announced at peak spreads.
  • Distribution numbers quoted as outlets "reached" rather than outlets billing.
  • Premiumisation claimed without a mix disclosure to support it.

18 · Myths and live debates

What you'll hear, and the evidence

ClaimEvidence
"The specialty oil companies have re-rated on structural growth."Gandhar's profit rose 689% on 8% more volume. That ratio is the definition of a price and inventory event.
"Castrol is a boring, ex-growth business."It grew revenue 25% and profit 43% in the quarter, holds a 21–24% structural margin and reaches 160,000 outlets — and its stock is up 2% this year.
"Electric vehicles will destroy lubricants."Slowly, and partially. EVs need coolants, greases and thermal fluids; the nearer-term volume threat is longer drain intervals on conventional engines.
"Lubricant demand follows vehicle sales."It follows kilometres driven. 2020 is the proof: vehicle sales recovered quickly, lubricant demand did not, because people were not driving.
"Blending is a moat."Blending is easy. Distribution, OEM approvals and electrical or pharmacopoeia certifications are the moats, which is why margins differ so much between companies making similar products.
"A 3% growth market cannot produce good returns."It can, through mix and distribution — but not through capacity. Volume-led expansion into a 3% market is how capital gets destroyed here.

19 · Glossary

Every term you'll meet

Products and chemistry

Lubricant
A fluid that reduces friction and wear between moving parts, and carries away heat.
Base oil
The refined mineral or synthetic oil that makes up most of a lubricant (see 032).
Group I, II, III base oils
Purity grades; higher groups are more refined, cost more and are needed for premium formulations.
Group IV and V
Synthetic base stocks — polyalphaolefins and esters — used in the highest-performance oils.
Additive package
Detergents, dispersants, anti-wear agents and viscosity modifiers that give an oil its performance.
Viscosity grade
The SAE rating, such as 10W-30, describing how an oil's thickness behaves hot and cold.
Synthetic and semi-synthetic oil
Formulations using synthetic base stocks; higher priced and longer lasting.
Grease
A thickened lubricant used where oil would drain away.
Transformer oil
Electrical-grade insulating and cooling oil (see 032).
Synthetic ester fluid
A biodegradable, fire-safer transformer fluid; Savita commissioned commercial-scale capacity in February 2026.
White oil
Ultra-pure mineral oil for cosmetics, pharmaceuticals and food contact; Gandhar is among India's largest producers.
Process oil
Oil blended into rubber, textiles or inks during manufacture (see 012, 025).
AdBlue / diesel exhaust fluid
A urea solution injected into exhaust to cut nitrogen oxides on BS-VI engines.
Immersion cooling fluid
Dielectric fluid in which data-centre servers are submerged to remove heat.
Drain interval
The distance or time between oil changes. Longer intervals mean fewer litres sold per vehicle.
Re-refining
Reprocessing used lubricant back into base oil.
Pour point
The lowest temperature at which an oil still flows; a specification that matters for cold-climate and industrial grades.
Oxidation stability
How long an oil resists breaking down under heat and air; the property that sets drain intervals.
Total base number (TBN)
An oil's reserve of alkalinity to neutralise acids from combustion; a headline engine-oil specification.
Dielectric strength
The voltage an insulating fluid withstands before it conducts; the defining test for transformer oil.
Coolant and e-axle fluid
Thermal and lubrication fluids designed for electric drivetrains (see 022).
Bright stock
A heavy, viscous base oil used in industrial and marine formulations.
Wax and petroleum jelly
Solid and semi-solid products from the same refining streams; part of Gandhar's range.

Commercial terms

Blending plant
Where base oil and additives are mixed into finished lubricant; low capital intensity and not a barrier to entry.
Kilolitre (kl)
The industry's volume unit. Gulf Oil sold a record 48,000 kl of lubricants in a quarter.
Premiumisation
Shifting the mix toward higher-priced synthetic grades; the main source of value growth (see 024).
Bazaar trade
The unorganised retail channel of small shops and mechanics.
Workshop channel
Service garages that choose the oil on the customer's behalf.
Factory fill
The oil a vehicle maker puts in at the factory, which seeds aftermarket preference (see 023).
OEM approval
A vehicle maker's certification that an oil meets its warranty specification.
Institutional and B2B sales
Bulk supply to fleets, factories and utilities, at lower margin than retail.
Inventory gain
Profit from holding stock as prices rise — the dominant factor in 2026's results (see 031, 032).
Replacement-cost margin
The margin recalculated at today's input prices; the number that survives a windfall.
Counterfeiting
Fake branded lubricant, a persistent threat to brand value in the bazaar trade.
Vehicle parc
The population of vehicles on the road, which sets replacement demand (see 025).
Structural margin band
The range a company says it manages to through the cycle — Castrol's is 21–24%.
Fill rate
The share of a distributor's order actually supplied on time; the operational measure behind a distribution moat.
Channel inventory
Stock held by distributors and retailers; destocking at financial year-end is the cause of the weak February–March window.
Toll blending
Blending someone else's formulation for a fee, taking no brand or price risk (see 032).

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. What are the two different businesses in this section?

Branded lubricant marketers (Castrol, Gulf Oil, Veedol), who earn a distribution-protected margin, and base-oil converters (Savita, Gandhar, GP Petroleums), who earn a spread.

2. How should Gandhar's 689% profit growth be read?

As a price and inventory event. Manufacturing volume rose only 8%; no business improves its economics sevenfold on that.

3. What did Castrol say about its own margin?

That the reported 26.4% was helped by inventory benefits and pricing, and that its structural range remains 21–24% — the most useful corroboration available for reading the whole sector.

4. Why is blending not a moat?

Mixing base oil with additives is technically easy and capital-light. The moats are distribution reach, OEM approvals and electrical or pharmacopoeia certifications.

5. What drives lubricant demand?

Kilometres driven and industrial activity, not vehicle sales — which is why 2020 was weak even as vehicle sales recovered.

6. How fast is the Indian market growing?

About 3% a year in volume. Value growth comes from premiumisation and mix.

7. Which seasonal window works, and why?

April–July: 9 of 15 years, median +7.1 points, matching the pre-monsoon vehicle service and industrial maintenance season.

8. What is the quiet structural threat to volumes?

Longer drain intervals. Better oils and engines mean fewer litres sold per vehicle, independent of electrification.

9. What is Savita's ester plant for?

Commercial-scale synthetic ester transformer fluids — biodegradable and fire-safer — aimed at grid, data-centre and energy-storage customers.

10. Which stocks rose in 2026, and which did not?

The converters: Savita +84%, Gandhar +79%, GP Petroleums +76%. The brands did not: Castrol +2%, Gulf Oil −11%, Veedol −16% — despite Gulf growing volumes 17%.
