Industry primer · 57 of 278 · Defence family, part 1

Aerospace & Defence Manufacturing

These are the companies that build India's fighter jets, helicopters, trainer aircraft and missiles, and the smaller firms that make their doors, hydraulics, composites, cables and submarine machinery. For most of the last decade the story has been told in order books: the government has placed record contracts with Indian manufacturers. The harder question — the one that now decides the share prices — is how fast those orders can be turned into delivered aircraft and missiles when the engines, seekers and many materials still come from abroad.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Orders are no longer the constraint; delivery is

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Imagine a bakery that has taken orders for thousands of birthday cakes, enough to keep it busy for seven years. The bakers are ready, but the ovens come from another country and arrive slowly, so many cakes sit half-finished waiting for an oven. That is India's defence industry today. The government has ordered many planes and missiles from Indian companies, but some key parts — the engines that push a fighter jet, the "eyes" that guide a missile — still come from abroad. The companies can only deliver, and get paid, as fast as those parts arrive.

Say it to a client in one minute

Indian defence manufacturing has moved from a demand problem to a supply problem.

  • Demand: the 2026–27 budget raised capital outlay 21.8% to ₹2.19 lakh cr, with about 75% of acquisition reserved for domestic industry. Exports hit a record ₹38,424 cr in FY26.
  • Orders: HAL's order book is ₹2.54 lakh cr, 7.7 times FY26 revenue; BDL's is ₹26,176 cr, about 11 times.
  • Execution: HAL's FY26 revenue grew only 7%; about 30 Tejas Mk1A jets wait for GE engines. BDL's FY26 revenue fell as imported seekers arrived late.
  • Structure: private firms are gaining — HAL was left out of the three shortlisted bidders for the AMCA stealth fighter.
  • Stocks: most names peaked in July 2024; the basket is +11% in 2026 but the median member is flat.

The industry has two tiers. At the top are platform makers — HAL for aircraft and helicopters, BDL for missiles — who sign contracts with the Ministry of Defence, integrate everything and deliver the finished product. Below them are suppliers who make parts for the platform makers and for foreign aerospace companies. Suppliers are smaller and riskier, but they also sell to Airbus, Boeing and foreign defence firms, which gives them a second market beyond Indian procurement.

The high-margin annuity

The chain in one breath

  • Metal and composite makers supply materials that take years to qualify.
  • Suppliers turn them into parts.
  • Foreign engine and seeker makers supply the hardest components.
  • HAL and BDL put it all together and deliver it to the forces.
  • The same companies keep the fleet flying for decades, which is often where the best margins are.
Fund-manager lens

The critical link is stage 3. A fighter without an engine cannot be delivered, and a missile without its seeker cannot be accepted; the platform maker books no revenue until it is. That is why HAL's order book grew from ₹1.89 lakh cr to ₹2.54 lakh cr in FY26 while revenue grew 7%, and why BDL's revenue fell in FY26 despite an order book of 11 years. Investors who bought order-book growth in 2023–24 learned that the market eventually pays for deliveries, margins and cash.

The two state-owned platform makers look different from most industrial companies. They receive large advances from the Ministry of Defence, so they carry big cash balances and little debt; their revenue is recognised as aircraft and missiles are delivered and accepted, which concentrates it at the end of the fiscal year. HAL's EBITDA margin was 27.7% in Q1 FY27 and management guides to around 30–31% for the year — high for a manufacturer, because the overhaul and spares businesses are profitable and much of the imported content is passed through.

Suppliers live differently. They must win approval from each customer, often after years of trials, and then receive orders that can swing from quarter to quarter. Their margins can be attractive — CFF's fourth-quarter EBITDA margin was 28.5% — but their revenue is small and lumpy. Dynamatic's aerospace business shows the export alternative: long-term contracts with Airbus and Boeing give visibility, but margins depend on volume and learning curves, and group EBITDA margin is 13%.

How a defence contract happens

  1. Acceptance of necessity (AoN): the Defence Acquisition Council approves the need.
  2. Request for proposal: vendors bid, or a company is nominated.
  3. Trials and evaluation: field trials can take years.
  4. Cost negotiation: the contract negotiation committee agrees a price.
  5. Approval: large contracts go to the Cabinet Committee on Security.
  6. Contract and advance: the order is signed and usually an advance paid.
  7. Delivery and acceptance: revenue is recognised as products are delivered and accepted.

The gap between an AoN headline and revenue can therefore be five to ten years. Markets often react to AoN approvals as though they were orders; they are only the first step.

The Tejas engine story

HAL signed the ₹48,000 cr contract for 83 Tejas Mk1A fighters in February 2021 and a second contract for 97 more in September 2025. Deliveries were meant to begin in 2024. They were held back by GE's F404 engines, whose supply chain was disrupted after the pandemic. By mid-2026 HAL had about 30 airframes built, flown and tested but waiting for engines. GE has committed to 24 engines in FY27 and 30 a year after that, and HAL's chairman has said 10 jets will be handed over by March 2027. At 30 engines a year, the 180 jets on order take about six years to deliver.

The missile seeker story

BDL's Q4 FY26 profit fell 58.5% and EBITDA 81.5% because deliveries of the Astra Mk1 air-to-air missile were delayed by seekers from an external vendor and because a higher share of bought-out components cut margins. Q1 FY27 recovered strongly — revenue +131% — but from a very low base. For a missile maker, the seeker (the sensor that guides the missile to its target) is the equivalent of the fighter's engine: small, expensive and often imported.

Why HAL was left out of AMCA

In 2026 the Ministry of Defence shortlisted three private-led bidders for the prototype phase of the Advanced Medium Combat Aircraft: Tata Advanced Systems; an L&T consortium with BEL and Dynamatic; and a Bharat Forge consortium with BEML and Data Patterns. HAL was not shortlisted, reportedly over concerns about its bandwidth given its existing backlog. That is a structural signal: the government wants a second aircraft-integration capability in India, and future platform programmes will not automatically go to the state-owned incumbent.

The pattern is clear. India now builds most of the structure of its aircraft and missiles, but the highest-value, hardest-to-make components are still imported or made under foreign licence. The government's indigenisation lists and the design-linked programmes aim to change that, but engines in particular take decades to develop — India's own Kaveri engine programme began in the 1980s and has not powered a fighter. Until that changes, foreign suppliers' delivery schedules set the pace of Indian production.

7 · The business calendar

Budgets in February, deliveries in March

  • 1 February: the Union Budget sets the capital outlay for the year. Markets trade the headline increase, which often disappoints high expectations.
  • January–March: the Ministry spends its remaining capital budget and the platform makers rush deliveries. HAL booked ₹13,942 cr of its ₹33,089 cr FY26 revenue in the fourth quarter — 42%.
  • April–May: new-year contracts are signed and fourth-quarter results show the year's biggest numbers.
  • April–June: the first quarter is usually the smallest; HAL's Q1 FY27 revenue was ₹5,515 cr.
  • Throughout the year: Defence Acquisition Council meetings announce approvals (AoNs); large contracts cluster around the fiscal year-end and Aero India (February, odd years).

For the stocks, that calendar means quarterly comparisons are noisy and annual numbers matter more. A weak first quarter for HAL or BDL is normal; a weak fourth quarter is a real miss, as BDL's Q4 FY26 showed.

Control: small caps as a whole (Nifty Smallcap 100) beat the Nifty in April in 11 of 15 years, by an average of 3.0 points, and lag in January–February. Part of any April strength is this market-wide effect. After removing it, this industry's own April edge is about +2.9 points.

Reading it: 2012–2017 rests on only two stocks (Dynamatic and Taneja); HAL and BDL join in 2018 and DCX and CFF later. April–July has beaten the Nifty in 11 of 15 years (median +19.3 points) and April alone in 11 of 15. February–March has worked in only 4 of 15 (median −4.1) and January–March in 6 of 15 (median −4.8). The business calendar explains it: the Union Budget on 1 February rarely matches the market's hopes for defence capital spending, while April and May bring the new year's first contracts and fourth-quarter results in which HAL books over 40% of its annual revenue.

A tendency, not a rule: small samples, survivorship bias (only names still listed) and equal weighting. Use it for timing context, not as a signal.

The exceptions, and why

  • April–July failed in 2012 (−25.4), 2018 (−24.9), 2013 (−14.9) and 2019 (−12.7) — the policy-paralysis years and the 2018–19 small-cap bust. Its best runs were 2023 (+53.1) and 2014 (+50.3).
  • February–March worked only in 2014 (+33.2), 2015 (+30.1), 2019 and 2023; it failed in each of 2024, 2025 and 2026 despite record budgets.
  • October–December has been weak lately, failing in 2019 (−10.1), 2020 (−13.8) and 2025 (−10.3).
How to use this

The April–July strength is the most consistent pattern in the Defence family so far, and it has a business reason: fourth-quarter results and new contracts arrive in April and May. The February–March weakness also has one — budgets priced in beforehand. But 2012–2017 rests on two small stocks, and the 2021–2024 re-rating lifted every window. Treat it as a tendency, strongest when valuations are not already stretched.

9 · What moves margins

Eight drivers

  1. Capital budget. Capital outlay and the share reserved for domestic industry set demand.
  2. Contract signing. AoN approvals become revenue only after contracts are signed.
  3. Imported components. Engines and seekers set the delivery pace.
  4. Indigenisation. More local content means more value added, and usually more margin, for Indian firms.
  5. Mix of manufacture and service. Overhaul and spares earn more than new production.
  6. Exports. Foreign orders add volume and often better pricing; exports rose to ₹38,424 cr in FY26.
  7. Global aerospace cycle. Airbus and Boeing production rates drive suppliers such as Dynamatic.
  8. Valuation. Defence stocks trade on long-dated expectations; the multiple has moved them more than earnings since 2021.

The anatomy of the cycles

  1. Import dependence (2012–2013): procurement stalled; the few listed suppliers fell.
  2. Make in India, first wave (2014–2017): FDI raised to 49%, new procurement categories; a burst in 2014, then little.
  3. PSU malaise (2018–2019): HAL and BDL listed into a small-cap crash and traded as low-growth government companies.
  4. The re-rating (2020–2024): Galwan, indigenisation lists, 74% FDI, the Tejas order and the Ukraine war. The basket rose 117% in 2021, 56% in 2022, 130% in 2023 and 39% in 2024.
  5. Execution reality (2025–2026): most stocks peaked in July 2024; Operation Sindoor produced a brief spike in May 2025; engine and seeker delays and high valuations have held the group back since.

Patterns that repeat

  • Events start rallies; deliveries sustain them. Galwan, Ukraine and Operation Sindoor each lifted the stocks. Only the 2021–2024 run, backed by real contract signings, lasted.
  • The same month marks the top. HAL, DCX, Nibe and Taneja all made their highs in July 2024.
  • PSU platform makers outlast small suppliers. HAL is 15% below its high; DCX is 63% and Taneja 59% below.
  • Pivots and renamed companies ride the theme hardest, which is why Jaykay and Nibe are kept out of the basket.

World military spending reached about $2.7 trillion in 2024, up 9.4% — the steepest rise since the end of the Cold War, according to SIPRI. India has long been one of the world's largest arms importers, but its policy since 2020 has been to reserve most procurement for domestic industry. Exports, from ₹686 cr in FY14 to ₹38,424 cr in FY26, show the shift: DPSUs accounted for 54.8% and private firms for 45.2% of FY26 exports, which went to more than 80 countries.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Fifth-generation fighters. AMCA brings stealth, internal weapons bays and sensor fusion, and a private-led production partner.
  2. Indigenous engines. Co-production of the GE F414 for Tejas Mk2 and a partnership with Safran for the AMCA engine aim to close the biggest gap.
  3. Drones and loitering munitions. Cheaper unmanned systems are changing what armies buy (see 061).
  4. Precision missiles at scale. Recent conflicts showed that stockpiles run out fast; volume production matters.
  5. Composites and additive manufacturing. Lighter structures and 3D-printed parts shorten supply chains.
  6. Digital engineering. Model-based design and digital twins reduce development time.

Each shift favours companies that can design, not just build to print. HAL's exclusion from AMCA, and the L&T and Bharat Forge consortia that include listed suppliers such as Dynamatic and Data Patterns, show how design capability is becoming the gateway to the next generation of programmes.

What is priced in

HAL trades at about 34 times FY26 profit — far below its 2024 peak multiple but still a premium to most PSUs, justified by a margin near 30% and a seven-year order book. The market is waiting for evidence that deliveries accelerate: the first Tejas Mk1A handovers and a revenue growth rate that finally reflects the order book. BDL's 41% fall from its May 2025 high already prices in execution trouble; its Q1 FY27 rebound is the first sign of recovery. The smaller suppliers are priced for growth — CFF at about 55 times and Dynamatic at about 98 times annualised Q1 profit — so any delay in shipyard or aircraft schedules will hurt them more.

The honest read

The investment case for Indian defence has moved from "will the government spend?" — answered decisively yes — to "can the industry deliver?" The answer depends on foreign engines and seekers in the near term and on Indian design capability in the long term. Companies that deliver on time and turn orders into cash will be rewarded; those that only add to their order books will not, as 2025–26 showed.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. How many aircraft or missiles will be delivered this year, and what limits the number?
  2. How many engines or seekers are in hand, and what is the supplier's committed schedule?
  3. How much of the order book is under signed contract, and how much is AoN or expected?
  4. What is the split of revenue between new production, overhaul and spares?
  5. What share of each product is now made in India, and what does it add to margin?
  6. How large are customer advances, and how is working capital changing?
  7. What export orders are in hand, and in which countries?
  8. What is the plan to add capacity, and who are the key suppliers?
  9. For suppliers: what share of revenue comes from the largest customer, and how are payment terms?
  10. What would a delay in the budget or a programme mean for this year's guidance?

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. What is an AoN, and why is it not an order?

Acceptance of necessity — the DAC's approval of a requirement. Trials, negotiation and approval must follow before a contract is signed.

2. How much did the 2026–27 budget allocate to capital outlay?

₹2.19 lakh cr, up 21.8%, with about 75% of acquisition reserved for domestic industry.

3. How large was HAL's order book at March 2026?

₹2.54 lakh cr, up from ₹1.89 lakh cr — about 7.7 years of revenue.

4. Why are Tejas Mk1A deliveries late?

GE F404 engines arrived slowly; about 30 airframes wait for engines.

5. Why did BDL's Q4 FY26 profit fall?

Delayed seekers from an external vendor held back Astra Mk1 deliveries, and a higher share of bought-out components cut margins.

6. Who was shortlisted for AMCA?

Tata Advanced Systems; an L&T consortium with BEL and Dynamatic; and a Bharat Forge consortium with BEML and Data Patterns. HAL was not.

7. What were India's defence exports in FY26?

A record ₹38,424 cr, up 62.7%, against ₹686 cr in FY14.

8. Which window has been the most reliable for the basket?

April–July, which beat the Nifty in 11 of 15 years with a median of +19.3 points.

9. Why are Jaykay and Nibe excluded from the basket?

Both pivoted into defence from other businesses; Jaykay was not a defence company before 2021 and Nibe's +683% in 2022 from a tiny base would dominate an equal-weighted basket.

10. What share of HAL's FY26 revenue came in the fourth quarter?

42% — ₹13,942 cr of ₹33,089 cr.
