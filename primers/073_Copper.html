Industry primer · 73 of 278 · Metals family, part 10

Copper

Copper is the metal that electricity actually travels through. A car needs about twenty kilograms of it; an electric car needs three or four times that. A wind turbine needs tonnes. A data centre needs miles of it. There is no substitute at scale and no new giant mine arriving soon, which is why copper reached an all-time high of $14,875 a tonne in September 2026. India, meanwhile, spent the last eight years as a net importer of a metal it used to export, because one court order closed a third of its refining capacity overnight. This primer explains the chain, the tariff distortion currently warping the world price, and why the Indian listed universe is so oddly thin.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

A structural shortage, distorted by a tariff

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Electricity is like water, and copper is the best pipe ever found for carrying it. Every wire in your house, every charger, every motor has copper inside. Now the whole world wants to run on electricity instead of petrol, so everyone needs more pipes at once — but copper comes from a few enormous holes in the ground, mostly in Chile and Peru, and digging a new one takes about fifteen years. Too many buyers, too few holes: the price goes up.

Say it to a client in one minute

The clearest structural story in metals, currently obscured by a policy distortion.

  • The price: an all-time London high of $14,875 a tonne on 10 September 2026, roughly triple the 2016 low.
  • The distortion: US Section 232 tariff uncertainty has pulled metal into America. COMEX stocks hit a record 675,185 tonnes in August; London stocks fell to 214,550 tonnes with 58% already on cancelled warrants.
  • The smelter squeeze: treatment and refining charges are negative — there is more smelting capacity than concentrate, so refiners pay miners.
  • The Indian position: Hindustan Copper's Q1 FY27 revenue rose 81% and profit 163%, with a 54% EBITDA margin, because it mines rather than smelts.
  • The rebuild: Adani's Kutch Copper at Mundra is ramping from 0.5 to 1 million tonnes by FY29, replacing the capacity lost when Sterlite's Tuticorin smelter closed in 2018.

The by-product line is more important than it looks. A copper smelter earns money on the gold and silver that come out of the concentrate and on the sulphuric acid made from the sulphur dioxide it must capture anyway. When acid prices are above $400 a tonne, as they are now, that credit is the difference between a profitable smelter and a loss-making one.

The chain in one breath

  • A mine digs rock containing about 1% copper.
  • Flotation concentrates it to a quarter or a third.
  • A smelter burns off the sulphur and makes acid from it.
  • A refinery electroplates the copper pure and collects the gold.
  • Fabricators draw it into the wire that electrification runs on.
Fund-manager lens

Copper's defining feature is that the scarcity sits at stage one and the capacity glut sits at stage three. There is too much smelting in the world — much of it built in China for strategic rather than commercial reasons — and not enough concentrate to feed it. That imbalance is why treatment charges, the fee a smelter earns for processing, have gone negative. The investable position in copper is therefore upstream: mines and, at a stretch, integrated producers with their own concentrate. A standalone smelter in this market is a toll booth on a road nobody is driving down.

The audit: where India's copper industry actually is

This section has six names and none of them refines copper at scale. That is not a classification error — it is the structure of the industry, and it is worth stating plainly because a screen on this watchlist section will badly mislead.

  • Hindalco (in 072) runs India's largest copper smelter and refinery at Dahej, at roughly 500,000 tonnes a year. Its copper division reported a record quarter alongside aluminium.
  • Adani Enterprises owns Kutch Copper at Mundra, a $1.2 billion plant that began operations in March 2024 at 0.5 million tonnes a year and is designed to reach 1 million tonnes by FY29 — which would make it the world's largest single-location custom smelter.
  • Vedanta owned Sterlite Copper at Tuticorin, about 400,000 tonnes a year, closed by order since May 2018 after protests and a court process. Its closure turned India from a refined-copper exporter into a net importer, and it has never reopened. Following Vedanta's 2026 demerger, the base metals business stayed with Vedanta Ltd rather than moving to one of the new entities.
  • Hindustan Copper produces around 60,000 tonnes of cathode equivalent and is primarily a miner.

So the listed "copper" section captures a state miner and four converters, while the country's refining capacity sits inside three diversified groups classified elsewhere. Any copper thesis in India has to be expressed through Hindustan Copper for mining leverage, Hindalco for refining, or Adani Enterprises for the new capacity — and each of those carries a great deal that has nothing to do with copper.

Treatment charges, and why negative is extraordinary

When a miner sells concentrate to a smelter, the smelter does not buy it outright. It pays the miner the value of the contained metal less a treatment charge (per tonne of concentrate) and a refining charge (per pound of metal). Those charges are the smelter's revenue. In a balanced market they might be $80 a tonne and 8 cents a pound. They are set annually in benchmark negotiations and traded in a spot market in between.

In 2026 spot treatment charges are negative. The smelter pays the miner a premium above the metal value just to obtain concentrate. This happens when smelting capacity — much of it built in China — grows faster than mine supply, and it means the only thing keeping a smelter running is its by-product stream: gold and silver from the anode slime and sulphuric acid, which is above $400 a tonne. For an investor the message is blunt: in this phase of the cycle, owning tonnes of ore in the ground beats owning the plant that processes them.

Why no new mines

A greenfield copper mine takes ten to fifteen years from discovery to first metal, through exploration, permitting, community consent and construction, and the world's average ore grade has been falling for decades, so each tonne of metal requires more rock, more water and more energy than the last. That is why the supply response to a record price is measured in years, not months — and why copper is the metal where a demand story can actually persist long enough to matter.

5b · The tariff distortion

Two prices for one metal

Anyone reading a copper price in 2026 needs to know which copper price they are reading, because the world briefly has two. The United States has been running a Section 232 national-security investigation into copper imports, and the possibility of tariffs on refined cathode set off one of the largest physical arbitrages in the metal's history.

  1. The spread blew out. The gap between the COMEX price in New York and the London Metal Exchange price went from a historical norm of roughly $50 a tonne to more than $800.
  2. Metal moved. Traders shipped cathode to the United States to capture the difference before any tariff landed. COMEX stocks reached a record 675,185 tonnes on 25 August 2026.
  3. London emptied. LME stocks fell to 214,550 tonnes, down 14% from the end of July, with 58% already on cancelled warrants — that is, committed for delivery and not really available.
  4. The visible price hit a record. Copper touched an all-time LME high of $14,875 a tonne on 10 September 2026, and a large part of that was a warehouse squeeze rather than end demand.
Read the number carefully

The copper column in the cycle table below is the COMEX front-month settlement in dollars per pound, which is the series with the longest continuous history on our data source. For every year up to 2024 it tracks the London price closely. For 2025 and 2026 it does not: COMEX carries the tariff premium, so the $6.83 shown for 2026 overstates the London price. We are flagging this rather than silently mixing two series, and the same caution applies to any copper chart you are shown this year — ask which exchange it is.

The investment consequence is that today's price contains two components with very different half-lives. The structural component — electrification demand against a decade of underinvestment in mines — is durable. The arbitrage component is binary and reversible: if the tariff arrives as proposed, the positioning was correct; if it is delayed or narrowed, the front-loading unwinds and metal flows back. There is also a serious counter-argument doing the rounds that the market faces a surplus of several hundred thousand tonnes once the distortion clears. Both can be true: a tight physical market and an inventory overhang sitting in the wrong warehouses.

That last row deserves emphasis. Sterlite's Tuticorin smelter was not closed by economics; it was closed by protest, pollution findings and a court process, and it has stayed closed for more than eight years despite the metal being in short supply and India importing what the plant used to make. For a copper investor in India, social licence is not a soft consideration — it is the single largest identifiable risk to any asset, and it should be diligenced as seriously as the ore body.

7 · The business calendar

The Chinese buying year

  • January–March: Chinese New Year halts construction and cable-making; the worst window anywhere in this project at 3 wins in 14 years.
  • March: the single worst month, winning twice in fourteen years with an average of −8.1 points.
  • April–July: the Chinese and Indian construction seasons and annual contracting; 10 wins in 14 years.
  • August–September: mixed, and the month that annual smelter benchmark talks begin.
  • October–December: restocking before the year end and benchmark treatment-charge settlement; 12 wins in 14 years, median +12.3 points.

  • Windows: Feb–Mar beat the Nifty in 3 of 14 years (median -10.6 pts); Apr–Jul beat the Nifty in 10 of 14 years (median +5.8 pts); Aug–Sep beat the Nifty in 7 of 14 years (median +1.3 pts); Oct–Dec beat the Nifty in 12 of 14 years (median +12.3 pts); Sep–Nov beat the Nifty in 12 of 14 years (median +5.4 pts); Jan–Mar beat the Nifty in 3 of 14 years (median -8.2 pts).

Control: small caps as a whole (Nifty Smallcap 100) beat the Nifty in April in 11 of 15 years, by an average of 3.0 points, and lag in January–February. Part of any April strength is this market-wide effect. After removing it, this industry's own April edge is about +6.5 points.

Reading it: four companies — Hindustan Copper, Cubex Tubings, Palco Metals (from 2016) and Bhagyanagar India (from 2018). This is the sharpest calendar in the whole project. October–December has beaten the Nifty in 12 of 14 years with a median of +12.3 points, and September–November also in 12 of 14. February–March has worked in 3 of 14 (median −10.6), January–March in 3 of 14, and March alone in only 2 of 14 (average −8.1). April has worked in 10 of 14 (+9.5). Copper is the most China-sensitive metal in this family, and the Chinese buying calendar — dead over the New Year, restocking from October — runs straight through these prices.

A tendency, not a rule: small samples, survivorship bias (only names still listed) and equal weighting. Use it for timing context, not as a signal.

The exceptions, and why

  • October–December failed only twice: 2012 (−29.1), when a disinvestment overhang hung over Hindustan Copper, and 2014 (−5.4), as the metal slid toward its low.
  • February–March's only wins were 2021 (+10.7) and 2024 (+6.2), both reflation episodes strong enough to override the seasonal pattern.
  • December has beaten the Nifty in 13 of 15 years, averaging +7.6 points — the most consistent single month found so far.
How to use this — and a warning

These are the strongest seasonal numbers in the project, and they come with the weakest basket: four companies, three of them micro-caps whose individual years include +212% and −33%. Some of what looks like seasonality is small-cap beta interacting with a metal that happens to be seasonal. The honest way to use it is as confirmation, not as a signal on its own — and to note that the same October–December pattern appears independently in aluminium (072), where the basket is far more liquid. When two different baskets with one common customer show the same calendar, the mechanism is probably real.

9 · What moves margins

Eight drivers

  1. The LME copper price. Everything upstream keys off it.
  2. Treatment and refining charges. The smelter's entire revenue line.
  3. Mine supply disruptions. Chile and Peru — strikes, water, grades, politics.
  4. Chinese construction and grid spending. Roughly half of world demand.
  5. Electrification demand. EVs, renewables, grids and data centres.
  6. US trade policy. The Section 232 process and the COMEX–LME spread.
  7. By-product prices. Sulphuric acid, gold and silver credits.
  8. Scrap availability. The swing supply that responds fastest to price.

The anatomy of the cycles

  1. The long slide (2012–2016): copper fell from $3.65 to $2.13 a pound as Chinese construction slowed; the basket lost to the index in four of five years.
  2. The false dawn and the closure (2017–2019): a strong 2017, then the trade war and the shutting of Tuticorin, which cost the basket 42% in 2018 alone.
  3. The electrification re-rating (2020–2024): copper became the transition metal; the basket beat the index every single year.
  4. The squeeze (2025–2026): tariff front-running, emptying London warehouses and a record price, with the mining name capturing most of the value.

Patterns that repeat

  • Since 2020 the basket has beaten the Nifty every year — the longest winning streak in the Metals family.
  • The metal price column explains direction, not magnitude; the micro-caps amplify everything.
  • Regulatory events have cost more than price cycles — 2018 is the largest single drawdown in the series.

The structural asymmetry is worth stating once more: mining is concentrated in a few countries with long permitting cycles and falling grades, while smelting is concentrated in one country that has built more of it than the world has ore to feed. Copper's price therefore spends long periods explaining the scarcity of rock rather than the scarcity of metal — and the profits accrue to whoever owns the rock.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Electrification demand. An electric vehicle uses roughly three to four times the copper of a petrol car, and grids and data centres need far more again.
  2. Aluminium substitution. Where weight and cost matter more than conductivity — power cables, some motors — aluminium takes share; see 072 and 044.
  3. Leaching of sulphide ores. New chemistry that could recover copper from waste rock without smelting, a genuine supply-side wildcard.
  4. Deeper and lower-grade mining. Block caving and automation to reach ore that was uneconomic.
  5. Recycling intensity. Scrap responds within months to price, unlike mines.
  6. Water-free processing. Desalination and dry stacking, driven by Chilean water scarcity.

What is priced in

Look at the first row. Copper is at an all-time high, Hindustan Copper's profit has risen 163% and its EBITDA margin is 54% — and the stock is down 4% for the year and 35% below its January peak. The market ran ahead of the fundamentals in January and has spent eight months waiting for them to arrive; now that they have, it is discounting the possibility that they do not last. That is a reasonable thing to discount when a third of the metal price may be a tariff artefact.

The structural case remains the best in metals: electrification demand that is not discretionary, meeting a supply base that cannot respond for a decade. But two things have to be separated before acting. The price is inflated by an arbitrage that could unwind within months. The volume story at Hindustan Copper — 4.21 to 12.2 million tonnes of ore by FY30 — is a ten-year execution question at a company with a long history of missing production targets, and ₹7,000 cr of capex is a large commitment against a ₹46,586 cr market value.

The honest read

Copper is the one metal where the long-term thesis genuinely does not depend on a cycle turning. But the listed Indian expression of it is poor: a single state-owned miner trading on an execution promise, plus four converters too small to matter. If the thesis is copper, the cleanest instruments are the global miners or an integrated Indian producer classified elsewhere. If the position is Hindustan Copper, be explicit that you are underwriting a state enterprise's mine-expansion plan, not the metal price.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What was ore production and metal-in-concentrate this quarter, against plan?
  2. What is the cash cost per tonne of copper produced?
  3. What are reserves and resources, and how have grades moved?
  4. For smelters: what treatment and refining charges were realised, and what is the by-product credit?
  5. What is the sulphuric acid realisation, and is offtake secured?
  6. How much of the capex plan is committed, and what return is expected?
  7. What is the status of environmental clearances and community agreements at each site?
  8. How is metal priced to customers — LME-linked, with what lag?
  9. For converters: what is the conversion charge, and how has working capital moved at record prices?
  10. What is the plan for overseas asset acquisition, and how would it be funded?

19 · Glossary

Every term you'll meet

The chain

Copper ore
Rock containing typically 0.5–2% copper.
Grade
The percentage of metal in the ore; falling worldwide.
Flotation
Separating copper minerals from rock using bubbles and reagents.
Concentrate
The flotation product, about 25–30% copper — what is traded to smelters.
Metal in concentrate
The contained copper tonnage a miner reports.
Smelting
Melting concentrate to drive off sulphur and iron, producing blister copper.
Blister copper
Roughly 98% pure copper from the smelter, before refining.
Electro-refining
Dissolving and replating copper to reach 99.99% purity.
Cathode
The refined sheet of pure copper — the exchange-traded form.
Anode slime
The residue left after refining, containing gold and silver.
Continuous cast rod
Cathode melted and cast into rod for wire drawing.
Leaching / SX-EW
Dissolving copper chemically and recovering it electrically, without smelting.
Block caving
A bulk underground mining method for deep, low-grade ore.
Tailings
Waste left after concentration; a major environmental liability.

Market terms

Treatment charge (TC)
What a smelter earns per tonne of concentrate processed.
Refining charge (RC)
What a refiner earns per pound of metal produced.
Negative TC/RC
When smelters pay miners a premium for concentrate — the state of the market in 2026.
Benchmark settlement
The annual TC/RC negotiated between major miners and smelters.
By-product credit
Revenue from gold, silver and acid that offsets smelting cost.
LME
The London Metal Exchange — the global benchmark price.
COMEX
The US exchange; in 2026 its price carries a tariff premium.
Cancelled warrant
Warehouse stock already committed for withdrawal, so not truly available.
Backwardation
Spot priced above future delivery — the signature of physical tightness.
Section 232
The US law under which imports can be restricted on national-security grounds.
Arbitrage
Profiting from the same metal having two prices in two places.
Cash cost
The operating cost of producing a tonne of metal, before capital charges.
C1 cost
The industry's standard cash-cost measure, net of by-product credits.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. Why can't copper supply respond to a high price?

A new mine takes ten to fifteen years from discovery to first metal, and average ore grades keep falling.

2. What does a smelter actually earn?

Treatment and refining charges, plus by-product credits — not the metal price.

3. What does a negative treatment charge mean?

There is more smelting capacity than concentrate, so smelters pay miners a premium to obtain feed.

4. Why are there two copper prices in 2026?

US Section 232 tariff uncertainty pushed the COMEX price above London by more than $800 a tonne, against a norm of about $50.

5. What does a cancelled warrant tell you?

That visible warehouse stock is already committed for delivery — 58% of remaining LME stock is in this state.

6. What happened to India's refining capacity in 2018?

Sterlite's Tuticorin smelter, about 400,000 tonnes a year, was closed by order and has not reopened; India became a net importer.

7. Who is rebuilding it?

Adani's Kutch Copper at Mundra, which began operations in 2024 at 0.5 million tonnes and is designed for 1 million by FY29.

8. Why did Hindustan Copper earn a 54% EBITDA margin?

It is a miner, not a smelter — it captures the metal price directly and is unaffected by negative treatment charges.

9. What is the strongest calendar pattern?

October–December: 12 wins in 14 years with a median of +12.3 points, driven by Chinese restocking and benchmark settlement.

10. Why is the listed Indian copper universe misleading?

It contains one state miner and four small converters; the actual refining capacity sits inside Hindalco, Adani and Vedanta, classified elsewhere.
