Power T&D EPC

The builders of the grid. Transmission and distribution (T&D) EPC contractors fabricate and erect steel towers, string conductors across rivers and deserts, build substations, lay underground cables in cities and rebuild village distribution networks — in India and in the Gulf, Africa, Latin America and Central Asia. Their order books are the largest they have ever been; their margins are not.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Contractors to the grid, paid for execution rather than technology

Explain it to a 10-year-old

You have seen the giant steel towers marching across fields, holding up thick wires. Someone has to make those towers, carry the pieces to the middle of nowhere, bolt them together, and then pull kilometres of heavy wire from one tower to the next without letting it touch the ground. Then they build the fenced yards full of equipment — substations — where the electricity changes voltage. T&D EPC companies do all of this. They get paid well when the work goes smoothly, but rain, blocked roads, a farmer who won't let them cross his field, or a war that makes ships take the long way round can eat up their profit.

Say it to a client in one minute

Demand for grid construction is at a record; the sector's problem is converting it into profit.

  • Orders are at records: KEC ₹37,697 cr, Transrail ₹16,035 cr, Techno Electric ₹11,000 cr, Vikran ₹6,496 cr, Bajel ₹4,055 cr.
  • Margins are under pressure: KEC's EBITDA margin fell to 5.8% and profit 42% as Gulf shipping disruption, freight and labour shortages hit projects; Transrail held 11.7%.
  • Domestic ordering paused early in 2026: slower TBCB awards cut inflows across the grid chain (GE Vernova T&D's orders fell 30%; see 043), even as private transmission companies' under-construction portfolio passed ₹1.12 lakh cr.
  • Diversification helps: Techno Electric's data-centre business and 13–14% margin guidance have kept it close to flat this year.
  • The stocks have de-rated hard: KEC is 67% below its December 2024 peak, Jyoti 74%, Transrail 49%, Vikran 51%.

Analogy

If the transmission company is the owner of a toll highway (see 039), the T&D EPC contractor is the road-building company. The owner earns a regulated return for 35 years; the builder earns a margin once, during construction, and carries the risk of rain, land disputes and cost overruns while it does.

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • The plan: the government decides where new power lines are needed.
  • Hiring the builder: the line owner chooses a construction company by tender.
  • The kit: steel, wires, glass or rubber insulators and equipment are bought.
  • Making towers: steel is cut, drilled and dipped in zinc so it will not rust for decades.
  • Building: teams dig foundations, bolt up the towers and pull the wires across.
  • Switching on: the line is tested, powered up and handed over.
Fund-manager lens

The critical difference between a transmission owner and a transmission contractor is who carries the risk. Owners earn a regulated or bid tariff for decades; contractors earn a one-time margin and bear execution risk, commodity risk and, increasingly, geopolitical risk on overseas projects. That is why T&D EPC stocks re-rated so sharply in 2023–24 when ordering surged and margins recovered, and de-rated as hard in 2025–26 when margins slipped. A useful discipline is to value these companies on through-cycle margins of 7–10% (KEC's historical range) rather than on peak order books, and to pay extra only for durable advantages: in-house tower capacity, a strong balance sheet, and a record of finishing projects on time.

What pushes margins down

  1. Legacy fixed-price contracts signed before commodity or freight costs jumped.
  2. Right-of-way and forest delays that stretch overheads and trigger claims (see 039).
  3. International disruption — shipping, insurance, labour and currency; the 2026 Gulf conflict is a live example.
  4. Working capital: retention money and slow payments, especially from discoms.
  5. Aggressive bidding when order books are thin.
Analogy

A transmission contractor is like a removals company hired to move a house across the country for a fixed quote. The quote was set months ago. If fuel prices jump, a bridge closes or the crew falls ill, the customer still pays the quote. Good removal companies build in contingencies and choose routes they know; bad ones win on price and lose on delivery.

7 · The business calendar

Execution follows the weather

  • April–June: the first quarter is slow to start as new projects mobilise; heat limits working hours in the north.
  • July–September: the monsoon halts foundations and stringing across much of India; the weakest execution season.
  • October–March: the main construction season; January–March is the heaviest billing quarter.
  • Gulf projects: summer heat restricts outdoor working hours from June to September.
  • Order calendar: TBCB awards and Power Grid packages arrive unevenly; a slow spell in early 2026 cut inflows across the grid chain.

  • Strongest months vs the Nifty: Apr +6.1 pts (beat in 11 of 15 years); Sep +4.4 pts (beat in 11 of 15 years); May +2.8 pts (beat in 6 of 15 years).
  • Weakest months: Aug -5.3 pts (beat in 3 of 16 years); Feb -3.4 pts (beat in 4 of 15 years); Mar -2.0 pts (beat in 4 of 15 years).
  • Windows: Feb–Mar beat the Nifty in 3 of 15 years (median -3.9 pts); Apr–Jul beat the Nifty in 11 of 15 years (median +13.1 pts); Aug–Sep beat the Nifty in 7 of 14 years (median -3.0 pts); Oct–Dec beat the Nifty in 7 of 14 years (median -0.1 pts); Sep–Nov beat the Nifty in 9 of 14 years (median +5.2 pts); Jan–Mar beat the Nifty in 6 of 15 years (median -6.6 pts).

Control: small caps as a whole (Nifty Smallcap 100) beat the Nifty in April in 11 of 15 years, by an average of 3.0 points, and lag in January–February. Part of any April strength is this market-wide effect. After removing it, this industry's own April edge is about +3.1 points.

Reading it: a clear pattern for a construction business. August is the worst month by far — the basket has beaten the Nifty in only 3 of 16 Augusts (average −5.3 points), and August–September in 7 of 14 (median −3.0). April is strong (11 of 15, +6.1 points) and April–July works 11 of 15 times with a median of +13.1. February–March is weak (3 of 15, median −3.9). The August weakness has two causes: the monsoon stops tower erection and stringing, and the April–June results, published in August, are always the smallest quarter of the year for line contractors.

A tendency, not a rule: small samples, survivorship bias (only names still listed) and equal weighting. Use it for timing context, not as a signal.

The exceptions, and why

  • April–July failed in 2018 (−54.9) and 2013 (−46.2) — Jyoti Structures' collapse into insolvency and the 2013 infrastructure slump — and marginally in 2012 (−6.0) and 2020 (−0.1). The best runs were 2024 (+57.0) and 2023 (+49.3).
  • August beat the Nifty only in 2018, 2020 and 2023; the worst were 2015 (−23.7) and 2014 (−17.5).
  • January–March succeeded in 6 of 15 years, but failed badly in 2025 (−29.2), 2015 (−27.7) and 2016 (−23.6).
  • September–November works 9 of 14 times (median +5.2), as the monsoon ends and execution resumes.
How to use this

The August weakness and the September–November recovery match the physical construction calendar and the timing of the smallest quarterly results, which makes the pattern more believable than most. The sample before 2019 is only two stocks, so give the recent years more weight: August has still been poor in five of the last seven years.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Eight drivers

  1. Transmission awards: TBCB and Power Grid packages under the national plan (see 039).
  2. Renewable evacuation: lines and pooling substations for solar and wind zones.
  3. Distribution reform: RDSS works on feeders, transformers and meters.
  4. Overseas utility spending: the Gulf, Africa, Latin America, Central Asia and the US (via KEC's SAE Towers).
  5. Commodity prices: steel, zinc and aluminium on fixed-price work.
  6. Geopolitics and logistics: 2026's Gulf shipping disruption is the current example.
  7. Data centres: substation and power-infrastructure work (Techno).
  8. Balance sheets: working capital and guarantee capacity limit growth.

The anatomy of the cycles

  1. The long drought (2012–2016): slowing transmission awards, stressed balance sheets and Jyoti's decline.
  2. The 2017 spike and 2018 bust: KEC +173% on diversification and international wins; then Jyoti's insolvency and the small-cap crash.
  3. Revival (2019–2022): Jyoti's resolution, steady KEC, and commodity squeezes on fixed-price work in 2022.
  4. The grid re-rating (2023–2024): transmission ordering for renewables, new listings, the basket +72% and +131%.
  5. Margins disappoint (2025–2026): record order books, slower awards early in 2026, Gulf disruption, and deep de-ratings.

Patterns that repeat

  • The stocks follow margins, not orders: KEC's order book is a record while its shares are 67% below peak.
  • Fixed-price contracts turn commodity or logistics shocks into a year of poor margins — 2022 and 2026.
  • Balance-sheet weakness is fatal in this business: Jyoti's 86% fall in 2018.
  • Listings arrive at the top of the cycle: Bajel, Transrail and Vikran all listed in 2023–25 and all trade well below their peaks.

11 · The global picture

Indian contractors build grids on five continents

Indian T&D contractors are among the most competitive in the world. KEC, Kalpataru and Transrail build lines across the Middle East, Africa, Central Asia and Latin America, competing with Chinese state-owned contractors, Turkish firms and European specialists. They win on cost, tower-design capability and a willingness to work in difficult terrain. The risks are equally international: currency, payment delays, security and logistics. The Gulf — Saudi Arabia above all — is now the biggest overseas market, which gave KEC its largest composite order in 2026 and also its largest margin problem, as the regional conflict disrupted shipping and raised costs.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • From public-sector to private transmission owners in India under TBCB, broadening the EPC customer base.
  • From India to international in the 2010s, as domestic ordering slowed; and back toward India in 2023–24.
  • From pure transmission to diversified EPC (civil, railways, data centres, solar) to smooth the cycle.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. 765 kV and HVDC: higher voltages and longer lines for renewable zones (see 039, 043).
  2. Reconductoring with HTLS: more capacity on existing towers, avoiding right-of-way battles (see 044).
  3. Monopoles and compact towers: smaller footprints in cities.
  4. Gas-insulated and digital substations: more engineering content for EPC specialists.
  5. Drones and mechanised stringing: faster surveys and safer work over rivers and forests.
  6. Underground cabling in cities: replacing overhead lines where land is scarce.

The honest read

This is the part of the grid chain with the least pricing power. Transformer and switchgear makers (042, 043) enjoy a global shortage; transmission owners (039) earn regulated returns; the contractors in between carry the execution risk. After de-ratings of 50–75%, the question is whether margins are cyclically or structurally lower. KEC's problem looks largely cyclical — legacy contracts and a Gulf disruption that should ease — and its ₹37,697 cr order book is real work; but it needs two or three clean quarters to prove it. Transrail's 11.7% margin and Techno's diversified model suggest that disciplined bidding and in-house manufacturing still earn a fair return. The smaller contractors are the most exposed if domestic awards stay slow.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What share of the order book is legacy fixed-price work, and when will it be completed?
  2. What is the margin on orders won in the last year compared with the book as a whole?
  3. How much of the order book is overseas, by region, and how exposed is it to the Gulf disruption?
  4. What share of the order book has right of way and land secured?
  5. How much tower and conductor capacity is in-house?
  6. What are working-capital days, and which customers are paying slowly?
  7. What claims and variations are outstanding, and how are they accounted for?
  8. What is the bid pipeline in India and abroad, and how competitive is pricing?

Red flags

  • Revenue growth without margin recovery over several quarters.
  • Rising receivables and unbilled revenue.
  • Large claims recognised as revenue before settlement.
  • Winning orders at margins well below the historical range.

Every term you'll meet

Towers and lines

Lattice tower
The familiar steel framework tower, bolted together from angle sections.
Suspension tower
A tower on a straight run that simply holds the conductor up.
Tension (angle) tower
A stronger tower where the line turns or ends, taking the pull of the conductors.
Monopole
A single tubular pole used where space is limited.
Tower fabrication
Cutting, punching and marking steel members for assembly.
Hot-dip galvanising
Coating steel with zinc to prevent rust for decades.
Tower testing station
A facility where a full-size prototype tower is loaded to failure to prove its design.
Pile and open foundations
Deep piles for soft ground or rivers; excavated foundations elsewhere.
Earthwire
The top wire on a line that shields conductors from lightning; often an OPGW (see 044).
Insulator string
The chain of insulators that holds a conductor away from the tower.
Composite (polymer) insulator
A lighter, pollution-resistant alternative to porcelain or glass.
Sag
How far a conductor droops between towers, which rises with temperature.
Power-line crossing
Stringing over an energised line, which needs a shutdown or special methods.
Shutdown
A planned outage on an existing line to allow work nearby.
Forest clearance
Environmental approval needed to route a line through forest.
Crop and tree compensation
Payments to landowners for damage during construction.
Reconductoring
Replacing conductors on existing towers, often with HTLS, to add capacity.
Switchyard
The outdoor area of a substation holding breakers, isolators and busbars.
Substation civil works
Foundations, buildings, cable trenches and roads inside a substation.

Distribution and adjacent work

RDSS
The Revamped Distribution Sector Scheme funding discom infrastructure and smart meters.
Feeder separation
Separate lines for agricultural and household supply, to control farm power hours.
Distribution transformer
The small transformer that steps voltage down for local use (see 042).
Underground distribution cabling
Replacing overhead distribution lines in cities with buried cable.
Railway electrification (OHE)
Overhead equipment that supplies power to electric trains.
Traction substation
A substation feeding a railway line.

Commercial

Regulated tariff mechanism (RTM)
Awarding transmission projects directly, usually to Power Grid, on cost-plus terms.
Transmission service provider (TSP)
The company that owns and operates a transmission project.
Bid process coordinator (BPC)
The agency that runs a TBCB auction.
Legacy contract
An older order priced before current cost conditions.
Variation order
A change to the scope or price of a contract during execution.
Claim
A contractor's demand for extra payment for delays or changes caused by others.
Force majeure
A contract clause excusing delay caused by extraordinary events, such as war.
Execution rate
Annual revenue as a share of the opening order book.
Defect-liability period
The period after handover during which the contractor must fix faults.
Freight surcharge and war-risk insurance
Extra shipping costs levied during conflicts.
Development-bank funding
Projects funded by the World Bank, AfDB or ADB, with their own procurement rules.
SAE Towers
KEC's tower-manufacturing subsidiary in the Americas.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. How does a T&D contractor's risk differ from a transmission owner's?

The owner earns a long-term tariff; the contractor earns a one-time margin and bears execution, commodity and geopolitical risk.

2. Why did KEC's margin fall to 5.8%?

Shipping disruption, freight and fuel costs, labour shortages and delays on legacy projects in the Middle East, about a quarter of its order book.

3. Why is August weak for these stocks?

The monsoon halts construction and the April–June results, published in August, are the smallest quarter; the basket beat the Nifty in only 3 of 16 Augusts.

4. What has kept Techno Electric close to flat in 2026?
