Power Trading

India's electricity marketplace. Most power is sold under twenty-five-year contracts, but a growing slice changes hands on an exchange, a day ahead or in real time, at whatever the market clears. One listed company runs the dominant exchange; another is the country's oldest power trader. In 2026 the market they operate is busier and pricier than ever — and the regulator is about to take away the thing that made the exchange valuable.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

A booming market and a regulatory guillotine

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Imagine a vegetable market where farmers bring what they have left over and shops come to buy what they are short of, and every morning the market announces one price that clears everything. Electricity has a market like that. Power stations with spare electricity offer it for tomorrow, state electricity boards that are short bid for it, and the exchange works out the price. The exchange charges a tiny fee on every unit, and because it is almost all computers, nearly all of that fee is profit. For years one company ran most of this market. Now the government wants all the exchanges to pool their orders and let a neutral referee set a single price — which means the biggest market loses the very thing that made people come to it.

Say it to a client in one minute

Two very different businesses share this section, and one of them is facing a structural break.

  • IEX is the dominant power exchange: volumes 37.5 BU in the June quarter (+15.9%), revenue ₹158 cr (+11%), profit ₹135 cr (+12%), EBITDA margin 87.8%. Its gas exchange subsidiary grew profit 28%.
  • The market is extraordinarily tight: day-ahead prices averaged ₹4.88 a unit in August (+22%) and ₹7.83 in the first half of September, frequently touching the ₹10 ceiling as demand hit 269 GW.
  • The break is market coupling. The grid operator will combine bids from every exchange and set a single clearing price. Exchanges keep collecting orders but lose price discovery — the source of IEX's liquidity moat. The Supreme Court dismissed IEX's challenge in August 2026 and the regulator said rules would follow within four to six weeks.
  • PTC India is a trader, not an exchange: volumes up 12% to 25.8 BU at a margin of 3.35 paise a unit, but consolidated profit fell 54% to ₹112 cr, and its financing subsidiary is being readied for sale after breaching an infrastructure-lending threshold.
  • Stocks: IEX −14% and 61% below its peak; PTC −3%.

Analogy

A power exchange is a stock exchange for electrons. Like a stock exchange, its value comes from liquidity: buyers go where the sellers are, which is why one venue usually dominates. Market coupling is the equivalent of a regulator deciding that every stock exchange's orders will be matched centrally at one national price — the exchanges survive as order collectors, but the reason to prefer one over another largely disappears.

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Sellers: power stations with electricity nobody has contracted for offer it on the market.
  • Auction: everyone's offers and bids are lined up and a single price is found where they match.
  • Traders: middlemen who find buyers and sellers and take a tiny cut.
  • Dispatch: the grid operator makes sure the electricity actually flows where the trade says it should.
  • Buyers: state electricity boards that are short, especially on very hot days.
Fund-manager lens

An exchange's value rests on network effects: liquidity attracts liquidity, so the leader earns near-monopoly margins on a tiny per-unit fee. Market coupling attacks precisely that network effect by pooling liquidity across venues and handing price discovery to a neutral operator. What survives is the order-collection and clearing business, which is real but competitive. The analytical question for IEX is therefore not volume growth — volumes are growing 16% — but what fee it can hold and what share of orders it keeps once a trader can route to any exchange and receive the same price. Note that volumes already grew faster than revenue this quarter (15.9% against 11.3%), a small sign of the fee compression to come.

What market coupling does to the exchange's economics

Today, a buyer or seller chooses the exchange with the deepest pool of counterparties, because deeper liquidity produces a more reliable price. That is why one exchange dominates, and why it can charge a fee that earns an 88% margin. Under coupling, the system operator combines bids from all exchanges and computes a single national clearing price. An order placed on a smaller exchange then receives exactly the same price as one placed on the leader, which removes the liquidity argument for concentration. The exchanges become order-collection and clearing agents competing on fees and service. The regulator's stated rollout begins with the day-ahead market, with real-time and term-ahead markets to follow after pilots — so the day-ahead market, the benchmark and a large share of volume, goes first.

Analogy

It is as if every shop in a street were told that customers can buy at any of them but will all pay one price set centrally. The largest shop still has the most customers on day one — but it can no longer argue that its prices are better, so the only competition left is on the checkout fee.

7 · The business calendar

Shortage seasons

  • April–June: the summer peak drives exchange volumes and prices; the June quarter of 2026 saw 37.5 BU traded on IEX.
  • July–August: the monsoon cuts demand and lifts hydro and wind; spot prices usually soften.
  • September–October: a post-monsoon squeeze when hydro fades and demand stays high — 2026's September prices averaged ₹7.83, more than double a year earlier.
  • November–February: winter demand and lower solar output; a second active season for real-time trading.
  • Regulatory calendar: market coupling rules, fee orders and price-cap revisions, none of which follow the weather.

Reading it: a two-stock basket, and only one stock before IEX listed in October 2017, so the numbers here are thin. The one window with a sensible cause is October–December (9 of 14 years, median +3.4), when exchange volumes build into the winter and the post-monsoon shortage season. April–July is a coin toss (8 of 15, median +3.3) and July is the weakest month (4 of 16). Regulation, not the calendar, has driven every big move in this section.

A tendency, not a rule: small samples, survivorship bias (only names still listed) and equal weighting. Use it for timing context, not as a signal.

The exceptions, and why

  • October–December works 9 of 14 years (median +3.4), with failures concentrated in regulatory years: 2024 (−12.7), 2025 (−9.4), 2020 (−7.9) and 2017 (−7.4).
  • April–July failed seven times, including 2013 (−26.7), 2015 (−15.1) and 2018 and 2019 (−14.5 each) — surplus years when spot prices collapsed.
  • July is the weakest single month (4 of 16), matching the monsoon's effect on spot prices.
  • The largest moves in the record were regulatory: 2021's +153% on re-rating, 2022's −35% on price caps and the first coupling proposal, and the single-session 26% fall in 2025 when implementation was announced.
How to use this

Do not. This is a two-stock basket, one stock for its first six years, and its big moves were caused by regulatory announcements that follow no calendar. The October–December pattern has a plausible cause in the post-monsoon shortage season, but it is too thin to act on. The dates that actually matter are regulatory: the notification of the coupling rules, the start date for the day-ahead market, and any revision to exchange fees.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Eight drivers

  1. Market coupling: the structural question for the exchange, now cleared by the Supreme Court.
  2. Spot volumes: 37.5 BU on IEX in the June quarter, up 15.9%, driven by record demand.
  3. Transaction fees: the one variable that coupling puts under pressure.
  4. Short-term share of total power: the exchange grows as utilities rely more on spot and less on long-term contracts.
  5. Price caps: ₹10 a unit in the main markets, binding more often; caps suppress price discovery and can shift trade off-exchange.
  6. Renewable penetration: intermittent supply increases the need for real-time balancing, which is volume for exchanges (see 038).
  7. Gas market development: IGX's growth, which is independent of power coupling (see 033).
  8. For PTC: financing subsidiary outcomes — the divestment and the classification breach dominate reported profit.

The anatomy of this section's cycles

  1. Surplus power collapses spot prices and trading margins — 2015 and 2019.
  2. Utilities switch toward the exchange when spot power is cheaper than contracted power — the 2020 lockdown year, which set up 2021's +153%.
  3. Shortages push prices to the cap, and the regulator intervenes — price caps in 2022, market coupling from 2025.
  4. The operating business keeps growing through each intervention; the share price does not. That gap has been widest in 2026.

Patterns that repeat

  • Regulation is the dominant variable. Every large negative year for IEX followed a regulatory announcement, not a volume disappointment.
  • A monopoly margin invites intervention. An 88% EBITDA margin on essential infrastructure is exactly what regulators target.
  • Traders earn thin, volatile margins and their reported profit is often driven by something other than trading — for PTC, its financing subsidiary.
  • Shortage years are good for volumes and bad for regulation, which is why 2022 and 2026 look alike.

11 · The global picture

India is adopting what Europe already did

Market coupling is not an Indian invention. Europe coupled its national day-ahead power markets over the 2010s, so that a single algorithm now clears most of the continent at once, with prices differing only where transmission is congested. The European experience is instructive for IEX: coupling improved efficiency and cross-border flows, and it turned exchanges from price-setters into competing service providers whose economics depend on fees and ancillary products. India's design differs in one important respect — the grid operator, a state entity, becomes the coupling operator — and in its starting point, since India has a single dominant exchange rather than several national ones.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • From bilateral trading to exchanges (2008 onwards): power exchanges took share from traders by offering transparent prices, which is why PTC now routes about 60% of its own volume through them.
  • From one market to many: real-time, green and term-ahead markets widened the exchange's product range.
  • From exchange price discovery to operator price discovery: the shift now under way.

12 · Where the business is going

Six shifts

  1. Market coupling, starting with the day-ahead market and extending to real-time and term-ahead after pilots.
  2. Real-time market growth: renewables make balancing more valuable, and the real-time segment is where that trade happens.
  3. Longer-duration contracts on exchange: term-ahead and forward products that compete with bilateral trading.
  4. Capacity and ancillary markets: paying for availability and grid services, not just energy.
  5. Gas and carbon trading: diversification beyond electricity — IGX already, and carbon credits as India's carbon market develops.
  6. Battery arbitrage: storage owners buying cheap daytime power and selling in the evening peak (see 046).

The honest read

IEX is running the best market conditions it has ever seen — record demand, prices at the cap, volumes up 16% — and the share price is responding to none of it, because the question is no longer how much electricity is traded but who sets the price. Market coupling has survived the Supreme Court and is expected to begin with the day-ahead market. The bear case is straightforward: the liquidity moat that justified an 88% margin is removed by regulation, and fees compress as exchanges compete for orders at an identical price. The bull case is narrower but real: IEX will still hold most orders on day one, its real-time, green and gas markets are not coupled immediately, and the regulator's timeline has slipped before. Which of these proves right depends on the coupling rules' treatment of fees and on how quickly the real-time market follows — details worth reading in full when the notification appears. PTC is a separate question entirely, and it is currently a question about its financing subsidiary rather than about power trading.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What share of revenue comes from the day-ahead market that will be coupled first?
  2. What fee structure do the coupling rules allow, and how will exchanges compete?
  3. What market share is expected to be retained after coupling, and why?
  4. When will real-time and term-ahead markets be coupled, and what is their revenue share?
  5. How much of revenue growth came from volume and how much from fees?
  6. What new products — capacity, ancillary, carbon — can offset coupled-market pressure?
  7. (PTC) What is the timetable and expected value for the financing subsidiary's sale?
  8. (PTC) What is the credit exposure to state utilities, and the provision against it?

Red flags

  • Revenue persistently growing slower than volume.
  • Market share loss in the real-time market ahead of its coupling.
  • Guidance that treats coupling as distant after the Supreme Court decision.
  • A trader's profit dominated by a non-trading subsidiary.

19 · Glossary

Every term you'll meet

Markets and products

Power exchange
A regulated platform where electricity is bought and sold through auctions.
Day-ahead market (DAM)
Trading of power for each 15-minute block of the following day, cleared once daily.
Real-time market (RTM)
Trading for delivery within the hour, cleared in half-hourly sessions.
Term-ahead market (TAM)
Contracts for delivery from the next day up to several months ahead.
Green day-ahead market
A segment trading only renewable power.
High-price day-ahead market
A separate segment for costlier generation, such as imported gas, with a higher price ceiling.
Market clearing price (MCP)
The single price at which supply and demand match in an auction block — ₹7.83 on average in early September 2026.
Market clearing volume
The quantity traded at that price.
Double-sided auction
An auction in which both buyers and sellers submit bids, cleared at one price.
Time block
The 15-minute interval for which power is priced.
Price cap
The regulatory ceiling on clearing prices — ₹10 a unit in the main markets.
Market splitting
When transmission congestion forces different clearing prices in different regions (see 039).
Renewable energy certificate (REC)
A tradable certificate representing one unit of renewable generation.
Energy saving certificate
A tradable certificate for efficiency beyond a mandated target.
Bilateral trade
A negotiated deal between two parties, outside the exchange auction.
Short-term power market
All power traded under contracts shorter than a year — exchange and bilateral.
Ancillary services
Grid-stabilising services such as reserves and frequency response, increasingly traded.
Billion units (BU)
Billions of kilowatt-hours; IEX traded 37.5 BU in the June quarter (see 037).
Portfolio bid
A bid covering a buyer's whole requirement across many time blocks, rather than block by block.
Block bid
A bid that must be filled across several consecutive time blocks or not at all.
Capacity market
A market paying generators for being available rather than for energy produced — a product exchanges hope to add.
Forward and futures contracts
Contracts fixing a price for delivery months ahead; electricity derivatives are a developing segment in India.
Carbon credit market
The emerging Indian market for tradable emission credits, a diversification route for exchanges.

Market structure and regulation

Market coupling
Combining bids from all exchanges into one national auction run by a coupling operator, producing a single clearing price.
Market coupling operator
The entity running the combined auction — here the grid operator.
Round-robin model
A design in which the coupling function rotates or is shared, as initially outlined for the day-ahead market.
Price discovery
The process by which a market arrives at a price; the function coupling moves from the exchange to the operator.
Liquidity network effect
The tendency of trading to concentrate where trading already is — the basis of an exchange's moat.
Transaction fee
The per-unit charge an exchange levies on buyer and seller.
Trading licence
The regulatory permission for a trader to buy and resell power.
Trading margin
A trader's per-unit spread, regulated and typically a few paise; PTC's was 3.35 paise.
Grid India
The national system operator, which schedules power and will run market coupling.
Scheduling
Assigning cleared trades to physical generation and flows on the grid.
Deviation settlement
Charges for drawing or injecting more or less than scheduled.
Open access consumer
A large user buying directly from the market rather than the local utility (see 037).
Infrastructure finance company
A lender classification requiring most assets in infrastructure — 75% — which PTC's subsidiary fell below.
Transaction adviser
A banker appointed to run a sale process — engaged for PTC's financing subsidiary.
Clearing and settlement
Ensuring buyers pay and sellers deliver after a trade; retained by exchanges even under coupling.
Payment security mechanism
Collateral buyers post so that exchange trades settle even if a utility is slow to pay.
