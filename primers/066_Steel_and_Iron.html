Industry primer · 66 of 278 · Metals family, part 3

Steel & Iron

Steel is the physical form of economic growth: every building, bridge, car, pipe and appliance is mostly steel. India is the world's second-largest producer and, unlike China, still a growing one — 168 million tonnes of crude steel in FY26 and a national target of 300 million tonnes of capacity by 2030-31. It is also India's most capital-intensive industry, where a single plant costs tens of thousands of crores and takes years to build, and where profits are decided not by volume but by the gap between the price of steel and the cost of ore and coal. This primer explains the two ways steel is made, where the money is made and lost, how the 87 listed companies sort into four different businesses, and what the safeguard duty has changed.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Protected prices, record volumes, rising costs

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Steel is made in two ways. The first is like baking from scratch: you melt iron ore with coal in a furnace as tall as a building and pour out liquid iron, then blow oxygen through it to turn it into steel. The second is like reheating leftovers: you melt old scrap steel in an electric furnace and make new steel from it. India mostly bakes from scratch, because it has ore but not much scrap. The tricky part is that the steel maker cannot set the price — if steel from China is cheap, everyone wants that instead. So the government has put a tax on imported steel to keep Indian factories busy.

Say it to a client in one minute

The domestic story is strong; the cost and capacity story is the risk.

  • Demand: finished steel consumption of 164 mt in FY26, growing 7–8% — the fastest of any large economy.
  • Protection: safeguard duties of 12%, then 11.5% and 11% over three years, cut rolled-steel imports 38.7% in the first half of FY26 and lifted hot-rolled coil to about ₹52,860 a tonne.
  • Volumes: Tata's India crude steel +11%, Jindal Steel +15%, JSW +3% (about +15% excluding a furnace under upgrade).
  • Costs: coking coal is forecast around $190 a tonne, and India's imports are heading toward 86 mt by 2027.
  • Capacity: the 300 mt target means years of capital spending; JSW alone has begun a ₹16,350 cr plant in Andhra Pradesh.
  • Europe: Tata's UK business still lost ₹340 cr in the quarter, and its Dutch plans are being reassessed.

The split that matters commercially is flat versus long. Flat steel — coils and sheets — is made by large integrated mills, is the product that competes with imports, and is what the safeguard duty protects. Long steel — bars and rods for construction — is made by hundreds of smaller mills using induction furnaces and sponge iron, is priced locally, and is far less exposed to imports. That is why the listed universe behaves like two industries.

The chain in one breath

  • Miners supply ore; coal comes mostly from Australia.
  • Coke ovens and sinter plants prepare the feed.
  • Blast furnaces or electric furnaces make the steel.
  • Casters and rolling mills turn it into coils and bars.
  • Construction buys most of it, and decides the cycle.
Fund-manager lens

The critical number in steel is the spread: the steel price minus the cost of the ore and coal needed to make a tonne. Volumes barely move it — a mill running at 94% cannot run at 130% — but a ₹5,000 a tonne move in the spread changes profit per tonne by the same amount, which on 20 million tonnes is ₹10,000 cr. In 2026 the spread has widened at home because the safeguard duty lifted prices while ore fell, and narrowed abroad because coking coal is rising. Every other detail in this industry is secondary to that arithmetic.

This is the largest section in the watchlist and the most mixed. Steel's economics are brutally simple and brutally cyclical. A tonne of steel made through the blast furnace route needs roughly 1.6 tonnes of iron ore and 0.7–0.8 tonnes of coking coal; at $97 ore and $190 coal, the raw material bill alone is close to ₹28,000–30,000 a tonne before conversion costs. With hot-rolled coil at about ₹52,860, the spread is comfortable today. In 2015, when Chinese steel was dumped into India below cost, it was not, and the industry's debts nearly took several banks down with it.

Why Europe is a separate problem

Tata Steel's Indian business earned about ₹9,900 cr of EBITDA in Q1 FY27, up 32%, while its UK business lost ₹340 cr and its Dutch business earned ₹40 cr, down 94%, after an emissions-related shutdown of its direct sheet plant. European steel faces high energy costs, carbon rules and weak demand at once. The transition plans — an electric arc furnace in the UK, a hydrogen-ready project in the Netherlands now under reassessment — are expensive and slow. For investors, Tata Steel is therefore two companies: a strong Indian producer and a European restructuring.

Safeguards are a blunt instrument, and they cut both ways. Indian mills get a protected price and the confidence to invest; Indian steel users — pipe makers, auto component firms, engineering companies — pay more than their global competitors, which matters for exports. The duty is also temporary by design and faces trade challenges. The investment question is therefore whether the capacity being built under this protection will still be profitable when it expires: the industry is adding tonnes toward a 300 mt target while China, with far more capacity than it needs, keeps exporting.

Coking coal is the industry's structural weakness. India has almost no good coking coal of its own, so every tonne of blast-furnace steel carries an imported input priced in dollars, shipped from Queensland, and subject to cyclones and strikes. It is the main reason Indian mills' costs rise when the rupee falls, and the main argument for the electric route — which India cannot yet take at scale because it has too little scrap.

7 · The business calendar

Construction sets the rhythm

  • January–March: the peak construction and dispatch quarter; mills push volumes before the fiscal year ends.
  • April–May: new-year contracts, but also the pre-monsoon slowdown in building; May has been the weakest month for the stocks.
  • June–September: the monsoon stalls construction; prices usually soften and mills take maintenance shutdowns, as SAIL and Jindal Steel did this year.
  • October–December: post-monsoon restocking and the festival-to-year-end construction push; December has beaten the Nifty in 12 of 15 years.
  • Throughout: monthly price announcements by the large mills, and quarterly import data, move sentiment.

Reading it: the basket is ten liquid steel makers — JSW, Tata, Jindal Steel and SAIL plus Godawari, Sunflag, Kalyani Steels, Vardhman Special Steels, Jai Balaji and Prakash Industries — equal-weighted, so mid-caps carry as much weight as the giants. The pattern follows construction: mills restock before the monsoon and again after it, while May brings the pre-monsoon slowdown and the year's first price cuts.

A tendency, not a rule: small samples, survivorship bias (only names still listed) and equal weighting. Use it for timing context, not as a signal.

The exceptions, and why

  • April has failed only in 2013 (−5.7), 2025 (−4.3) and 2019 (−1.7) — and delivered +33.9 in 2021, +19.4 in 2026 and +12.9 in 2014.
  • December has failed only in 2011, 2016 and 2014, and produced +16.6 in 2015, +16.1 in 2017 and +15.6 in 2022.
  • May is the weakest month, beating the Nifty in 5 of 15 years, with −16.0 in 2022 when the export duty was announced.
How to use this

Steel's calendar is the most usable in the Metals family because the underlying driver — construction activity and the monsoon — is physical and repeats. But policy overrides it: May 2022's export duty and April 2025's safeguard duty both moved the stocks more than any season. Use the calendar for timing, and watch Delhi for direction.

9 · What moves margins

Eight drivers

  1. Domestic steel prices. Set by import parity, now with a safeguard duty on top.
  2. Coking coal. The biggest imported cost; forecast around $190 a tonne for 2026.
  3. Iron ore. Domestic, but priced off import parity (064).
  4. Chinese exports. The global swing factor and the reason for protection.
  5. Capacity ramp-ups. New furnaces at JSW, Jindal Steel and Lloyds add volume and debt.
  6. Product mix. Value-added and special steels earn more per tonne.
  7. Leverage. Net debt decides who survives the down years.
  8. Carbon rules. The green steel taxonomy at home and carbon border charges in Europe.

The anatomy of the cycles

  1. Over-expansion and dumping (2012–2015): capacity built on debt met Chinese exports; the basket lost 23% in 2013 and 19% in 2015, and several producers ended up in insolvency.
  2. Protection and recovery (2016–2017): a minimum import price and anti-dumping duties turned prices, and the basket rose 155% in 2017.
  3. The squeeze (2018–2019): prices peaked, costs rose, credit tightened.
  4. The stimulus boom (2020–2021): Chinese stimulus and post-lockdown demand produced record spreads.
  5. Policy whiplash (2022–2025): an export duty in 2022, an import surge in 2024, a safeguard duty in 2025.
  6. Protected growth (2026): record domestic volumes and prices, with cost and capacity risk ahead.

Patterns that repeat

  • Policy, not demand, marks the turns. The minimum import price in 2016, the export duty in 2022 and the safeguard duty in 2025 each reset the stocks.
  • Small caps overshoot. Jai Balaji rose 1,293% in 2023 and fell 60% in 2025; the majors moved a fraction of that.
  • Balance sheets decide the survivors. The 2012–2016 debt cycle ended with Essar, Bhushan and Monnet in insolvency and their assets in stronger hands.

India is the only large steel market still growing quickly, which is why global producers want access to it and why its government keeps raising barriers. The national ambition is to double capacity to 300 mt by 2030-31 and to reach 500 mt by 2047; the Green Steel Taxonomy published in December 2024 sets star ratings by carbon intensity — five stars below 1.6 tonnes of CO₂ per tonne of steel — and by March 2026, 89 units covering 12.34 mt of production had been certified. Those two goals pull against each other: blast furnaces are the cheapest way to add tonnes and the hardest to decarbonise.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Electric arc furnaces. Tata's ₹3,000 cr, 0.85 mt Ludhiana plant emits about 0.3 tonnes of CO₂ per tonne against 2.2 at Jamshedpur.
  2. Scrap collection. A formal scrap industry is the precondition for electric steel at scale.
  3. Hydrogen and direct reduction. Promising but expensive; Tata's Dutch project is being reassessed.
  4. Slurry pipelines and conveyors. Cheaper ore logistics, as at Jindal Steel and Lloyds.
  5. Value-added mixes. Coated, automotive and special steels to escape commodity pricing.
  6. Carbon accounting. Star ratings at home and border charges in Europe make emissions a commercial number.

What is priced in

The market is paying for protected domestic volumes but not for a new upcycle. The large producers have risen modestly — JSW +10%, Jindal Steel +6%, Tata +3% — while the mid-caps closest to the domestic construction market have done better. That looks right: the safeguard duty supports prices for three years, but coking coal is rising, Chinese exports have not stopped, and the industry is committing enormous capital to reach 300 mt. The best outcomes will come from producers that add tonnes without adding debt and that can hold a value-added mix.

The honest read

Steel is the sector where balance sheets, not forecasts, decide returns. The last capacity cycle ended in insolvency courts. This one starts from a healthier place — lower debt, protected prices, real domestic demand — but the same test applies: what happens to the new furnaces when the duty lapses in 2028 and China is still exporting. Own the low-cost, low-debt producers; treat the small sponge-iron names as leveraged bets on the spread, not as businesses.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What was EBITDA per tonne, and how much came from price versus cost?
  2. What is the landed cost of coking coal, and how many months of inventory are held?
  3. What share of iron ore is captive, and at what cost?
  4. How much of the volume growth is new capacity and how much is utilisation?
  5. What is the value-added share of the mix, and where is it going?
  6. What is net debt, and what is the peak capex year?
  7. What happens to pricing if the safeguard duty is diluted or challenged?
  8. What is the export share and the effect of Europe's carbon border charges?
  9. What is the carbon intensity per tonne and the plan to reduce it?
  10. For Tata Steel: what is the cash requirement of the UK and Dutch operations over the next three years?

Spread

EBITDA per tonne
The industry's core profitability measure.

Output as a share of capacity; blast furnaces must run near full.
Captive mine
A mine owned by the steel maker.

A temporary tariff imposed when imports surge.
Anti-dumping duty
A duty on imports sold below fair value.
Minimum import price
A price floor for imports, used in India in 2016.
DGTR
The Directorate General of Trade Remedies, which investigates import surges.

The plan targeting 300 mt of capacity by 2030-31.

India's 2024 star-rating system for steel by carbon intensity.

Europe's carbon border charge on imported steel from 2026.

The process that transferred Essar, Bhushan and Monnet to new owners.
Integrated producer
A mill that runs the whole chain from ore to rolled steel.

Conversion cost
Everything besides raw materials needed to turn ore into steel.

Periodic rebuilding of a furnace's lining, which halts output for months.
Scrap
Recycled steel, the main feed for electric furnaces.
Captive jetty
A port berth owned by a plant to land coal and ship steel.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. What are the two routes to making steel?

The blast furnace route from ore and coke, and the electric route melting scrap or sponge iron.

2. What is the spread, and why does it matter more than volume?

Steel price minus raw-material cost per tonne; mills already run near full capacity, so profit moves with the spread.

3. What is the safeguard duty schedule?

12% from April 2025, 11.5% from April 2026 and 11% from April 2027, with imports above $675/t CIF exempt.

4. What happened to imports after it was imposed?

Rolled-steel imports fell 38.7% year on year in the first half of FY26 to 3.04 mt.

5. Why did Jindal Steel's profit fall 44% while volumes rose 15%?

Maintenance shutdowns, margin pressure and higher finance costs.

6. What is India's biggest imported input?

Coking coal — about 82 mt in 2026, forecast near $190 a tonne.

7. What is the national capacity target?

300 mt of capacity and 255 mt of production by 2030-31.

8. Which months have worked best for these stocks?

April and December, each beating the Nifty in 12 of 15 years; May is the weakest.

9. What is Tata Steel's European problem?

The UK lost ₹340 cr in Q1 FY27 and the Dutch green-steel project is being reassessed amid high energy costs and emissions rules.

10. What does the Green Steel Taxonomy measure?

Carbon intensity per tonne: five stars below 1.6 t CO₂e, four stars 1.6–2.0, three stars 2.0–2.2.
