Gas Transmission & LNG

The toll booths of the gas chain. One company owns most of the pipeline grid and is paid a regulated tariff for every unit that passes through it; another owns the terminals that turn imported liquid gas back into gas and is paid a fee per unit regasified. Neither takes much price risk on the molecule — but both live or die on volume, and in 2026 the volume is being priced away by the most expensive LNG market in years.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Paid by the unit, hostage to the price

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Gas is awkward stuff: it takes up a huge amount of space, so you cannot just put it in a truck. There are two ways to move it. If the gas is close by, you push it through a buried steel pipe for hundreds of kilometres — and the company that owns the pipe charges a small fee for every unit that passes. If the gas is on the other side of the world, you make it so cold that it turns into a liquid six hundred times smaller, sail it over in a special ship, and warm it back into gas at a terminal on the coast — and the terminal charges a fee for doing that. Neither company owns the gas. They own the road and the doorway. But if the gas becomes too expensive for anyone to buy, the road is empty and the doorway is quiet, and both still have to pay for the pipe and the terminal they built.

Say it to a client in one minute

These are regulated infrastructure businesses with volume risk and almost no commodity price risk — which sounds defensive and is not quite.

  • GAIL earns a regulated tariff on pipeline throughput, plus gas marketing and a petrochemical business that is currently losing money. Q1 FY27 profit nearly doubled, helped by transmission and pricing gains; transmission ran at 122.36 mmscmd against 118.99 in the prior quarter, with about 123 guided for the year.
  • Petronet LNG earns a regasification fee — about ₹69 per mmbtu at Dahej and ₹98 at Kochi. Profit rose 33%, yet Dahej ran at 65.62% of its newly expanded 22.5 mtpa capacity and company-wide utilisation fell to 58% from 76%.
  • The squeeze is the LNG price. Spot cargoes tested $25 per mmbtu in 2026; Indian buyers are price-sensitive and simply stop. Petronet is also awaiting clarity on Qatari cargoes amid shipping disruption.
  • The long-run case is intact: LNG imports are still expected to grow about 10% in 2026, city gas demand grew about 8% in 2025, and the trunk network keeps expanding (see 030).
  • Confidence Petroleum, up 152%, is an LPG bottler and does not belong in the same analysis as the other two.

Analogy

Think of a toll road and a ferry terminal. The toll road (transmission) collects a fixed fee per vehicle regardless of what the cargo is worth. The ferry terminal (regasification) charges each container that is unloaded. Neither cares about the price of the goods inside — until that price gets so high that nobody ships anything, at which point the toll booth and the terminal are both idle while their construction loans keep accruing. That is exactly what $25 LNG does to a terminal.

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Supply: India does not produce enough gas, so much of it arrives by ship as a liquid.
  • Liquefaction: abroad, the gas is chilled until it becomes a liquid that takes six hundred times less space.
  • Regasification: at an Indian port, the liquid is warmed back into gas and pushed into pipes.
  • Transmission: the gas travels across the country in big buried pipes.
  • Distribution: smaller city pipes take it to kitchens and to pumps that fill CNG cars.
Fund-manager lens

These are volume businesses wearing a tariff's clothing. The tariff protects the price you earn per unit; nothing protects the number of units. So the analysis reduces to three questions. Is the molecule affordable? At $25 per mmbtu, no — Indian industrial and power buyers switch away, and terminal utilisation falls to 58%. Is the network growing? Yes: the grid is heading towards 35,000 km and 307 city gas areas are authorised (see 030, 034). Is the tariff being reset? PNGRB reviews are the standing regulatory risk for the transmission owner. A defensive-looking infrastructure stock that is actually a leveraged bet on the affordability of imported gas is the thing to understand here.

Classification note

Confidence Petroleum belongs in a different bucket. It bottles and distributes LPG and makes cylinders; it owns no transmission pipeline and no regasification terminal, and its economics are contract manufacturing and retail distribution rather than regulated tariffs. Its 152% rise this year pulls the equal-weighted basket to +43% while the median stock is +3%. Every aggregate figure in this primer is quoted with the median alongside for that reason, and the recommendation is to move it to LPG distribution or to the gas cylinders section.

Why GAIL's profit doubled while Petronet's terminals emptied

Both companies are exposed to the same expensive-LNG environment, but at different points. GAIL's transmission volumes actually rose, because a large share of pipeline throughput is domestic gas and price-insensitive demand — fertiliser plants and city gas networks that must take the molecule. On top of that, its gas marketing arm made pricing gains in a volatile market, which is a trading outcome rather than a tariff one. Petronet, by contrast, is almost purely a function of how much LNG India chooses to import: when spot cargoes cost $25 per mmbtu, price-sensitive industrial and power buyers withdraw, and a terminal that has just expanded from 17.5 to 22.5 mtpa finds itself running at 65.62%. Its profit still rose 33%, which shows how well long-term use-or-pay contracts protect the earnings — but utilisation at 58% company-wide is the number that decides the next three years.

Analogy

A regulated tariff is an umbrella, not a roof. It keeps the rain off the price you charge, but if fewer people walk past your stall you still sell less. Petronet just built a bigger stall in the year the crowd stayed home.

7 · The business calendar

A northern-hemisphere calendar, not an Indian one

  • Northern winter (November–February): European and North-East Asian heating demand sets the global spot price, and therefore what India can afford.
  • Indian summer (April–June): peak power demand, when gas-fired generation competes with coal — economic only at low LNG prices.
  • Monsoon (June–September): industrial demand softens; it is also the season in which shipping and offshore supply are most disrupted.
  • Tariff and contract cycles: PNGRB tariff orders and annual escalation of regasification fees are scheduled, not seasonal.
  • Contract renegotiations: long-term LNG contracts are periodically reopened, and each renegotiation resets the economics for years — as the Qatar contract did in 2015.

  • Windows: Feb–Mar beat the Nifty in 5 of 15 years (median -1.3 pts); Apr–Jul beat the Nifty in 7 of 15 years (median -1.6 pts); Aug–Sep beat the Nifty in 8 of 14 years (median +4.5 pts); Oct–Dec beat the Nifty in 6 of 14 years (median -1.7 pts); Sep–Nov beat the Nifty in 7 of 14 years (median -0.0 pts); Jan–Mar beat the Nifty in 6 of 15 years (median -2.7 pts).

Control: small caps as a whole (Nifty Smallcap 100) beat the Nifty in April in 11 of 15 years, by an average of 3.0 points, and lag in January–February. Part of any April strength is this market-wide effect. After removing it, this industry's own April edge is about +0.6 points.

Reading it: unusually weak, and that is informative. April is the only dependable month (11 of 15 years, +3.6 points) and August–September the only decent window (8 of 14, median +4.5). April–July has beaten the Nifty in just 7 of 15 years with a negative median of −1.6, and October–December in 6 of 14. A regulated tariff business with multi-year contracts has no reason to display a seasonal pattern — what it displays instead is the LNG price, and that follows northern-hemisphere winters and geopolitics rather than the Indian calendar.

A tendency, not a rule: small samples, survivorship bias (only names still listed) and equal weighting. Use it for timing context, not as a signal.

The exceptions, and why

  • April–July is the weakest such window in the series so far — 7 of 15 years and a median of −1.6. Its failures are numerous rather than dramatic: 2019 (−16.3), 2013 (−10.1), 2017 (−8.9), 2012 (−8.1).
  • 2026 is the great exception at +39.6, almost entirely Confidence Petroleum rather than the gas utilities.
  • August–September is the one window that works (8 of 14, median +4.5), with 2017 (+15.4) the best.
  • October–December has failed in eight of fourteen years, which fits: that is when northern-hemisphere winter pricing starts to bite on Indian affordability.
How to use this

Do not use it. This is the clearest case in the series of a sector with no usable seasonality: a two-stock basket for most of the record, regulated revenue, and a dominant variable — the global LNG price — that follows a foreign winter and a geopolitical calendar. The honest substitutes are the spot LNG price, Petronet's quarterly utilisation, and GAIL's transmission volumes. If the LNG price falls to the $6–7 range that buyers describe as desirable, volumes return and both stocks re-rate; if it stays near $25, no month of the year will help.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Eight drivers

  1. Spot LNG prices: the affordability gate for the whole chain. India is a price-elastic buyer, which is the single most important fact about this industry.
  2. Long-term contract terms: crude-linked formulae that determine the base load of imports.
  3. Domestic gas production: the cheap molecule that fills pipelines regardless of LNG prices (see 028).
  4. Transmission tariffs: PNGRB determinations, including unified tariff arrangements that change who pays what across the grid.
  5. Terminal utilisation: 58% company-wide at Petronet against 76% a year earlier.
  6. City gas and fertiliser demand: the price-insensitive base that protects volumes (see 034).
  7. Network expansion: new trunk lines connecting the east and north-east to the grid (see 030).
  8. Petrochemical spreads: for GAIL specifically, a ₹130 cr quarterly segment loss shows how much this can matter.

The anatomy of a gas cycle

  1. LNG becomes cheap, usually because crude falls or new liquefaction capacity starts up.
  2. Price-sensitive demand returns — industry, power, and spot buying by city gas licensees. Terminals fill and transmission volumes rise.
  3. Infrastructure is built into the optimism: Dahej expanded from 17.5 to 22.5 mtpa, new pipelines are authorised.
  4. A supply shock lifts spot prices — 2022's war, 2026's West Asian disruption — and Indian buyers withdraw within weeks. Utilisation falls, new capacity looks premature, and the stocks de-rate even though tariffs are untouched.

Patterns that repeat

  • Indian gas demand is the most price-elastic in Asia. That is why 2022 and 2026 look so similar despite different causes.
  • Tariffs protect profit far better than they protect the share price. Petronet's profit rose 33% this quarter and the stock is up 3%.
  • The best years follow crude collapses — 2014 (+50%) and 2016 (+31%) — because oil-linked LNG contracts reprice downward.
  • GAIL's petrochemical arm is a persistent distraction: it caused the 2019 underperformance and cost ₹130 cr this quarter.

11 · The global picture

India buys last, and that is a strategy

The global LNG market is a contest between three demand centres — North-East Asia, Europe and South Asia — for cargoes produced in Qatar, the United States, Australia and a handful of other exporters. Europe and Japan will pay almost any price for security of supply; India will not. Indian buyers are famously price-elastic, stepping back when spot prices rise and returning when they fall, which makes the country the market's swing buyer and its own terminals the shock absorber. In 2026, with spot cargoes testing $25 per mmbtu, that elasticity is visible in Petronet's utilisation. The offsetting force is that a large wave of new global liquefaction capacity is expected to arrive, which would limit prices and spur exactly the demand India has been deferring — and Indian LNG imports were still expected to grow around 10% for the year.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • From domestic gas to imports (2010s): the decline of KG-basin output turned India into a structural LNG importer and created the regasification business.
  • From oil-linked to hybrid pricing: renegotiated long-term contracts and a growing spot market changed how the landed cost is set.
  • From a single terminal to a network: capacity has been built along both coasts, and the constraint has shifted from terminals to affordability and last-mile pipelines.

12 · Where the business is going

Six shifts

  1. Unified tariff regimes: a single national transmission tariff structure rather than zone-by-zone pricing, which changes the economics of distant customers.
  2. Grid integration of the east and north-east: connecting previously stranded regions, which adds volume without adding much cost.
  3. Small-scale LNG and trucked LNG: supplying industry and long-haul trucking beyond the pipeline network.
  4. LNG for heavy transport: a potential new demand pool if the fuel-price differential holds.
  5. Gas exchanges and market-based pricing: the Indian Gas Exchange traded 58.2 million mmbtu in nine months, up 46%, building a transparent spot market.
  6. Hydrogen blending readiness: early-stage specification work on transporting hydrogen in existing networks (see 030).

The honest read

This is the most interesting set-up in the Oil & Gas family, precisely because nothing is broken. The assets are regulated, the profits are at or near records, the network keeps expanding and the long-run demand case — city gas growth of about 8%, LNG imports still expected to grow about 10% — is intact. What is missing is an affordable molecule, and that is a global variable neither company controls. If the expected wave of new liquefaction capacity arrives and spot prices fall towards the $6–7 range buyers call desirable, the deferred demand returns into infrastructure that has already been built and paid for, which is exactly the configuration that produced 2014 and 2016. If West Asian disruption persists and prices stay near $25, terminals stay half empty and the expansion looks early. Own this for the price of gas falling, not for the tariff — and do not let Confidence Petroleum's 152% tell you the sector is working, because the two companies that actually own the infrastructure are up 1% and 3%.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What is transmission volume by customer segment, and how much is price-insensitive?
  2. What is terminal utilisation by terminal, and how much of it is use-or-pay rather than actual offtake?
  3. At what delivered LNG price does deferred industrial and power demand return?
  4. What share of sourcing is long-term contracted versus spot, and on what formula?
  5. How much of this quarter's profit came from gas marketing spreads rather than tariffs?
  6. What is the status of contracted cargoes given shipping disruption?
  7. What is the expected tariff outcome at the next PNGRB review?
  8. (GAIL) What is the plan for the petrochemical segment at current gas costs, given the ₹130 cr quarterly loss?
  9. (Petronet) What is the payback assumption on the Dahej expansion at 58–65% utilisation?

Red flags

  • Profit held up by trading gains while volumes fall.
  • Use-or-pay revenue masking a structural loss of offtake.
  • Capacity expansion commissioned into a demand pause.
  • Petrochemical losses treated as temporary for several consecutive years.

18 · Myths and live debates

What you'll hear, and the evidence

ClaimEvidence
"Regulated tariffs make these defensive stocks."They defend the price per unit, not the number of units. Petronet's company-wide utilisation fell from 76% to 58% while its tariff was untouched.
"High gas prices are good for gas infrastructure."The opposite. India is the market's price-elastic buyer; at $25 per mmbtu the molecule does not move, and an empty pipe earns nothing.
"The sector is up 43% this year."The median stock is up 3%. An LPG bottler that owns no pipelines is carrying the basket.
"Gas will be 15% of India's energy mix."It is a long-standing policy goal that remains far off, and each LNG price spike pushes it further away by teaching industrial users to switch fuels.
"The Dahej expansion was a mistake."Premature, perhaps; a mistake only if prices stay high. Infrastructure built into a price trough is how 2014 and 2016 were won — the capacity is there when affordability returns.
"GAIL is a pure gas utility."It is a utility with a petrochemical business attached, and that business lost ₹130 cr in a single quarter. Model the segments separately.

19 · Glossary

Every term you'll meet

The physical chain

Natural gas
Mainly methane, produced from gas fields or alongside oil (see 028).
LNG
Liquefied natural gas: gas cooled to about −162°C, shrinking roughly 600-fold so it can be shipped.
Liquefaction
The process of cooling gas into liquid at the exporting end.
Regasification
Warming LNG back into gas at an importing terminal — the service Petronet sells.
LNG carrier
The specialised insulated ship that transports LNG.
Terminal
The port facility that receives, stores and regasifies LNG. Dahej is 22.5 mtpa after expansion from 17.5.
Trunk pipeline
The high-pressure cross-country network that carries gas between regions (see 030).
Compressor station
Equipment along a pipeline that maintains pressure so gas keeps moving (see 029).
Burner tip
The point of final consumption — the phrase used for delivered gas prices.
Liquid hydrocarbons (LHC)
Propane, butane and heavier liquids extracted from gas; GAIL produced 232 TMT in the quarter.

Units

mmscmd
Million metric standard cubic metres per day, the unit for pipeline flow. GAIL moved 122.36.
mmbtu
Million British thermal units, the unit in which gas and LNG are priced (see 028).
TBTU
Trillion British thermal units, used for terminal throughput. Dahej handled 192 in Q1 FY27.
MTPA
Million tonnes per annum, the unit of terminal or plant capacity.
TMT
Thousand metric tonnes, used for polymer and LPG volumes.

Commercial and regulatory

Transmission tariff
The regulated charge per unit for moving gas through a pipeline.
Unified tariff
A single national tariff structure replacing zone-based pricing, which lowers delivered cost for distant users.
Regasification tariff
The fee per mmbtu for converting LNG back to gas: about ₹69 at Dahej, ₹98 at Kochi.
Use-or-pay
A contract requiring the customer to pay for booked terminal capacity whether or not it is used — what protected Petronet's profit as volumes fell.
Take-or-pay
The equivalent obligation on a gas purchase contract: pay for the gas whether or not it is taken.
Long-term contract
A multi-year LNG supply agreement, usually priced off a crude formula.
Spot cargo
A single LNG shipment bought at the prevailing market price.
Crude-linked / oil-indexed pricing
LNG priced as a percentage of a crude benchmark, which is why 2014's crude collapse cut gas costs.
Henry Hub and JKM
The American gas benchmark and the North-East Asian LNG spot benchmark; India buys against the latter.
Price elasticity
How much demand falls when price rises. Indian gas demand is highly elastic — the defining feature of this market.
Utilisation
Throughput as a share of capacity; 58% company-wide at Petronet against 76% a year earlier.
Open access
The right of third parties to use a pipeline or terminal they do not own.
PNGRB
The Petroleum and Natural Gas Regulatory Board, which sets tariffs and authorisations (see 030).
Indian Gas Exchange (IGX)
The domestic gas trading platform; it traded 58.2 million mmbtu in nine months, up 46%.
Gas marketing
Buying and reselling gas for a spread — the part of GAIL that carries genuine price risk.
Priority allocation
The rule directing cheap domestic gas first to city gas and fertiliser users.
Auto LPG
LPG dispensed as a vehicle fuel; Confidence Petroleum's expansion area.
Bottling plant
A facility that fills LPG cylinders, usually under contract to a state marketer.
Ship-or-pay
A pipeline contract obliging the shipper to pay for booked capacity whether or not gas is flowed; the transmission equivalent of use-or-pay.
Capacity booking
Reserving terminal or pipeline capacity in advance, which converts a volume business into a partly fixed-revenue one.
Landed cost
The delivered price of imported gas including shipping, regasification and transmission — the number an Indian buyer actually compares with coal or naphtha.
Fuel switching
Industrial users moving between gas, coal, furnace oil and naphtha as relative prices change; the mechanism behind India's price elasticity.
Floating storage and regasification unit (FSRU)
A moored vessel that regasifies LNG, a faster and cheaper alternative to building a land terminal.
Boil-off gas
The small fraction of LNG that evaporates in storage and transit; managing it is part of terminal efficiency.
Destination clause
A contract term restricting where a cargo may be delivered; removing it lets a buyer resell cargoes it cannot use.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. What do these companies actually sell?

A service, not a molecule: transmission charges a regulated tariff per unit moved, regasification a fee per unit converted from liquid back to gas.

2. Why did Petronet's utilisation fall to 58%?

Spot LNG tested $25 per mmbtu. Indian buyers are price-elastic and withdraw, so a terminal expanded from 17.5 to 22.5 mtpa ran at 65.62%.

3. How did Petronet's profit rise 33% anyway?

Use-or-pay contracts and operational efficiency: customers pay for booked capacity whether or not they use it, which protects earnings but not the long-run volume case.

4. Why did GAIL's transmission volumes rise while imports fell?

Much of the pipeline throughput is domestic gas going to price-insensitive users — fertiliser and city gas — plus gas marketing gains in a volatile market.

5. What is the section's 2026 return, properly stated?

The basket is +43% against a Nifty −11%, but the median stock is +3%: GAIL +1%, Petronet +3%, and Confidence Petroleum +152%.

6. Why doesn't Confidence Petroleum belong here?

It bottles LPG, makes cylinders and runs auto-LPG stations. It owns no pipeline and no terminal, and its economics are contract manufacturing and distribution.

7. What LNG price would restore Indian demand?

Buyers describe $9–10 per mmbtu as reasonable for peak power use and $6–7 as economically desirable — against spot testing $25.

8. Why were 2014 and 2016 such good years?

Crude collapsed, and because long-term LNG contracts are oil-linked, the landed cost of gas fell with it. Volumes and stocks both responded.

9. What is GAIL's persistent distraction?

Its petrochemical segment: 51 TMT of polymer produced and a ₹130 cr loss in Q1 FY27, and a major cause of its 2019 underperformance.

10. Why is there no usable seasonality here?

Revenue is regulated and contracted, and the dominant variable is the global LNG price, which follows northern-hemisphere winters and geopolitics rather than the Indian calendar. April–July has a negative median.
