1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Medicine for crops, sold like medicine for people

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Farmers grow food. Bugs eat it, weeds steal its water and sunlight, and fungus makes it sick. Agrochemical companies make medicines for plants. A handful of very rich companies invent brand-new medicines, which takes more than ten years and roughly ₹2,500 crore each. After about 20 years the recipe becomes free, and many companies can make cheaper copies. India makes a lot of these copies, for its own farmers and for the world. Most of the medicine is sold when the rains come (June to September), because that is when crops grow and pests multiply.

Say it to a client in one minute

Indian agrochem is two businesses glued together. One is a domestic, monsoon-driven branded business, where the moat is distribution and new product launches. The other is an export and B2B business in technicals and contract manufacturing, where China sets the price. Rain moves domestic volumes; Chinese technical prices move margins; and margins move the stocks more than rain does. 2021–22 was a China-driven price boom. 2023–24 was a global destocking bust. 2025 brought volume recovery at low prices. 2026 has a weak El Niño monsoon, but Chinese prices are firming after Beijing removed export tax rebates. Historically these stocks run from April to July on monsoon hopes and fade once the season is known.

2 · What farmers are fighting

Three enemies, three product families

Insecticides — the thieves

Insects that chew leaves, bore into stems (rice stem borer), eat bolls (cotton bollworm) or suck sap (whitefly, aphids, jassids, thrips). Examples: chlorpyrifos and acephate (old organophosphates), cypermethrin (pyrethroid), imidacloprid (neonicotinoid), chlorantraniliprole (diamide).

Herbicides — the freeloaders

Weeds compete with the crop for water, nutrients and light. Selective herbicides kill weeds but spare the crop (2,4-D in wheat, pretilachlor in rice). Non-selective ones kill everything green (glyphosate, glufosinate, paraquat).

Fungicides — the infections

Fungal diseases spread in humid, wet weather: rice blast and sheath blight, potato late blight, grape mildew, wheat rust. Examples: mancozeb (the volume workhorse), hexaconazole and tebuconazole (triazoles), azoxystrobin (strobilurin).

Everything else

Plant growth regulators (paclobutrazol to induce mango flowering), seed treatments, biopesticides (Bt, Trichoderma, neem) and biostimulants (seaweed extracts, amino acids) that improve plant vigour rather than kill anything.

Why India's mix looks different from the world's

Globally, herbicides are the largest category. India has historically been insecticide-heavy: one recent industry estimate puts insecticides at ~41% of the market, herbicides ~22%, fungicides ~21%, and biostimulants and plant growth regulators most of the rest. Three reasons:

  • Tropical climate. Heat and humidity mean more insect generations per season.
  • Cheap labour. Weeding was done by hand. As rural wages rise and workers migrate, herbicides gain every year. This is the most dependable structural shift in the Indian market.
  • Crop mix. Rice is the single largest consumer of crop protection, followed by cotton, then fruits and vegetables, soybean, chilli and wheat. Cotton used to be dominant, until Bt cotton (2002) cut bollworm spraying sharply.

India uses only about 0.3–0.5 kg of pesticide per hectare, against ~13 kg in China, ~12 in Japan and ~3.6 in the US. Read that number carefully (see debates): modern molecules are dosed in grams, not kilograms, so kg per hectare understates value.

3 · The value chain

From benzene to a 250 ml bottle in a village shop

Analogy: making orange-juice concentrate and selling it as a ready-to-drink bottle. The concentrate (technical) is too strong to use directly. Formulation dilutes it, stabilises it and makes it spread and stick on a leaf. The brand and the dealer get it to the farmer on credit, with advice.

Fund-manager lens

Profit pools sit at the two ends: invention and patents (innovators and their CSM partners), and the relationship with the dealer (branded domestic formulators and registration owners). The middle, off-patent technicals, is a spread business where China decides your margin. Backward integration only helps if it takes you out of a molecule China floods.

How the domestic channel really works

  1. Placement (sell-in), March–June. Companies push stock into distributors ahead of Kharif, often with credit and early-booking discounts. Revenue is booked here.
  2. Liquidation (sell-through), July–September. Farmers actually buy when pests appear. Good liquidation means the channel is clean; poor liquidation means stock sits with dealers.
  3. Returns and credit notes. Unsold or expiring stock comes back, or is discounted, after the season. This hits the weakest quarter, January–March.
  4. Collections. Dealers pay after farmers pay, after harvest. Receivables can run 100+ days at peak, so working capital is the silent risk in domestic agrochem.
Fund-manager lens

For a domestic formulator, watch three things together: revenue growth, receivable days and next quarter's returns. Revenue up with receivables up faster is channel stuffing. Reported growth that reverses as credit notes in Q4 was never real.

7 · What the stocks do each year

The April–September rally is really April–July

You mentioned that agrochem stocks rally from April to September. I tested that on 15 years of TradingView prices, using an equal-weighted basket of 10–19 listed agrochem names (more as they listed) against the Nifty 50, from July 2011 to September 2026.

  • April is the strongest month. The basket beat the Nifty in 14 of 15 years, by a median 7.3 points. April is when IMD issues its first monsoon forecast and companies start Kharif placement.
  • April–July is the window. The basket beat the Nifty in 13 of 15 years, by a median 13.7 points. Even excluding the two giant years (2014, 2020) the average edge is about 14.5 points.
  • August–September is a coin flip (8 of 15 years, average roughly zero). By then the monsoon outcome is known: buy the forecast, sell the rain.
  • February–March is reliably weak. The basket lagged in 12 of 15 years, by a median 3.5 points. That's the dead season: returns, collections, and the weakest quarter's results ahead.

The exceptions, and why

  • 2019 (−9.3 points). The monsoon arrived late (Kerala onset on 8 June) and June rain was about a third below normal, during a broad small-cap bear market. The season eventually ended 10% above normal, but the stocks had already de-rated.
  • 2023 (−6.5 points). An El Niño year that coincided with the global destocking bust. Chinese overcapacity collapsed generic prices, so margins fell even where volumes held.
  • Late-season sell-offs (August–September). 2015: drought became visible and the yuan devaluation crash hit. 2018: the IL&FS small-cap sell-off. 2021: after a +36-point run the basket topped out while the Nifty rallied. 2025: US tariffs rose to 50% and floods hit Punjab.
  • 2026 so far. March fell 16% on the Iran-war oil shock, and April rebounded 25%, the textbook April move. From May the basket faded as the El Niño deficit built.
Control test: is this just small caps?

Most of the basket is small- and mid-cap, and small caps as a group also tend to do well in April: the Nifty Smallcap 100 beat the Nifty in 11 of 15 Aprils, by 3.0 points on average. So the test was re-run against the small-cap index instead of the Nifty. April still wins in 11 of 15 years, by an average 4.6 points (median 5.1), against 14 of 15 and 7.5 points versus the Nifty. Roughly 40% of the April edge is therefore the general small-cap April effect, and the rest is specific to agrochem. April–July holds up better: the basket beat the small-cap index in 13 of 15 years by a median 17.0 points (the misses were 2023 and 2026), while small caps’ own median edge over the Nifty in the same window was 6.3 points. The four-month monsoon trade is an industry effect, not just a size effect.

How to use this

This is a tendency, not a rule. The average hides wide ranges, the basket carries survivorship bias (it holds only names still listed), and 15 observations is a small sample. Used well, it tells you when a cheap agrochem idea is likely to be re-rated (April–July) and when patience is cheap (February–March). It isn't an investment recommendation.

8 · What moves demand and margins

Rain moves volumes; China moves margins

Demand (volumes)

  • Monsoon timing and spread matter more than the season total. June rain decides sowing; July–August rain decides pest pressure; the share of districts in deficit tells you how widespread the damage is.
  • Pest and disease pressure. Humid spells bring fungus and sucking pests; dry spells favour whitefly and mites. Too much rain washes sprays off and leaves fewer days to spray.
  • Acreage and crop mix. Cotton is insecticide-intensive, rice uses everything, maize and wheat lean to herbicides. Ethanol-driven maize expansion shifts demand.
  • Farm income. MSPs, mandi prices and PM-KISAN transfers decide whether a farmer sprays once or three times.
  • Labour. Rising rural wages keep pushing herbicide adoption (and direct-seeded rice).
  • Export markets. Brazil's soybean season (September–December), US planting, EU approvals, currency.

Margins (price minus cost)

  • Chinese technical prices. The single biggest swing factor: 2017–18 environmental closures, 2021 energy "dual control", the 2023–24 capacity glut, 2025–26 "anti-involution" and the removal of export rebates.
  • Raw materials and energy. Crude-linked solvents and intermediates; the 2026 oil shock lifted costs, and price hikes taken in March–May were later rolled back.
  • Patent expiries. Year one after expiry is a bonanza for the first generics; year three is a price war (chlorantraniliprole is the live example).
  • Product mix. New molecules, in-licensed products and mixtures carry higher margins than old generics.
  • Rupee. A weak rupee helps exporters and hurts importers of technicals. The dollar was about 12% stronger against the rupee year on year by mid-2026.
  • Channel inventory. High stocks mean discounts and returns; lean stocks give pricing power.

9 · Twelve years of cycles

2014 to 2026, year by year

YearMonsoon (% of LPA)What happenedAgro basketNifty

Monsoon: IMD June–September all-India rainfall as a share of the long-period average; 2026 is season-to-date (~15% deficit in September). Returns: calendar-year, price only; 2026 runs to 18 September.

The anatomy of an agrochem cycle

  1. China tightens (environmental shutdowns, energy curbs) → technical prices rise.
  2. The world panic-buys → distributors overstock and Indian technical makers post record margins; IPOs cluster here (2021: Heranba, India Pesticides).
  3. China adds capacity and inventory piles up → prices collapse and everyone destocks; it took two seasons (2023–24) to clear.
  4. Trough and normalisation → volumes recover before prices do (2025), then prices firm (2026).

Patterns that repeat

  • Rain alone is a poor guide to annual returns. The stocks rose in the 2014 and 2015 droughts, and fell in 2019 despite 110% rain. The margin cycle and starting valuation dominate.
  • IPO clusters mark peaks. From their 2021–23 highs, Heranba is down ~80%, India Pesticides ~63% and Best Agrolife ~84%.
  • Exporters and domestic players take turns. In FY24 exports fell ~20% while domestic held up; in Q1 FY27 exporters did better while domestic stalled on a weak monsoon.
  • Destocking takes about two seasons, because inventory built over one hot year has to be sold through two normal ones.

10 · The global picture

Who invents, who manufactures, who buys

The global crop-protection market was about $74.8bn in 2023, $70.1bn in 2024 and $68.8bn in 2025 in nominal dollars (2025 was +2.0% in constant currency), per AgbioInvestor. Latin America is the largest region ($21.5bn, Brazil above all), followed by Asia-Pacific ($19.3bn), Europe ($13.6bn) and North America ($11.8bn). The eight majors (BASF, Bayer, Corteva, FMC, Sumitomo Chemical, Syngenta, ADAMA, UPL) sold $58.2bn in 2025. Off-patent products make up most of the volume.

How leadership moved

  • US and Europe invented and made most molecules until the 1990s. They still invent: Syngenta (now Chinese-owned), Bayer, Corteva and BASF.
  • Japan became the quiet innovation hub (Sumitomo, Nissan Chemical, Mitsui, Nihon Nohyaku, ISK, Kumiai). Japanese innovators license molecules to Indian partners such as Dhanuka and Insecticides India.
  • China became the world's factory for technicals and intermediates in the 2000s, with integrated chlor-alkali, phosphorus and glycine chains. It also owns Syngenta and ADAMA.
  • India rose as the trusted second source ("China+1"): chemistry talent, respect for IP (which the CSM model needs), and the registrations built by UPL and Sharda. India is now the third-largest exporter; exports nearly trebled from $1.3bn in FY15 to $3.3bn in FY25.

India's edge over China is trust and reliability, not cost. China still makes most key intermediates more cheaply. When China floods the market, as in 2023–24, Indian generic technicals lose, and only CSM, niche molecules and domestic brands hold up.

11 · Where technology is going

Six shifts to follow

  • New modes of action. Resistance keeps old molecules dying. Recent launches include diamides, mesoionics and new SDHIs; BASF launched fenmezoditiaz (for rice) and dimpropyridaz in India. New molecules mean in-licensing deals for Indian formulators and CSM contracts for PI.
  • Mixtures. Two or three actives in one pack slow resistance and let Indian companies differentiate generics; each mixture needs its own registration.
  • Herbicide tolerance. ICAR released non-GM herbicide-tolerant basmati varieties in 2024, and direct-seeded rice is spreading. Herbicide-tolerant cotton remains unapproved; if it is ever cleared, glyphosate demand jumps.
  • Biologicals. Biopesticides and biostimulants are growing from a small base. Regulation just got strict: 9,352 provisional biostimulant registrations lapsed in June 2025, leaving 146 fully approved products by September 2025. That's a shakeout that favours serious players.
  • Drones and precision spraying. Government SOPs allow drone application of registered products, and the Namo Drone Didi scheme is putting drones with women's self-help groups. Long term, precision spraying uses less product per acre.
  • Seed treatment and gene technology. New GM traits can reduce spraying (Bt cotton did), a structural negative for insecticide volumes.

Using this with your inflection skill

Before calling any agrochem company an "inflection", subtract the cycle. Margin expansion in 2026 when Chinese prices firm is cycle; volume growth in a good monsoon is cycle. Company-level inflection looks like: market-share gains in a bad monsoon, margins rising while Chinese prices fall, new 9(3) launches and in-licensing deals, a CSM molecule win, or receivable days falling while revenue grows. This baseline is the antidote to the skill's habit of finding inflections everywhere.

16 · Questions for management

A concall checklist that works for any agrochem company

Ask

  1. How much of this quarter's growth was volume, and how much price?
  2. Was liquidation ahead of placement? How many weeks of channel inventory do you see against normal?
  3. What share of sales comes from products launched in the last three and five years? How many 9(3) registrations are pending?
  4. What share do your top five molecules make up, and which face generic entry in the next 24 months?
  5. What share of raw materials comes from China, and which key materials are single-sourced?
  6. Receivable days and credit notes this season versus last?
  7. For CSM: how many molecules are commercial versus in development, what is the top customer's share, and is any molecule near patent expiry?
  8. For exporters: registrations owned by region, US tariff exposure by product, Brazil credit exposure?
  9. At what Chinese price does your new plant earn its cost of capital?

Red flags

  • Receivables growing faster than sales, especially in Q4.
  • "One-off" returns or credit notes every year.
  • Inventory built ahead of a weak monsoon.
  • Margin gains that are entirely lower raw-material costs.
  • Capex on molecules China makes at scale.
  • Revenue booked through related-party distributors.
  • A company calling itself "specialty" with no new registrations or CSM contracts to show for it.

17 · Myths and live debates

What people say, and what the evidence says

What you'll hearWhat the evidence says
"India uses 0.5 kg/ha against China's 13, so the runway is huge."Partly misleading: modern molecules are dosed in grams (a diamide at ~30 g of active per hectare vs hundreds of grams for old organophosphates). Growth comes from mix (herbicides, fungicides, new modes of action), not kilograms.
"Good monsoon means a good agrochem year."Distribution and pest pressure matter more, and too much rain cuts spray days. For the stocks, China-driven margins mattered more than rain: they rose in the 2014–15 droughts and fell in 2019's excess year.
"China+1 is permanent."India wins on trust, not cost. 2023–24 proved China can flood the market. The 2026 rebate removal and anti-involution push help, but they are policies, and policies change.
"CSM is an annuity."It is lumpy: innovator destocking and patent expiries hit CSM volumes in 2024–26, and customer and molecule concentration is high.
"A patent expiry is a bonanza."For about a year. Then dozens of 9(4) registrations arrive and prices fall; FMC cited Rynaxypyr (chlorantraniliprole) price declines.
"Biologicals will replace chemicals."They are growing from a small base, and regulation just removed thousands of products. They will complement chemicals rather than replace them.

18 · Glossary

Every term you'll meet in this industry

Product and chemistry

Active ingredient (AI, a.i.)
The molecule that actually controls the pest; everything else in the bottle is a carrier.
Technical (TC)
The active ingredient manufactured at high purity (typically 90–98%). The agrochem equivalent of an API; sold B2B, never to farmers.
Formulation
Technical plus solvents, surfactants and fillers, turned into a usable product; "5% EC" means 5% active by weight.
EC
Emulsifiable concentrate: an oil-based liquid that turns milky in water. Solvent-heavy, so crude-linked.
SC
Suspension concentrate: fine solid particles suspended in water. Safer and more modern than EC.
WP / WG (WDG)
Wettable powder / water-dispersible granules. WG is dust-free and premium.
GR / G
Granules broadcast into paddy water or soil.
SL, CS, OD, FS
Soluble liquid; capsule suspension (micro-encapsulated, slow release); oil dispersion; flowable concentrate for seed treatment.
Adjuvant / surfactant
A helper chemical that makes the spray spread and stick on the leaf.
Dose (g a.i./ha)
Grams of active ingredient per hectare. Old organophosphates need hundreds of grams; diamides need tens.
Mixture / premix
Two or more actives in one pack, for resistance management and differentiation. Needs its own registration.
Tank mix
The farmer mixes separate products in the sprayer.
Systemic vs contact
Systemic moves inside the plant; contact kills on touch.
Translaminar
Moves through the leaf to reach pests on the underside.
Selective vs non-selective
Selective herbicides spare the crop (2,4-D in wheat); non-selective ones kill all green (glyphosate, paraquat, glufosinate).
Pre- vs post-emergence
Applied before weeds sprout vs after they appear.
Residual activity
How long a product keeps working after spraying.
Mode of action (MoA)
The biological target a molecule hits. Numbered groups are set by IRAC (insecticides), FRAC (fungicides) and HRAC (herbicides); rotating groups slows resistance.
Resistance
Pests surviving a molecule that used to kill them. The reason new MoAs command premiums.
Seed treatment
Chemical or biological coating on seed before sowing.
PGR
Plant growth regulator, e.g. paclobutrazol (mango flowering), ethephon (ripening).
Biopesticide
Living organisms or natural products that control pests: Bt, Trichoderma, Beauveria, neem, NPV viruses.
Biostimulant
Improves vigour or stress tolerance without being a nutrient or pesticide (seaweed, humic acids, amino acids). Regulated under FCO Schedule VI.
Organophosphates (OP)
Old, cheap, broad-spectrum insecticides (chlorpyrifos, acephate, profenofos); being phased out for toxicity.
Synthetic pyrethroids (SP)
Fast knock-down insecticides (cypermethrin, lambda-cyhalothrin). Heranba's core.
Neonicotinoids
Systemic insecticides for sucking pests (imidacloprid, thiamethoxam); restricted in the EU over bee safety.
Diamides
Modern chewing-pest insecticides (chlorantraniliprole/Rynaxypyr, flubendiamide, cyantraniliprole). The blockbuster class that went generic.
Triazoles
Systemic fungicides (tebuconazole, hexaconazole, propiconazole). Astec's core.
Strobilurins (QoI)
Broad-spectrum fungicides (azoxystrobin, pyraclostrobin).
SDHI
Newer fungicide class (fluxapyroxad, fluindapyr).
Mancozeb
A dithiocarbamate contact fungicide and the volume workhorse.
Glyphosate / glufosinate / paraquat
Non-selective herbicides. Glyphosate is the world's largest by volume; glufosinate is its fast-growing alternative.
Pretilachlor, pendimethalin, atrazine, 2,4-D, metribuzin
Key selective herbicides: rice pre-emergent; pre-emergent in many crops; maize; wheat broadleaf; soybean and potato.
HPPD / PPO inhibitors
Newer herbicide modes (mesotrione, tembotrione for maize; PPO for broadleaf weeds).
Toxicity label triangle
The coloured half-diamond on every Indian pesticide label: red = extremely toxic, yellow = highly toxic, blue = moderately toxic, green = slightly toxic.
LD50
The dose that kills half of test animals; the basis for the toxicity colour.
PHI
Pre-harvest interval: minimum days between the last spray and harvest.
MRL
Maximum residue limit allowed on food; export markets (EU) are strict.

Agronomy and weather

Kharif / Rabi / Zaid
Monsoon-sown (June–July), winter-sown (October–December) and summer crop seasons.
LPA
Long-period average rainfall: 87 cm for June–September (1971–2020 base). Normal = 96–104% of LPA; deficient = below 90%; excess = above 110%.
El Niño / La Niña / IOD
Pacific warming usually weakens the Indian monsoon; La Niña usually strengthens it. The Indian Ocean Dipole can offset either.
Monsoon onset / withdrawal
Arrival over Kerala (normal ~1 June) and retreat from north-west India (September–October).
Spray window
Dry days when spraying works; excess rain shrinks it.
Pest pressure
How severe infestations are this season; the real demand driver.
ETL
Economic threshold level: pest density at which spraying pays.
IPM
Integrated pest management: combining biological, cultural and chemical control.
Sucking vs chewing pests
Aphids, jassids, whitefly, thrips vs bollworms, stem borers, fall armyworm.
BPH
Brown plant hopper, a major rice pest.
Pink bollworm
Cotton pest that developed resistance to Bt cotton.
Fall armyworm
Invasive maize pest; reached India in 2018.
Blast / sheath blight / late blight / rust / mildew
Major fungal diseases of rice, rice, potato and tomato, wheat, and grapes and vegetables.
DSR
Direct-seeded rice: sown instead of transplanted; saves water and labour, needs herbicides.
Bt cotton / HTBt cotton
Insect-resistant GM cotton (approved 2002) / herbicide-tolerant version (not approved; grown illegally).
MSP
Minimum support price the government announces for crops; anchors farm income.

Regulation

CIB&RC
Central Insecticides Board & Registration Committee, under the agriculture ministry's plant-protection directorate; registers every product.
Section 9(3)
Registration of a molecule new to India; full data package, typically 3–5 years.
Section 9(3B)
Provisional registration, usually for two years.
Section 9(4) / me-too
Registration of a copy of an already-registered molecule; mainly documentation, typically 6–12 months.
Label claim
The specific crop–pest pairs a product is approved for.
Ban / restricted use
Prohibited outright, or allowed only through licensed operators (glyphosate since 2022).
PCO
Pest control operator, a licensed applicator.
FCO
Fertiliser Control Order, which also governs biostimulants.
DGTR / ADD / CVD
Trade-remedies directorate; anti-dumping duty; countervailing (anti-subsidy) duty.
Pesticides Management Bill
Proposed replacement for the 1968 Act (2020 draft, revised 2025 draft).
EPA / EU approval / Brazil MAPA
Registration regimes abroad; slow and data-heavy, hence valuable once held.
Data protection
Exclusive use of the data an innovator submitted; a long-running industry demand in India.

Business and channel

Innovator / R&D company
Discovers and patents molecules (Syngenta Group, Bayer, Corteva, BASF — the "Big 4").
Off-patent / generic / post-patent
Molecules free to copy after patent expiry; the majority of global volume.
Differentiated generic
A generic made special by a mixture, new formulation or registration others lack.
CSM / CRAMS / CDMO
Custom synthesis manufacturing for an innovator, under contract and confidentiality.
Toll / job-work manufacturing
Making product for a brand owner who supplies the specification.
In-licensing
An Indian company selling an innovator's molecule in India under agreement (common with Japanese innovators).
Registration dossier
The data package behind a registration; for asset-light players, it is the asset itself.
Backward integration
Making your own technicals or intermediates instead of buying them.
KSM / intermediate
Key starting material: the chemical building block before the technical.
China+1
Buyers adding a non-Chinese second source; India's main export tailwind since 2017.
Anti-involution
China's 2025–26 campaign against below-cost price wars in overbuilt industries, including pesticides.
Export VAT rebate
Chinese tax refunded on exports; removing it raises China's export price.
Placement (sell-in)
Stock pushed into distributors ahead of a season; where revenue is booked.
Liquidation (sell-through)
Stock actually bought by farmers.
Channel inventory
Stock sitting with distributors and retailers.
Destocking / restocking
The channel running inventory down (sales fall) or building it up (sales rise faster than use).
Sales returns / credit notes
Unsold stock sent back, or discounts given after the season.
Early-booking / pre-season scheme
Discounts for dealers who order early.
Cash discount
A reduction for paying early; a way to pull in collections.
Spurious / counterfeit
Fake or sub-standard products; official sampling finds ~2.5% misbranded, while industry claims higher illegal share.

Financial KPIs

Volume vs price growth
The split of revenue growth; price-led growth in a falling-cost year is fragile.
Realisation
Average price received per kg or litre.
Spread
Selling price minus raw-material cost per kg; the core of technical economics.
Gross margin
Revenue minus materials; shows pricing power and mix.
Working-capital days
Receivables + inventory − payables, in days; domestic players often run 100+.
Receivable days
How long dealers take to pay; rises before trouble.
Asset turnover
Revenue ÷ fixed assets; technical plants typically turn 1–1.5× at full use.
Capacity utilisation
Plant output vs capacity; drives fixed-cost absorption.
Product vintage
Share of revenue from products launched recently; a proxy for innovation.
Molecule concentration
Share of revenue from the top few molecules; a patent-expiry and CSM risk.

19 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. Why can't a farmer use a technical directly?

It is too concentrated, often not water-mixable, and wouldn't spread or stick. Formulation (EC, SC, WG) makes it dilutable, stable and effective on a leaf.

2. 9(3) vs 9(4): why does it matter for margins?

9(3) brings a new molecule to India (3–5 years, heavy data), which gives pricing power. 9(4) copies an existing registration in months, so many players pile in and prices fall.

3. Why did agrochem stocks rise in 2014–15 despite two droughts?

Rain drives volumes, but returns were driven by a re-rating and margins. Annual stock returns track the margin cycle and valuation more than rainfall.

4. Which months have historically been best and worst against the Nifty, and why?

April (14 of 15 years) and the April–July window (13 of 15) are the best, on the IMD forecast and pre-season placement. February–March is the worst (12 of 15 lagged): returns, collections and the weakest quarter.

5. Explain the 2021–24 boom and bust in three steps.

China's energy curbs raised technical prices and the world panic-stocked (2021–22). China added capacity while inventories sat high, so prices collapsed and the channel destocked (2023–24). Volumes recovered at low prices (2025).

6. Why is India's 0.5 kg/ha figure misleading as a measure of headroom?

Modern molecules are dosed in grams per hectare, so value per hectare and the adoption of newer chemistry are better measures than kilograms.

7. Name two China-controlled inputs and what they feed.

Glycine and yellow phosphorus feed glyphosate. Triazole feeds tebuconazole and hexaconazole. Pyridines feed imidacloprid and paraquat.

8. How would you tell a formulator's margin gain is company-specific?

If it holds while Chinese prices fall, in a weak monsoon, and comes with new 9(3) launches, in-licensed molecules or better mix, rather than just cheaper raw materials.

9. What does China's April 2026 rebate removal do to Indian players?

Chinese export prices of those technicals rise ~9%, giving Indian makers of the same molecules pricing room. It raises costs for Indian formulators who import them, and Chinese formulations (not covered) gain an edge abroad.

10. How can too much rain hurt sales?

It leaves fewer spray days, washes sprays off and can damage crops, so farmers skip or delay applications.
