Industry primer · 61 of 278 · Defence family, part 5

Drones & Space Technology

Drones have changed how wars are fought and how farms, mines and power lines are inspected; private rockets and satellites are changing who can reach space. India has policies to support both — a ban on imported drones, production incentives, fast-track military procurement, and a space sector opened to private companies. Yet the listed universe is tiny: one real drone maker, one satellite-communications company and a handful of micro-caps. This primer explains the technology and the value chain, where the money is made, why the listed drone stocks have mostly disappointed, and where the space story sits today.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Big themes, a small listed universe

Explain it to a 10-year-old

A drone is a small flying robot with a camera. A soldier can fly one over a hill to see what is on the other side without being seen; a farmer can fly one to spray crops; an engineer can fly one along a power line to find damaged wires. A satellite is like a very tall lighthouse in the sky that can see or talk to the whole country at once, and a rocket is the lift that carries it up. India used to buy many drones from other countries and only the government could launch rockets. Now Indian companies make drones, and private companies have started launching rockets too.

Say it to a client in one minute

Drones and space are among India's fastest-growing defence and technology themes, but there are few ways to own them in the listed market.

  • Drones: ideaForge is the only listed drone maker of scale — Q1 FY27 revenue ₹68.6 cr (+437%), EBITDA positive, order book over ₹300 cr at the start of the year, 60% defence. The stock is still 44% below its July 2023 listing-day close.
  • Pipeline: about ₹20,000 cr of military drone and counter-drone purchases are reported to be moving through fast-track procurement after Operation Sindoor.
  • Space communications: Avantel makes satellite and defence communication systems — Q1 FY27 revenue +36%, profit +67%, order book about ₹1,000 cr — at a very high multiple.
  • Space: the real action is unlisted — Skyroot reached orbit in July 2026; more than 440 space start-ups are registered.
  • Caution: the listed drone SMEs have fallen 50–90% from their highs; Space Incubatrics is an IT company in insolvency, not a space company.

A drone is mostly electronics and software: flight controller, motors, batteries, cameras, radio link and the software that makes it fly and see. That is why drone economics resemble electronics more than aviation — the hardware becomes cheaper every year, and value moves to sensors, secure communications, autonomy and service. In space, the same logic applies to small satellites, while rockets remain capital-intensive, high-risk engineering projects.

The chain in one breath

  • Parts makers — many abroad — supply motors, batteries, sensors and chips.
  • Software makes the drone fly, see and stay secure.
  • Drone makers assemble and certify the aircraft for the forces and industry.
  • Service firms train pilots and fly missions.
  • Space firms build the links and rockets that reach orbit — mostly unlisted.
Fund-manager lens

The critical weakness in the drone chain is stage 1. The forces now insist on drones without Chinese-origin parts in critical systems, and Indian component supply is still thin, so makers face higher costs and delays. The critical strength is stage 2: software, secure links and navigation without GPS are what the forces pay for, and what separate a defence supplier from an assembler. ideaForge's investment case rests on its software and its long record with the Army; the SME service companies have neither.

5 · How money is made

Lumpy government orders on a thin base

ideaForge's history shows how lumpy the military drone business is. Revenue rose sharply after the Army's emergency purchases in 2021–2022, then fell in FY25 as those purchases ended and newer orders took time — the stock fell 29% in 2024 and 21% in 2025. In Q1 FY27, revenue jumped more than fivefold from that low base and EBITDA turned positive, but gross margin fell as a higher-cost mix shipped. Management expects the Army's higher procurement limits for field commanders and about ₹20,000 cr of fast-track purchases to lift orders in the second half.

Avantel's economics are steadier but concentrated: a few defence and space customers, contracts measured in tens or low hundreds of crores, and margins that vary with the mix of development and production work. Its profit of ₹5.4 cr in Q1 FY27 against a market value of about ₹3,774 cr shows how much growth is already priced in.

Why the service SMEs struggled

A wave of drone-training and services companies listed on the SME platforms in 2022–2023, riding government schemes to train pilots and put drones on farms. Training demand fell once the first wave of certifications was done, and services proved price-competitive. Droneacharya's revenue halved in FY26; Drone Destination's stock is 93% below its high. The lesson is the same as in other themes in this project: a fashionable theme does not guarantee a profitable business model.

5b · Military drone demand

From emergency buys to a standing requirement

The war in Ukraine and India's own Operation Sindoor in May 2025 showed that cheap drones — for surveillance, strike and as decoys — are now part of every battle, and that air defences must be able to stop them. The Indian forces responded in three ways: emergency and fast-track purchases, raising the financial powers of field commanders so units can buy drones directly, and the iDEX programme, which by February 2026 had cleared 58 prototypes worth ₹3,853 cr for procurement.

For listed investors, the key point is that this demand will be spread across many suppliers — ideaForge, Solar, BEL, Zen, Paras and dozens of unlisted start-ups — and that the forces increasingly insist on Indian software and non-Chinese components. Market share will go to companies that can pass those tests at scale.

The shift away from Chinese parts is the defining supply issue. For years, most small drones worldwide were built with Chinese motors, batteries, cameras and flight controllers. India's forces now screen military drones for Chinese-origin components, which raises costs and forces makers to requalify suppliers. That is both a burden and a moat: a company that has built a clean supply chain has an advantage over new entrants that have not.

7 · The business calendar

Year-end defence buying and scheme cycles

  • January–March: the forces spend remaining budgets; deliveries and billing peak.
  • April–May: fourth-quarter results; new-year contracts and emergency-procurement windows.
  • July–September: monsoon limits agricultural spraying and outdoor surveys; defence demonstrations and trials continue.
  • October–March: the rabi crop season supports agricultural drone use; defence orders accumulate for year-end.
  • Event-driven: border incidents and conflicts trigger emergency purchases, as in 2020–2021 and after May 2025.

Management commentary from ideaForge points to the third and fourth quarters of FY27 for the next wave of orders. As elsewhere in defence, quarterly results swing with delivery timing, so trailing twelve-month numbers are more useful than single quarters.

Control: small caps as a whole (Nifty Smallcap 100) beat the Nifty in April in 11 of 15 years, by an average of 3.0 points, and lag in January–February. Part of any April strength is this market-wide effect. After removing it, this industry's own April edge is about +7.0 points.

Reading it: until July 2023 the basket is Avantel alone — a satellite and defence-communications company, not a drone maker — and after that Avantel plus ideaForge. April has beaten the Nifty in 13 of 15 years (average +10.0 points), the strongest April in the Defence family, and April–July in 10 of 15 (median +17.9). January–March has worked in only 6 of 15 (median −4.6) and August–September in 6 of 14 (median −6.8). December has been unusually firm (12 of 15). The April strength matches the rest of the family — fourth-quarter results and new-year defence orders — but rests largely on one company.

A tendency, not a rule: small samples, survivorship bias (only names still listed) and equal weighting. Use it for timing context, not as a signal.

The exceptions, and why

  • April–July failed in 2018 (−51.2), 2015 (−13.4), 2012 (−7.8), 2016 (−7.4) and 2019 (−6.8); the best runs were 2021 (+103.1) and 2023 (+88.8).
  • April itself failed only twice, and narrowly: 2019 (−0.6) and 2018 (−0.0).
  • January–March failed in 2025 (−33.4), 2015 (−22.9) and 2024 (−16.5), among nine losing years.
How to use this

This is mostly Avantel's record, a thinly traded stock that tripled twice (2014, 2017) and quadrupled once (2023). The April pattern matches the Defence family and has a business reason, but with one or two stocks the sample is small. Use it as context alongside 057–060, where the same April–July strength appears across more companies.

9 · What moves margins

Eight drivers

  1. Defence orders. Emergency and fast-track purchases drive volume.
  2. Component sourcing. Non-Chinese parts cost more.
  3. Product mix. Military systems earn more than civil drones and services.
  4. Software content. Autonomy and secure links command a premium.
  5. Scale. Fixed engineering costs are large relative to revenue at today's size.
  6. Government schemes. PLI, Drone Shakti and agricultural subsidies support civil demand.
  7. Competition. Many unlisted start-ups compete for the same orders.
  8. Space programme budgets. ISRO and defence space spending drive Avantel-type suppliers.

The anatomy of the cycles

  1. Communications era (2012–2020): the listed record is Avantel, a small communications supplier with violent swings — +227% in 2014, +242% in 2017, −49% in 2018.
  2. The drone moment (2021–2023): emergency Army purchases after Galwan, the 2022 import ban and drone PLI; ideaForge's IPO was heavily oversubscribed and listed 93% higher.
  3. Disappointment (2024–2025): emergency orders ended, ideaForge's revenue fell, and the SME drone companies collapsed.
  4. Operation Sindoor and fast-track buying (2025–2026): new demand, a revenue recovery at ideaForge, and a private rocket in orbit.

Patterns that repeat

  • IPO excitement fades. ideaForge's listing day remains its high; the drone SMEs peaked soon after listing.
  • Emergency orders are not a run-rate. Revenue built on one-off purchases falls when they end.
  • Drone businesses re-rate fast and de-rate fast, in India and the US alike.

The war in Ukraine turned drones from a niche into a mass-produced weapon: both sides use millions a year, and cheap drones now destroy expensive equipment. That has pushed every military to buy drones and counter-drone systems quickly and to secure supply chains outside China. In space, reusable rockets have cut launch costs sharply and made small satellite constellations viable; India's opening of the sector in 2020 aims to capture a share of that market, from about $8.4 bn today toward a $44 bn target by 2033.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Autonomy. Drones that navigate and choose targets with less human control.
  2. GPS-denied navigation. Jamming is common; drones must navigate by vision and inertial sensors.
  3. Swarms. Many cheap drones coordinated together.
  4. Counter-drone lasers and electronic warfare. Cheap defences against cheap drones.
  5. Small satellites and constellations. Earth observation and communications from many small satellites.
  6. Private launch. Skyroot's Vikram-1 and others make Indian launch capacity commercial.

Each shift increases the software and electronics content of the product and shortens product cycles. That favours companies with strong engineering teams and makes last year's hardware obsolete quickly — a risk for companies whose advantage rests on a single product.

What is priced in

ideaForge, at about ₹3,491 cr, is priced for its order book to grow several times over as fast-track purchases turn into contracts; its recovery from a low base is real, but its margins have not yet proved themselves. Avantel's multiple assumes that defence and space communications keep compounding. Both are small companies in a sector where orders are lumpy and competition from unlisted start-ups is intense. The most exciting parts of the theme — private rockets, satellite constellations, large military drones — are not available on the Indian market at all.

The honest read

Drones and space are real, durable growth areas for India, and demand is rising. But the listed universe is thin, and history — ideaForge's post-IPO decline, the collapse of the drone SMEs — shows that theme enthusiasm runs ahead of profits. The better-established ways to own the theme may be indirect: Solar (loitering munitions), BEL and Zen (counter-drone), and the space suppliers in defence electronics.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. How much of the order book is signed, and when will it be delivered?
  2. What share of revenue is defence, and how much comes from one-off emergency purchases?
  3. What share of components is still imported, and how much from China?
  4. How has the move to non-Chinese parts affected cost and margin?
  5. What is the gross margin by product, and what explains this quarter's change?
  6. Which fast-track opportunities are you bidding for, and against whom?
  7. How much do you spend on R&D, and what share of your product is your own software?
  8. What is your export pipeline?
  9. How are receivables from government customers trending?
  10. For Avantel: what share of revenue comes from space, and how large is the ISRO pipeline?
