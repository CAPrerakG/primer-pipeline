Oil & Gas EPC

Engineering, procurement and construction for hydrocarbons: the companies that survey a route, buy the pipe, weld it into a line hundreds of kilometres long, bury it, test it and hand it over. 1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Building the pipes that the rest of this family fills

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Gas has to travel from where it comes out of the ground to the stove in your kitchen, and it travels through a steel pipe buried in the earth. Somebody has to put that pipe there. First they walk the whole route and decide where it goes, then they have to get permission from every farmer whose field it crosses, then trucks bring steel pipes twelve metres at a time, a machine digs a trench, welders join the pipes end to end, and the whole line is filled with water at high pressure to check it does not leak. Only then is it buried and handed over. The company doing all that is an EPC contractor. It is paid for building the pipe, not for the gas inside it.

Say it to a client in one minute

This is a one-stock section attached to a very large infrastructure programme.

  • The programme is real: a national gas grid heading towards 35,000 km, four new trunk pipelines of about 2,500 km in progress, and 307 authorised city gas areas covering essentially the entire population.
  • The listed exposure is one company. Likhitha Infrastructure, about ₹900 cr, builds cross-country pipelines, city gas networks and terminals. The large contractors in this space — L&T's hydrocarbon arm, Megha Engineering — are either unlisted or listed as diversified conglomerates.
  • The current picture is a divergence: Q1 FY27 revenue −30.5% and profit −47%, but an order book of ₹1,454 cr and a first large international win, the ₹510 cr Abu Dhabi contract.
  • The risk in EPC is never demand; it is execution — right-of-way, monsoon, client clearances and receivables.

Treat the section as a single-stock bet on conversion, not as sector exposure to the gas build-out.

Analogy

A pipeline contractor is a tailor who is given the cloth, the measurements and a deadline, and is paid a fixed price for the finished suit. If the cloth arrives late, or the customer changes the measurements halfway, or it rains for six weeks so the workshop cannot open, the price does not change — only the cost does. That asymmetry is the whole risk profile of EPC.

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Survey: engineers walk and map the route, deciding how to get under rivers and roads.
  • Right of use: every landowner along the way has to be compensated and persuaded. This takes longer than the building does.
  • Procurement: the steel pipe arrives in lengths, often bought by the customer rather than the builder.
  • Construction: a trench is dug, pipes are welded into a long string, x-rayed to check the welds, then lowered in and covered.
  • Testing: the line is filled with water at high pressure. If it holds, it is dried and connected.
  • Maintenance: for decades afterwards someone has to check the coating and repair corrosion.
Fund-manager lens

In EPC, revenue is a function of what can be executed, not of what has been won — and the two diverge routinely. Likhitha's June quarter is the textbook illustration: an order book of ₹1,454 cr, roughly four times annual revenue, and yet revenue down 30.5% because sites were not moving. The three questions that matter are how much of the order book is executable today (right-of-way cleared, drawings approved), who supplies the pipe (free-issue materially reduces working capital and risk), and what the receivable cycle looks like on public-sector clients. A large order book with slow conversion is a liability dressed as an asset.

Why the stock rose while the profit halved

Because in contracting, the market pays for the forward book and treats a weak quarter as a timing problem — provided it believes the work is still there. Likhitha's book at 30 June was ₹1,454 cr against annual revenue of a few hundred crore, and the June award from CPECC Abu Dhabi did something the domestic order flow could not: it proved the company can qualify for international work as a subcontractor to a global prime. That is a re-rating argument. The risk is the mirror image: if the same book is still unconverted a year from now, the multiple compresses and the 2025 experience (−45%) repeats.

Analogy

An EPC order book is a restaurant's reservation list. A long list is good news only if the kitchen can cook. When the kitchen is blocked — no gas, no ingredients, the road outside dug up — the list keeps growing while the tills stay quiet, and eventually the diners leave.

7 · The business calendar

Built around the rain

  • April–June: the strongest construction window — dry ground, new budgets, fresh awards.
  • July–September: the monsoon halts trenching and lowering across most of the country; revenue recognition slows and crews idle.
  • October–December: the post-monsoon push, when the year's execution has to be recovered.
  • January–March: year-end billing, milestone certification and collections; the quarter that decides the reported year.
  • Award cycle: public-sector tenders cluster around budget approvals, so order intake is lumpy and unpredictable.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Eight drivers

  1. The national gas grid programme: a network heading towards 35,000 km, with four new trunk pipelines of about 2,500 km under development.
  2. City gas roll-out: 307 authorised geographical areas, each requiring steel mains, polyethylene networks and CNG stations (see 034).
  3. Refinery and terminal capital expenditure: expansions such as Numaligarh's 3-to-9 MMTPA programme create facility work (see 028, 031).
  4. Right-of-way clearance: the practical determinant of how fast an order book converts.
  5. Order intake and ticket size: lumpy, and a single ₹510 cr award can change the book by a third.
  6. Competitive intensity in bidding: pipeline EPC has low entry barriers at small ticket sizes, which caps margins.
  7. Working capital and receivables: public-sector payment behaviour.
  8. International expansion: Middle East contracts as a subcontractor to global primes — new, and unproven at scale.

What the record shows about the stock

  • The re-rating came first, the execution later. The share rose 109% in 2021 and 31% in 2022 on order intake, then gave back 45% in 2025 when conversion disappointed.
  • Order books do not protect a share price. 2025's fall happened with a healthy book, and 2026's rise happened with falling revenue. What changes is belief in conversion.
  • Scarcity cuts both ways. Being the only listed pure play attracts a premium in a theme-led market and offers no support when the theme fades.

11 · The global picture

A commoditised craft with regional champions

Pipeline construction is a global industry with no technological moat and significant execution skill: the winners are those who can mobilise certified crews at scale and absorb weather and land risk. The Middle East is the most active market, with national oil companies running continuous programmes, and Chinese state contractors such as CPECC frequently taking prime-contractor roles there — which is how an Indian mid-cap ends up with an Abu Dhabi package. For Indian contractors, international work is attractive because payment terms are usually better than domestic public-sector cycles, and dangerous because a bad overseas project can consume years of domestic profit.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • From owner-built to contracted: state gas utilities once built their own lines; competitive tendering created a contractor industry.
  • From steel-only to polyethylene networks: city gas distribution uses plastic mains at low pressure, a different and more repetitive skill than trunk-line welding.
  • From domestic to export services: Indian contractors following global primes into the Gulf, initially as subcontractors.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Horizontal directional drilling: crossing rivers, roads and railways without trenching — the difference between a three-week crossing and a three-month one.
  2. Automated welding and inspection: mechanised welding with automated ultrasonic testing raises kilometres per day and cuts rework.
  3. Smart pigging and integrity management: instrumented devices run through a line to find corrosion, creating recurring inspection work.
  4. Digital project controls: drone survey, GIS-based route management and progress tracking, which matter most where right-of-way is fragmented.
  5. Composite and polyethylene networks: faster to lay in cities, and central to the city gas roll-out.
  6. Hydrogen readiness: specifications for blending hydrogen into gas networks, which will eventually drive re-qualification work on existing lines.

The honest read

The industry behind this section is one of the more certain infrastructure programmes in India: the gas grid and the city gas roll-out are authorised, funded and under construction, and they generate work for a decade. The listed expression of it is a ₹900 cr contractor whose revenue fell 30% last quarter. Those two sentences are both true, and the gap between them is the entire investment problem. The single number that resolves it is the conversion rate — order book turning into revenue — and it should be checked every quarter, because the 2025 experience showed how fast a scarcity premium unwinds when the book stops moving. For exposure to the programme rather than to one contractor's site problems, 033 and 034 are the better vehicles.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. Of the ₹1,454 cr order book, how much is executable today with right-of-way cleared and drawings approved?
  2. What caused the 30.5% revenue decline, project by project, and when does each site restart?
  3. What proportion of contracts are on free-issue pipe versus contractor-procured material?
  4. What is the expected execution schedule and margin profile of the ASAB package, and what currency and payment terms apply?
  5. What are receivable and retention days, and how much is outstanding beyond 180 days?
  6. What is the bid pipeline, and what is the win rate on it?
  7. How many construction spreads are active, and what is the cost of an idle spread per month?
  8. What margin is being bid on new domestic work, given competitive intensity?

Red flags

  • An order book growing faster than revenue for several consecutive quarters.
  • Margin falling while revenue falls — idle cost, not mix.
  • A first overseas contract that expands in scope before the first one is finished.
  • Receivables concentrated in one public-sector client.

18 · Myths and live debates

What you'll hear, and the evidence

ClaimEvidence
"This is the way to play India's gas build-out."It is one ₹900 cr contractor. The programme is real, but the pipeline owners (033) and city gas licensees (034) capture the annuity; the contractor captures a one-time construction margin with execution risk attached.
"A record order book means record revenue is coming."Not on its own. The book was ₹1,454 cr while revenue fell 30.5%. Conversion depends on land and clearances, not on the contract.
"The international contract transforms the company."It is a genuine qualification milestone — a ₹510 cr package for a global prime — but it is a 21-month subcontract, and overseas execution risk is precisely how mid-cap contractors have historically hurt themselves.
"EPC margins expand with scale."In pipeline work they usually do not: entry barriers are low at small ticket sizes and public tenders are price-competitive. The 14–17% band has been remarkably stable.
"The 45% fall in 2025 was an overreaction."It was a de-rating of a scarcity premium on slow conversion. Whether it was wrong is answerable only by the execution rate over the next four quarters.

19 · Glossary

Every term you'll meet

Construction

EPC
Engineering, procurement and construction: a contract to design, buy materials for and build a facility, usually at a fixed price.
Spread
A complete pipeline construction crew with its equipment, working along a section of route. Contractors measure capacity in spreads.
Stringing
Laying individual pipe lengths end to end along the trench before welding.
Trenching
Digging the channel the pipe is laid in.
Lowering-in and backfill
Placing the welded string into the trench and covering it.
Radiographic testing
X-raying each weld to confirm it is sound; every joint is inspected.
Hydrostatic testing
Filling the completed line with water at high pressure to prove it does not leak before commissioning.
Pigging
Running a device through a pipeline to clean it or, when instrumented, to inspect it for corrosion.
Cathodic protection
An electrical technique that stops buried steel corroding.
Coating
The protective layer on line pipe; re-coating ageing lines is a recurring maintenance contract, as in Likhitha's Oil India award.
Horizontal directional drilling (HDD)
Boring beneath a river, road or railway instead of trenching across it.
Tie-in
Connecting a new section to an existing live network.
Commissioning
Handing over a tested, filled and operating line to the owner.
Line pipe
The large-diameter steel pipe used for trunk pipelines.
Polyethylene mains
Plastic piping used at low pressure in city gas networks.
CNG station
A compressed natural gas refuelling outlet, built as part of city gas networks (see 034).

Contracting and commercial

Order book
Contracted work not yet executed (see 029).
Order intake
New contracts won in a period; lumpy in this industry.
Conversion / execution rate
How quickly the order book becomes revenue — the decisive metric here.
Free-issue material
Material supplied by the client, which the contractor installs but does not buy. It lowers working capital and headline revenue together.
Right of use (RoU)
The statutory right to lay and maintain a pipeline across land, acquired with compensation. The commonest cause of delay.
Retention money
A share of each bill held back until commissioning or the end of a defect liability period.
Defect liability period
The window after handover during which the contractor must fix faults.
Mobilisation advance
An upfront payment to move crews and equipment to site, usually recovered from later bills.
Percentage of completion
The accounting basis on which EPC revenue is recognised as work progresses.
Prime contractor and subcontractor
The party holding the client contract, and those it engages beneath it. Likhitha's Abu Dhabi work sits under CPECC as prime.
Bid pipeline
Tenders submitted and awaiting award.
L1 bidder
The lowest bidder in a public tender, usually the winner — which is why margins stay compressed.
Escalation clause
A contract provision adjusting price for input-cost inflation; its absence turns cost rises into losses.
Defect-free kilometre rate
Kilometres completed per day per spread, the operational productivity measure.

Sector and policy

PNGRB
The Petroleum and Natural Gas Regulatory Board, which authorises pipelines and city gas areas.
Geographical area (GA)
A city gas licence territory; 307 have been authorised.
National Gas Grid
The programme to connect the country with a network heading towards 35,000 km.
Infrastructure exclusivity
The protected period during which a city gas licensee alone may build network in its area; PNGRB is opening networks to third-party capital afterwards.
Strategic petroleum reserve
Emergency crude storage; India's first private reserve, of 2.5 million tonnes, was awarded to Megha Engineering.
PM Gati Shakti
The coordinated infrastructure planning platform intended to speed clearances.
PNG and the National PNG Drive
Piped natural gas to homes, and the campaign to accelerate connections — 367,000 gasified since March, with about 400,000 more registered.
