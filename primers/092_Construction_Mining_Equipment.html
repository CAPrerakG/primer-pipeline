Mining Equipment

A contractor buys a crane or a concrete mixer because it earns more on site than it costs to own. A mine buys an underground loader to move ore, and then buys liners and grinding balls every month for as long as it grinds ore. This primer separates the machines that are bought once from the parts that are bought forever, and the rental fleets that own the machines, across nine listed names that the watchlist groups together.

The industry in 60 seconds

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Imagine building a sandcastle with a toy truck, a toy crane and a bucket. You buy the truck and crane once, and they last for years. But the bucket gets cracked from scooping sand and you buy a new one every summer. Some families do not buy a crane at all; they borrow one from a shop that rents cranes and pay by the day. Big builders and mines work the same way. Some companies sell the big machines, some sell the parts that wear out, and some rent the machines to people who need them only for a while.

Say it to a client in one minute

Five of nine watchlist names make up the price basket. ACE is India’s largest maker of mobile pick-and-carry cranes and also sells tower cranes, forklifts, road machines and tractors. Eimco Elecon makes underground mining loaders and drills. TIL makes cranes, reach-stackers and port equipment, and has just bought a gas-compression business. Gujarat Apollo makes crushers and screens and is restarting road equipment, but most of its reported profit is other income. Tega sells mill liners and, since June 2026, grinding media through its acquisition of Molycop, which made it a much larger and more indebted company overnight.

The basket is up 11.7% this year against a 10.7% fall in the Nifty, but that comes from two names, Eimco and ACE. Industry unit sales fell about 2% in FY26, domestic demand fell about 7%, and exports rose about 32%. The useful split is between machines that follow contractors’ order books and financing, and consumables that follow tonnes of ore ground. Tega has just moved most of its business into the second group; the others remain in the first.

Construction and mining machines are long-lived capital goods. A contractor buys one when its expected hours of work, hire rate, financing cost and resale value add up. That makes demand follow project awards, site execution and credit availability, with a lag and with amplification: a small slowdown in projects can halve machine orders, because contractors stop buying before they stop working. Mining consumables behave differently. Mill liners and grinding balls are consumed in proportion to the ore a concentrator processes, so demand tracks mine throughput rather than mine construction.

The engineering tests from the earlier primers carry forward: who specifies the part and whether it wears out (see 078, 078 — Castings); how much value lies in processing rather than steel weight (see 080, 080 — Forgings); and who carries steel cost between quote and delivery (see 081, 081 — Fabrication). 091 — Material Handling Equipment covers the conveyor, crane-hire and overhead-crane names that sit next to this roster (see 091). 090 — Industrial Machinery covers order-to-cash in project equipment, and 088 — Compressors & Engines covers the gas-compression economics that TIL has just bought into.

Read reported profits carefully in this roster. Tega’s June quarter contains one month of Molycop, ₹190.96 crore of one-time acquisition expenses and a large new interest bill. Gujarat Apollo’s other income exceeds its pre-tax profit. ACE earns a material part of its profit from treasury income. TIL’s owner loss differs from its group loss. Machines that earn their keep

A pick-and-carry crane is the workhorse of Indian industrial yards: it lifts a load and drives with it, which a conventional mobile crane cannot do safely. It is bought mostly by small owners who hire it out locally, often on finance. That makes its demand sensitive to diesel prices, hire rates and the cost and availability of equipment loans. On ACE’s July 2026 call, management said hydra cranes were about 60–65% of the market in FY25 and slightly under 60% in FY26, with new-generation cranes rising to slightly over 40%. Hydras moved directly from BS-III to the CEV-V emission standard, raising their price more than that of new-generation cranes. ACE Q1 FY27 earnings call, July 2026

An underground loader is different: it is bought by a mine through a technical tender, it must be approved for use underground, and it needs spares and service for its whole life. Eimco Elecon sells through a distributor, and “compensation to distributors” was ₹8.71 crore in the June quarter, 11.2% of revenue. That line shows that part of the installed-base economics is shared with the channel, a point that matters for anyone valuing its spares income.

Parts that wear out

A mill liner protects the steel shell of a grinding mill from the ore tumbling inside it; grinding media are the balls that do the grinding. Both are destroyed by use. A concentrator that runs continuously consumes them in proportion to tonnes processed, ore hardness and mill design. Customers buy on cost per tonne ground, not price per kilogram, which rewards suppliers who can prove longer wear life or better grinding efficiency. This is the consumable logic of 078: the supplier who designs the wear part for a specific mill owns more of the replacement cycle than one who casts to someone else’s drawing.

The margin pools sit at the two ends of the chain. At the front, a dominant product in a defined niche — ACE in pick-and-carry cranes, Ajax in self-loading mixers — can price ahead of costs and spread fixed costs across high volumes. At the back, consumables and spares earn repeat margins as long as the machines run. The middle — fabrication and assembly of undifferentiated machines — is competitive and cyclical. Rental operators sit outside the chain as customers; their returns come from yield and utilisation on a large, debt-funded gross block.

Market share is unusually concentrated in some niches. Ajax reported that its self-loading concrete mixer share rose to 75.1% in the June quarter, from about 69% a year earlier and 73.5% for FY26. Ajax Q1 FY27 earnings call, August 2026 A leader with three-quarters of a niche can still see falling revenue if the niche shrinks: Ajax said volumes fell sharply in some states, such as Madhya Pradesh, while its share rose.

Fund-manager lens

For each OEM, separate share gains from market growth. Ajax’s share rose while some state markets shrank; ACE’s crane volumes grew 17.25% while industry material-handling units fell. Share is a moat signal; market growth is a cycle signal. The stock price usually rewards the second and is surprised by the first.

The audit

Four exclusions; three sensitivities; no price repairs. Virat Crane is a dairy company; Cranex makes factory overhead cranes and its June review report is qualified (joint-venture equity method not followed, balances not confirmed, inventory register not produced); Sanghvi is a rental operator; Ajax has only 392 sessions of history. All nine series end on 18 September 2026. No basket member has a daily move of −30% or worse or +50% or more in the study window. Eimco has a 91-day gap in 2006 and TIL in 2007–08, both before the July 2011 start; Eimco’s 31-day gap in May 2013 is inside the window and is not filled.

TIL completed a rights issue in 2026 in which 1,20,91,760 shares were issued partly paid at ₹7.50 of ₹10 face value; a partly paid security trades separately. The price series used is TradingView’s adjusted close for the fully paid share and shows no step on the record date, but it is not a shareholder total-return series that values the right. Tega’s history begins only at its December 2021 listing. Gujarat Apollo’s price partly reflects its investments and associates; the page reports the basket with and without it.

Two inclusions need a defence. Gujarat Apollo is kept because crushers and screens are a genuine part of mining and construction equipment, even though in the June quarter other income of ₹7.50 crore exceeded revenue-generating operations that lost money. TIL is kept despite years of losses because excluding weak performers would bias the history upward. Tega is kept although its business is mainly consumables, because mining equipment and mining consumables are bought by the same customers and respond to the same commodity cycle; the sensitivity shows what changes without it.

ACE: volume, price and treasury

ACE’s revenue rose 20.5%; management reported crane, construction and material-handling segment revenue of ₹738.37 crore, up about 22%, with volumes up 17.25%, and agricultural equipment revenue of ₹42.67 crore. The operating margin rose from 14.2% to 15.0% on calibrated price increases and cost control while steel, rubber, oil and freight remained elevated. Other income of ₹54.61 crore is about a third of pre-tax profit; it is mostly treasury income on ACE’s cash. That is real profit, but it is not crane profit and would not grow with crane volumes.

Eimco: steady margins, distributor share

Eimco’s revenue rose 14.7% and its operating margin held at 18.4%. Other income of ₹7.76 crore is more than half the operating proxy; Eimco holds large investments relative to its size. Distributor compensation of ₹8.71 crore is a material cost line and a reminder that the distributor shares in the installed-base economics.

TIL: an acquisition changes the denominator

TIL’s revenue rose 86.2%, but ₹38.52 crore came from Tulip Compression, consolidated from May 2026. Without it, TIL’s own revenue was about ₹78.61 crore, up about 25%. The group moved from a negative to a positive operating proxy, helped by nine reach-stacker deliveries, but finance costs of ₹11.67 crore still exceeded operating earnings of ₹7.00 crore, leaving a loss. Management reported an order book of ₹211 crore and a pipeline of about ₹373 crore. TIL Q1 FY27 earnings call, August 2026

Gujarat Apollo: an equipment company in name

Gujarat Apollo’s revenue was below ₹10 crore and its operating proxy was negative; its reported profit came entirely from ₹7.50 crore of other income. Its March 2026 presentation describes a restart of road-construction equipment after a non-compete with the buyer of its former road business ended, and a three-year target of ₹210 crore of revenue. Gujarat Apollo presentation, March 2026 That is a plan from a very small base; the current filing shows the cost of the restart, not its revenue.

Tega: one month that changes everything

Tega’s legacy business performed well: management said its revenue rose 21% to ₹4.3 billion and EBITDA 42% to ₹1 billion, a 22.1% margin, with a legacy order book of ₹12.3 billion. Molycop contributed ₹12.9 billion of revenue in its first month with EBITDA before one-time costs of ₹1.6 billion, about 13%. One-time acquisition and integration costs were about ₹1.95 billion on the call; the filing shows ₹190.955 crore of acquisition expenses in other expenses. Group debt was ₹112 billion including about ₹26 billion of redeemable preference shares, and finance costs jumped to ₹116.71 crore in the quarter. Tega Q1 FY27 earnings call, August 2026 Tega is now a global grinding-media company with a liner business attached, and its margin and leverage must be read on that basis.

The honest read

Only ACE and Eimco show rising operating earnings from their own machines this quarter. Ajax held its share but not its margin. TIL’s improvement is real but partly bought. Gujarat Apollo’s profit is other income. Tega’s legacy business is strong but its group numbers now describe a different, much larger and more leveraged company.

The scale change is the first point. If June is representative, Molycop alone adds revenue of roughly ₹1,300 crore a month, against legacy Tega revenue of about ₹430 crore a quarter. A full quarter of the combined group would be about ten times Tega’s former quarterly revenue. The margin change is the second: grinding media at about 13% EBITDA dilutes a liner business earning 22%, so the group margin will fall even if both parts perform. Synergies of USD 20 million a year, against combined annual revenue of roughly USD 1.8–1.9 billion at the June run-rate, would add about one percentage point to the combined margin.

The balance sheet is the third. Group debt of about ₹11,200 crore, including preference capital, compares with Tega’s FY26 operating cash of ₹350 crore. June-quarter finance costs were ₹116.71 crore, more than twice the operating proxy after one-time costs. The investment case now rests on Molycop’s cash generation paying down that debt — the call reported a large reduction in Molycop’s net debt at closing through refinancing and new equity — and on the purchase-price allocation not producing impairments.

The minority loss is the fourth. Group loss of ₹108.25 crore was split ₹86.19 crore to Tega’s owners and ₹22.06 crore to non-controlling holders, which shows that Tega does not own all of the acquisition vehicle. Owner EPS must be computed on owner profit; group EBITDA overstates what belongs to Tega’s shareholders by the minority share.

Fund-manager lens

Tega has moved from a high-margin, cash-generative niche in India’s listed universe to a leveraged global consumables platform. Re-underwrite it from scratch: Molycop’s full-year cash flow, interest cost, minority share and synergy delivery. The legacy business’s strength is real but is now the smaller part of the story.

Quarter-to-year bridges

ACE’s four quarters reconcile to the year within integer-lakh rounding at group level; annual group profit of ₹415.10 crore differs from owner profit of ₹415.09 crore by a sub-lakh minority. Eimco has no subsidiaries, joint ventures or associates as of June 2026 according to its own filing, which makes its standalone result the whole company. TIL’s FY26 consolidated operating proxy was about ₹4.44 crore on revenue of ₹323.25 crore (₹ lakh: −3,506 + 4,626 + 735 − 1,411), so finance costs of ₹46.26 crore were ten times operating earnings. Gujarat Apollo’s associate share must be added below the PAT line in both periods to reproduce EPS. Tega’s annual numbers are pre-acquisition; its June quarter is not comparable with any earlier period.

These are presentation traps, not accounting irregularities. The risk is mechanical: a screen that takes the PAT line for Gujarat Apollo, group loss for Tega or a rights-adjusted comparative for TIL will produce per-share numbers that do not match the company’s own EPS.

The cash pattern sorts the business models cleanly. A dominant OEM selling through dealers who arrange customer finance converts profit to cash, as ACE did. A mining OEM selling to PSU miners on tender terms carries more working capital, as Eimco did. A loss-making OEM rebuilding its order book consumes cash, as TIL did, and needs equity to fund the gap. A rental fleet generates strong operating cash because depreciation is a large non-cash cost, but returns depend on what it spends on new cranes: Sanghvi’s gross block is about ₹3,300 crore for 492 cranes, and its India and Botswana fleet ran at about 86% utilisation at a yield of 2.29%, against 86% and 4.10% in the Gulf. Sanghvi Q1 FY27 earnings call, August 2026

Consumables convert well once the customer base is stable: Tega’s legacy business produced operating cash two and a half times its profit in FY26. The open question is Molycop. Grinding media is a large-volume, steel-intensive product sold on long contracts, and its working capital and the acquisition debt will set the group’s cash profile from FY27.

The test to apply

For OEMs, compare three-year operating cash with profit and watch dealer inventory. For rental fleets, compare operating cash with replacement and growth capex and check yield on gross block. For Tega, ask for Molycop’s standalone cash conversion and the debt-reduction plan, and judge the deal on cash after interest, not on EBITDA.

The buyer’s arithmetic: buy, rent or wait

Every machine in this primer is sold to someone who must earn more from it than it costs to own. Understanding that customer arithmetic explains why equipment demand swings harder than construction activity, why financing matters as much as projects, and why rental fleets grow when owners hesitate.

What a crane-hire fleet actually earns

Sanghvi Movers is the clearest disclosed example of the buyer’s economics. On its August 2026 call it reported a fleet of 492 cranes with a gross block of about ₹3,300 crore. Its India and Botswana fleet ran at about 86% utilisation at a yield of 2.29%; the Gulf fleet ran at the same utilisation at a yield of 4.10%. Crane rental contributed about 60% of June-quarter revenue, renewable engineering and construction 37% and project EPC 3%. Sanghvi Q1 FY27 call

The figures can be cross-checked. Sixty per cent of June-quarter revenue of ₹379.67 crore is about ₹228 crore, or about ₹911 crore a year. Divided by a ₹3,300 crore gross block, that is about 27.6% a year — almost exactly twelve times 2.29%. So the yield is best read as a monthly rental return on the original cost of the fleet, which is how Indian crane-hire companies conventionally quote it. On that basis a crane in India earns back roughly a quarter of its cost in rent each year before operating costs, interest and depreciation; in the Gulf, nearly half.

That arithmetic is what decides whether a crane maker’s customer buys. A fleet owner adds cranes when utilisation is high and yields are rising, and the call said Indian yields improved during the quarter. Sanghvi’s FY27 capex pool of ₹652 crore, with roughly ₹190 crore planned for India and ₹200 crore for Saudi Arabia according to an analyst’s question that management did not dispute, is demand for crane makers — though heavy crawler and truck cranes are mostly imported or made by global OEMs, not by the listed Indian companies here. Its gross debt-to-equity was 0.54 times against a self-imposed ceiling of 0.72 times, and group return on capital was about 16% at March 2026.

Collection matters as much as yield. Sanghvi reported group receivable days of 116, with crane rental at 124 days, renewable E&C at 98 and the Gulf business at 201. A fleet that earns a high yield but waits four to seven months to be paid needs more debt than its yield suggests. The same pressure runs down the chain: contractors who are paid late by project owners delay paying hire bills and defer machine purchases, which is exactly the mechanism ICEMA cited for FY26.

The small buyer’s decision

Most hydra cranes and self-loading mixers are bought by small owners on loans. Their decision depends on four numbers: the machine’s price, the hire rate or work it can earn, the loan’s rate and tenure, and what the machine will fetch second-hand. The CEV-V transition raised the first number by double digits in percentage terms without changing the second. That alone explains much of the FY26 domestic slowdown: some buyers waited for hire rates to catch up, some bought used machines, and some rented instead. ACE’s management said hydra prices rose more than new-generation crane prices because hydras jumped two emission stages at once; that pushed some demand toward new-generation cranes, whose share rose to slightly over 40%.

The same arithmetic explains why rental grows in uncertain years. A contractor unsure of the next project can hire a crane for the job rather than commit to a loan. That supports rental yields and utilisation, which eventually brings fleet owners back to the OEMs as buyers. The lag between a rise in rental utilisation and a rise in OEM orders is a useful leading indicator, but it is not disclosed systematically and should be tracked through company calls.

Mines buy differently

A mine buys an underground loader through a technical tender evaluated on capability, availability and lifetime cost, and buys liners and media on cost per tonne processed. Neither decision depends on retail finance. That is why Eimco’s demand follows mine-development plans and Tega’s follows ore throughput, and why both held up in FY26 when financed retail construction demand fell. The trade-off is concentration: a mining OEM can depend on a handful of large customers and their tender calendars.

How to use the buyer’s arithmetic

When rental utilisation and yields rise while OEM retail is flat, expect OEM orders to follow. When financing tightens or a regulation lifts machine prices, expect retail to fall faster than construction activity. And when a mining customer’s throughput rises, expect consumables to respond before equipment.

The critical constraint for Indian construction-equipment OEMs in FY26 was the emission transition. CEV Stage V norms took effect from 1 January 2025 for construction equipment, tightening particulate limits and adding particle-number limits for engines from 19 kW to 560 kW. ICRA estimated that the changes could raise equipment prices by 12–15%, passed on over 12–18 months. Steel is the next constraint. A crane maker selling through dealers can reprice within a quarter; ACE described “calibrated pricing actions” in its call. April and the April–July window

April beat the Nifty in 13 of 15 years by an average of 5.7 points; the failures were 2019 (−4.7) and 2025 (−1.7). The largest April gains were 2024 at 26.1 points, 2014 at 14.6 and 2020 at 11.4. A plausible explanation is that fiscal-year results and the start of the government capex year in April draw attention to the sector before the monsoon slows activity; it is consistent with the data but not tested here.

Three cycles

The first cycle ran from the 2013 trough through the 2014–2017 recovery: the basket more than doubled in 2014 and again in 2017. The global industry and India’s place

The global construction and mining equipment industry is led by Caterpillar, Komatsu, Volvo, Hitachi, Liebherr, Sany, XCMG and others, most of which manufacture in India. Caterpillar reported record full-year 2025 sales and revenues of about $67.6 billion. Caterpillar full-year 2025 results In Indian earthmoving, the largest players are subsidiaries of global groups or JCB, none of which is in this roster. The listed Indian companies are niche leaders: ACE in pick-and-carry cranes, Ajax in self-loading mixers, Eimco in certain underground mining machines. The roster is therefore a sample of niches, not a proxy for the Indian equipment market.

India’s place in the global market rose with exports: ICEMA reported that exports grew about 32% in FY26 to record levels. That includes the Indian factories of global OEMs exporting to their networks, as well as Indian companies. Ajax made its first exports in the June 2026 quarter; ACE says its KATO joint venture should support export-ready heavy cranes over the medium term.

Tega is the exception: with Molycop it becomes one of the world’s largest suppliers of grinding media and mill liners, with operations across the Americas, Australia and Africa. Its revenue is now overwhelmingly global and driven by gold, copper and iron-ore mining, not Indian construction. The company described Molycop’s exposure to gold and copper end markets as a strength. For an Indian investor, Tega has become a global mining-consumables company listed in India.

Global consumables competition is concentrated among a few groups making liners, media and mill equipment. Tega’s legacy model — engineered rubber and composite liners designed for each mill — earns higher margins than commodity grinding media; the June quarter shows legacy EBITDA margins of 22.1% against about 13% at Molycop. The combined group’s margin will therefore fall in percentage terms even if each part performs to plan.

Policy and regulation

Emission norms. CEV Stage V applies to construction equipment from 1 January 2025, with associated safety and noise requirements. Its price effect dominated FY26 retail demand, as ACE and Ajax both described on their calls. Any future norm step should be expected to create a pre-buy and a lull.

Infrastructure spending. Demand rests on government capital expenditure in roads, railways, water and urban infrastructure, and on its execution. ICEMA explicitly attributed FY26 weakness to slower execution and contractor payment delays rather than to budget cuts. The policy variable to watch is therefore project execution and payment discipline, not headline allocations.

Trade remedies. The DGTR issued final findings on 19 September 2025 in an anti-dumping investigation into certain cranes from China, covering mobile crawler and truck cranes; it was initiated on 30 September 2024. DGTR case page, certain cranes from China The product scope overlaps the heavy-crane segment that ACE’s KATO JV is entering. Whether the finance ministry had notified a duty by the data date is not established here; until it is, the recommendation should not be treated as a price-support catalyst.

Mining policy. Coal and mineral auctions, underground mining approvals and mine-safety equipment rules drive Eimco’s market. Coal India’s FY26 production of 768.19 million tonnes, slightly below FY25, is discussed in 091. Tega’s demand is driven by global mining policy and metal prices more than Indian rules.

Withdrawn machinery order. The 2024 machinery omnibus technical regulation was withdrawn in January 2026, as recorded in 089 — Machine Tools; its former timetable should not be presented as a pending requirement for these companies.

Questions to ask management

Each question asks for a figure that can be checked against the next filing.

What share of revenue is spares, service and consumables?

Split revenue between new machines, spares, service and wear parts, and show the trend. Explain who supplies spares to your installed base and what share you capture.

How much of growth is price and how much volume?

Separate emission-norm pass-through from underlying price and from volume, by product family.

What is dealer inventory? (ACE, Ajax)

Give dealer stock in weeks of retail, by product, and how it compares with a year ago.

How does the KATO JV work? (ACE)

State current ownership, KATO’s pending contribution, products, localisation timeline, royalty terms and how the JV will be consolidated.

How much of profit is treasury income? (ACE, Eimco)

Split other income into interest, dividends and fair-value gains, and explain capital-allocation plans for the cash.

What does the distributor earn? (Eimco)

Explain the distributor agreement, compensation basis, related-party status and who owns the spares relationship with the miner.

When will TIL’s own operations cover interest?

Give standalone operating earnings, finance cost, rights-issue proceeds and their use, and the deferred-tax-asset recovery plan.

What will Molycop earn after interest? (Tega)

Give Molycop’s full-year revenue, EBITDA, capex and working capital, the acquisition debt’s cost and maturity, preference-share terms, and the deleveraging plan.

How are steel costs passed through in grinding-media contracts? (Tega)

Describe the formulas, lag and share of volume covered.

What is the road-equipment restart costing? (Gujarat Apollo)

Give revenue, orders and losses for the restarted business separately from investment income and associates.

What would make you cut production?

Describe the order, dealer-stock and financing conditions under which you would slow output.

The central debate is how much of this group’s value comes from niches that compound regardless of the cycle — ACE’s cranes, Ajax’s mixers, Tega’s liners — and how much from the cycle itself. The decision rule

Prefer niche leaders that convert profit to cash and do not depend on other income. In this roster that describes ACE most clearly. Treat Tega as a separate global consumables thesis with acquisition risk, and require TIL and Gujarat Apollo to show operating earnings before giving them the benefit of the cycle.

Glossary: find a term

Machines and products

Pick-and-carry crane
A mobile crane that can lift a load and drive with it; called a “hydra” in India.
New-generation crane
Industry term for modern higher-capacity mobile cranes replacing basic hydras.
Tower crane
A fixed crane with a tall mast used on high-rise and large construction sites.
Truck crane
A crane mounted on a truck chassis for road travel between sites.
Crawler crane
A heavy crane on tracks for large lifts at project sites.
Rough-terrain crane
A crane built for unpaved sites with large tyres and four-wheel drive.
Reach-stacker
A vehicle that lifts and stacks shipping containers at ports and depots.
Self-loading concrete mixer (SLCM)
A machine that loads materials, mixes concrete and delivers it on site.
Batching plant
A fixed plant that weighs and mixes concrete ingredients.
Crusher
A machine that breaks rock into smaller pieces; jaw, cone and impact types.
Vibrating screen
Equipment that sorts crushed material by size.
Load-haul-dumper (LHD)
An underground loader that scoops, carries and dumps ore.
Side-dump loader
A compact underground loader that tips its bucket sideways in narrow tunnels.
Mill liner
A replaceable lining that protects a grinding mill’s shell and lifts the charge.
Grinding media
Steel balls or rods that grind ore inside a mill; consumed continuously.
Earthmoving equipment
Excavators, backhoe loaders, loaders and similar machines; about 71% of Indian unit sales.
Asphalt mixing plant
A plant that heats and mixes aggregate and bitumen for road surfacing.
Gas compression package
A skid-mounted compressor system for gas pipelines and fields; Tulip’s business.

Industry, regulation and operations

ICEMA
Indian Construction Equipment Manufacturers’ Association, which publishes unit sales data.
CEV Stage V
Construction-equipment vehicle emission norms in force from 1 January 2025.
BS-III / BS-IV
Earlier Indian emission stages; hydras moved directly from BS-III to CEV-V.
Particle number limit
A cap on the count of exhaust particles, introduced under CEV-V for many engine sizes.
OEM
Original equipment manufacturer: the company that designs and sells the machine.
Dealer inventory
Machines held by dealers awaiting retail sale; high stock can hide weak demand.
Distributor compensation
Payments to a distributor for sales and service; a material cost at Eimco.
Installed base
Machines already in use; the market for spares and service.
Utilisation (rental)
Share of fleet capacity on hire in a period.
Rental yield
Rental revenue relative to the fleet’s gross block; Indian crane-hire companies usually quote it per month.
Gross block
The original cost of fixed assets before depreciation; for a rental fleet, its capital base.
Mobilisation
Moving and assembling equipment at a site before work begins.
Concentrator
A mineral-processing plant that crushes and grinds ore to separate the valuable mineral.
Relining
Replacing worn mill liners, usually during a planned shutdown.
Anti-dumping duty
A tariff on imports sold below fair value, recommended by DGTR and notified by the finance ministry.
Pre-buy
Purchases brought forward ahead of a price rise or regulatory deadline.

Financial statements and shareholder claims

Other income
Income outside operations: interest, dividends, investment gains, asset-sale gains.
Operating proxy
This page’s bridge: pre-tax profit plus finance cost and depreciation, less other income, exceptional items and JV/associate share.
Treasury income
Returns on surplus cash and investments.
Associate share
The investor’s share of an associate’s profit, reported in one line.
Owner PAT
Profit attributable to the parent’s shareholders.
Non-controlling interest
Outside shareholders’ share of a subsidiary’s profit or loss.
Basic EPS
Owner profit divided by weighted shares in issue.
Weighted shares
Shares averaged over the period; differ from closing shares after issues.
ESOP trust shares
Shares held by an employee trust, deducted when computing EPS.
Rights issue
New shares offered to existing holders; can be partly paid.
Partly paid share
A share on which only part of the issue price has been called.
Purchase-price allocation
Assigning an acquisition’s cost to assets, liabilities, intangibles and goodwill.
Goodwill
The excess of acquisition cost over identifiable net assets.
Redeemable preference shares
Fixed-dividend shares that must be repaid; often counted with debt.
Acquisition expenses
One-time costs of completing and integrating an acquisition.
Deferred-tax asset
Future tax savings recognised on the expectation of taxable profits.
Qualified review
An auditor’s limited review report with specific reservations.
Operating cash flow
Cash from operations after working-capital changes.

Price-study vocabulary

Equal weighting
Each available member gets the same weight each month.
Relative return
Basket return minus Nifty return, in percentage points.
Hit rate
Share of periods with positive relative return; not a forecast.
Median
The middle observation, less sensitive to extremes.
Monthly clipping
Capping each member’s monthly return at ±50% before averaging.
Sensitivity test
Recomputing the basket without one member.
Window drawdown
Distance from the highest close in a stated window.
Survivorship bias
Distortion from studying only companies present today.

Check your understanding

Why is Virat Crane excluded?

It is a dairy company; “Crane” is a brand name. Its data vendor also tagged it as tobacco.

Why is Tega’s June quarter not comparable with last year?

It includes one month of Molycop (₹1,291.64 crore of ₹1,723.44 crore revenue), ₹190.96 crore of acquisition costs and new acquisition debt.

Where does Gujarat Apollo’s profit come from?

From ₹7.50 crore of other income; its operating proxy was −₹3.44 crore.

Did TIL’s revenue really grow 86%?

Partly. ₹38.52 crore came from Tulip, acquired in May; TIL’s own revenue rose about 25%.

Why did industry sales fall in FY26 while ACE grew?

Domestic units fell about 7% on slower execution and CEV-V price increases; ACE gained volume as a niche leader and exports rose.

Why is Sanghvi excluded but discussed?

It rents cranes; its economics are yield and utilisation on a debt-funded fleet. It is a customer and a demand indicator for crane makers.

Sources and method

Primary result filings govern revenue, other income, profit, EPS and capital. Annual reports govern cash flows, share counts and business descriptions. Earnings calls and presentations supply management statements and plans, dated and attributed. Industry data are from ICEMA releases as published or reported. All evidence is dated no later than 18 September 2026; Tega announcements after that date are excluded.

Units: ACE, Eimco, Gujarat Apollo, TIL and Sanghvi file in ₹ lakh; Ajax and Tega in ₹ million. All are converted to crore. Where text extraction was ambiguous, the PDF page was rendered and read visually (TIL’s consolidated statement, Ajax’s statement). The five price series were checked for the fixed endpoint; sensitivities and member returns are reproduced by audit_092.py after compute_stats.py 092. The research package that preceded this page is preserved in the repository’s pilot folder with its source manifest.
