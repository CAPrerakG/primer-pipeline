Ferro Alloys

Steel is mostly iron, but the few percent that is not iron decides what the steel can do. Manganese makes it tough, silicon removes oxygen, chromium makes it stainless. Those elements are delivered to the furnace as ferro alloys — lumps of iron combined with manganese, silicon or chromium, made by melting ore with coke in an electric furnace that draws as much power as a small town. That is the key to the industry: a ferro alloy plant is really a way of selling electricity in solid form. This primer explains the products, the cost structure, why captive power and captive mines decide who survives a downturn, and why chrome and manganese have moved in opposite directions in 2026.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Electricity, ore and a spread

Explain it to a 10-year-old

If you bake a cake with only flour it tastes of nothing. Steel is the same: iron alone is soft and breaks. So steel makers add small amounts of other metals — manganese to make it tough, chromium to stop it rusting. Those metals are hard to add on their own, so a different factory melts their ore with coal in a giant electric oven and pours out lumps that are half iron and half the other metal. Those lumps are ferro alloys. The oven uses an enormous amount of electricity, which is why these factories are usually built next to their own power plant.

Say it to a client in one minute

A commodity conversion business where power and ore decide the winners, now split between a strong chrome market and a weak manganese one.

  • Chrome: IMFA's Q1 FY27 revenue rose 50% to ₹960 cr and profit 108% to ₹192 cr at a 29.3% EBITDA margin; realisations rose 26% and output topped 80,000 tonnes in a quarter for the first time.
  • Why: South Africa, long the world's ferrochrome workshop, has been closing smelters as power tariffs rose more than 900% since 2008; its 2026 output may be just 1–1.5 mt.
  • Manganese alloys: weak. Maithan's revenue fell 15% to ₹539 cr, and most of its reported profit is investment income, not operations.
  • Structure: the biggest Indian producers — Shyam Metalics and Sarda Energy — are integrated steel groups; the pure plays are small.
  • Cycle: the basket rose 156% in 2021 when China cut power to its smelters, and fell 51% in 2018 and 47% in 2013.

Roughly ten to fifteen kilograms of manganese alloy go into every tonne of ordinary steel, and stainless steel is about 16–18% chromium, delivered as ferrochrome. So alloy demand rises and falls with steel output, but the two chains have different geographies: manganese alloys follow construction steel, while ferrochrome follows stainless steel, which is concentrated in China, Indonesia and Europe.

The chain in one breath

  • Miners supply manganese and chrome ore.
  • Power plants supply the electricity that does the real work.
  • Smelters melt ore with coke and pour out alloy.
  • Steel mills add it to set the steel's properties.
  • Slag and gas are sold or reused.
Fund-manager lens

The critical question for any ferro alloy producer is what it pays for power and ore. A producer buying grid power at ₹7 a unit and imported ore competes against one running captive coal or hydro power at ₹3 and mining its own chrome — and in a downturn only the second survives. IMFA has both captive mines and captive power, which is why it earns a 29% margin in a market where others are shutting furnaces. When judging any company here, find the power cost per tonne before looking at anything else.

5 · How money is made

The conversion spread

Cost lineShare of costWhat decides it
OreThe largest raw-material costManganese and chrome ore prices; captive mines remove the risk
PowerTypically a third to a half of total costCaptive thermal or hydro versus grid tariffs and electricity duty
ReductantsCoke, coal and charcoalCoking coal prices
Electrodes and consumablesSmall but unavoidableGraphite electrode prices
Conversion and labourModestFurnace efficiency and recovery rates

The product is a commodity with a published price, so the only variable a producer controls is cost. That is why the industry's history is a series of shake-outs: when alloy prices fall, high-cost smelters stop, supply shrinks, prices recover, and the low-cost producers earn extraordinary margins in between. IMFA's 29.3% EBITDA margin in Q1 FY27 and the closure of South African capacity are two sides of the same cycle.

Why power matters more than anything

A submerged arc furnace runs continuously for years and consumes several thousand units of electricity per tonne of alloy — ferro silicon is the most power-hungry, ferrochrome and manganese alloys less so but still enormous. A one-rupee change in the cost of a unit of electricity can move the cost of a tonne of alloy by several thousand rupees. This is why the industry clusters where power is cheap: hydro in the north-east and Bhutan, captive coal in Odisha and Chhattisgarh, and, historically, cheap coal power in South Africa — until that advantage disappeared.

A warning about reported profits

Maithan Alloys shows why the income statement needs reading carefully. In Q1 FY27 it reported a profit of ₹395 cr on revenue of ₹539 cr. No smelter earns that: the bulk came from non-operating income on the company's large investment portfolio, built during the good years. The operating business was weak, with revenue down 15% on soft silico-manganese prices. Always separate the smelting result from the treasury result.

5b · The chrome story

South Africa's retreat and India's opportunity

For decades South Africa mined most of the world's chrome ore and smelted much of it into ferrochrome, using cheap coal power. Both halves of that model have broken. Eskom's electricity tariffs have risen more than 900% since 2008, and smelter after smelter has closed; South African ferrochrome output may be only 1–1.5 million tonnes in 2026. Its ore, meanwhile, is increasingly shipped raw to China, whose smelters now produce about 800,000–850,000 tonnes a month, up from 600,000–700,000 in early 2025.

For India this is an opening: low-cost chrome ore in Odisha, captive power, and a shrinking competitor. The risk is that China's smelters, which buy ore rather than alloy, set the price and can expand faster than anyone. Indian producers are price takers in a market whose marginal cost is Chinese, not Indian.

7 · The business calendar

Contracts, monsoon and Chinese holidays

  • January–March: steel mills destock before the fiscal year-end; Chinese New Year slows Asian buying; alloy prices often drift.
  • April–June: new-year contracts, pre-monsoon steel production and restocking; historically the best months for the stocks.
  • July–September: the monsoon slows construction steel demand; ore movement is harder.
  • October–December: post-monsoon construction, the festival-season steel push and export contract negotiations for the next year.
  • Power tariff cycle: state tariff orders usually take effect from April, resetting the cost base for grid-connected smelters.

The calendar reinforces the price cycle rather than creating it. April and October — the two restocking windows — have been the strongest months for these stocks, and February–March, the destocking window, the weakest.

Control: small caps as a whole (Nifty Smallcap 100) beat the Nifty in April in 11 of 15 years, by an average of 3.0 points, and lag in January–February. Part of any April strength is this market-wide effect. After removing it, this industry's own April edge is about +5.5 points.

Reading it: the basket is the four listed pure-play alloy makers — IMFA, Maithan, Visa Chrome and Shyam Century — not the integrated steel groups that also make alloys. February–March has beaten the Nifty in only 3 of 15 years (median −7.6 points) and March alone in 4 of 15. April has worked in 11 of 15 (average +8.5) and October in 9 of 15. October–December has worked in 9 of 14 (median +5.0). The pattern follows the steel mills' buying year: contracts and prices reset before and after the monsoon, while the first quarter of the calendar year combines the Chinese New Year lull with year-end destocking.

A tendency, not a rule: small samples, survivorship bias (only names still listed) and equal weighting. Use it for timing context, not as a signal.

The exceptions, and why

  • February–March worked only in 2022 (+15.5), 2019 (+14.4) and 2014 — moments when alloy prices were turning up. Its worst years were 2025 (−21.4), 2018 (−15.2) and 2023 (−13.8).
  • April–July failed in 2013 (−34.8), 2018 (−30.3), 2019 (−29.1) and 2022 (−17.0), and produced +120.5 in 2021 and +57.4 in 2016 — the two upcycles.
  • October–December has worked in 9 of 14 years (median +5.0), the post-monsoon restocking window.
How to use this

The seasonality is consistent with the mining and steel calendar in 064 and 066, which makes it more believable than a single industry's pattern. But the amplitude here comes from the price cycle: in 2021 April–July alone returned 120 points more than the Nifty. Use the calendar to time entries within a view on alloy prices and power costs.

9 · What moves margins

Eight drivers

  1. Alloy prices. Set in China and by the marginal smelter's cost.
  2. Power cost. Captive versus grid; hydro versus coal; state tariffs.
  3. Ore cost. Captive mines versus imports.
  4. Coke prices. The other big input.
  5. Steel output. India's mills set domestic volume.
  6. Stainless demand. Drives ferrochrome specifically.
  7. Global capacity closures. South Africa's retreat is the current swing factor.
  8. Currency and freight. Exports and imported inputs both move with the rupee.

The anatomy of the cycles

  1. The cost squeeze (2012–2013): rising power tariffs and falling alloy prices; the basket lost 48% in 2013.
  2. The recovery (2014–2017): Chinese supply-side cuts lifted alloy prices; IMFA rose 315% in 2016 and Maithan 175% in 2017.
  3. The bust (2018–2019): prices fell, costs rose, and small caps were sold; two years of −51% and −32%.
  4. The energy spike (2020–2021): China's power cuts and the global energy crisis produced the industry's best year ever, +156%.
  5. The split (2022–2026): chrome held up on South African closures while manganese alloys stayed weak; IMFA up in four of the last five years, Maithan and Shyam Century down.

Patterns that repeat

  • Margins are made in shortages. The industry's best years follow supply closures, not demand booms.
  • Power policy is the quiet driver. South Africa's tariff rises created India's opportunity; Indian tariff changes could reverse it.
  • Balance sheets decide survival. Debt-free producers such as Maithan and IMFA live through the down years that close others.

11 · The global picture

China smelts, South Africa retreats, India fills gaps

CountryPositionDirection
ChinaThe largest producer and consumer of ferro alloys, and of stainless steelExpanding smelting; imports ore directly

KazakhstanLow-cost integrated ferrochromeStable, competitive

IndonesiaFast-growing stainless capacityDemand for ferrochrome and nickel alloys
EuropeShrinking smelting under high energy pricesImport dependent; carbon border rules apply from 2026

Two forces shape the trade. The first is energy: ferro alloys migrate to wherever power is cheapest, which over twenty years has meant China, Kazakhstan and, in ferrochrome, increasingly Indonesia and India rather than South Africa or Europe. The second is carbon: the European Union's carbon border mechanism begins charging importers of iron and steel products in 2026, which will raise the cost of exporting energy-intensive materials into Europe and give an advantage to producers with low-carbon power — such as the hydro-powered smelters of the Indian north-east and Bhutan.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Bigger, closed furnaces. Higher capacity with gas recovery, which cuts power use per tonne.
  2. Energy recovery. Furnace off-gas is burned to generate power, improving the cost structure.
  3. Renewables and hydro. Cheap round-the-clock renewable power is the industry's long-term prize.
  4. Ore beneficiation and pelletising. Using lower-grade chrome and manganese fines.
  5. Slag valorisation. Selling slag to cement and construction instead of dumping it.
  6. Carbon accounting. Export markets increasingly ask for the carbon intensity of each tonne.

What is priced in

IMFA, 24% below its April high despite a doubling of profit, is priced for realisations to fall back — the market treats a 26% jump in chrome prices as a cyclical gift, not a trend. That is a defensible view: the price is set by Chinese smelters, and their capacity is growing. The manganese-alloy names are priced for continued weakness; Maithan's value rests as much on its investment portfolio as on its furnaces. The one structural change worth watching is South Africa's withdrawal from smelting, which, if it continues, leaves a long-term gap that Indian and Indonesian producers can fill.

The honest read

This is a classic conversion business: no pricing power, no brand, and margins that swing from 5% to 30% with the cycle. It rewards two things only — the lowest cost per tonne and a balance sheet strong enough to survive the bad years. IMFA has both in chrome; Maithan has the balance sheet but a weak market. Buying either at the top of the margin cycle has been the industry's most reliable way to lose money.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What was the realisation per tonne, and how much of the change was price versus mix?
  2. What is the power cost per unit, and what share of it is captive?
  3. What share of ore is captive, and at what cost against market prices?
  4. What is the conversion cost per tonne, and how has it moved?
  5. How much of reported profit is operating and how much is treasury income?
  6. What is the export share, and how will the EU carbon border mechanism affect it?
  7. What is the utilisation rate, and what would make you idle a furnace?
  8. What capacity is being added, when does it start, and at what capital cost per tonne?
  9. How do you see Chinese and South African supply developing?
  10. What is the capital-allocation plan for the cash generated in this upcycle?

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. What is a ferro alloy, and why is it needed?

An alloy of iron with manganese, silicon or chromium, added to steel to make it tough, clean or stainless.

2. Why is power the defining cost?

Smelting happens in an electric furnace that uses thousands of units of electricity per tonne; power is typically a third to a half of cost.

3. What did IMFA report in Q1 FY27?

Revenue ₹960 cr (+50%), EBITDA ₹280 cr at 29.3%, profit ₹192 cr (+108%), with realisations up 26%.

4. Why is ferrochrome tight?

South African smelters have been closing as power tariffs rose over 900% since 2008; 2026 output may be 1–1.5 mt.

5. What should you check in Maithan's profit?

How much is operating: it reported ₹395 cr of profit on ₹539 cr of revenue, mostly non-operating income.

6. Which listed companies are the largest Indian alloy producers?

Shyam Metalics and Sarda Energy — but both are integrated steel groups, so they are excluded from this basket.

7. Which member is misclassified, and what does it actually make?

Raghav Productivity Enhancers, which makes quartz ramming mass for furnace linings.

8. What happened in 2021?

China's power cuts and the global energy crisis sent alloy prices to records; the basket rose 156%.

9. Which months have worked best and worst?

April (11 of 15) and October (9 of 15) best; February–March worst, with only 3 wins in 15 years.

10. Why does the EU's carbon border mechanism matter here?

It charges importers for embedded carbon from 2026, favouring producers using low-carbon power such as hydro.
