A factory runs on loops: a sensor measures, a controller decides, an actuator acts, software records. The companies in this watchlist sit at different points on that loop — or nowhere near it. This primer separates the owners of control and measurement technology from the integrators who assemble other people’s robots, the panel builders who distribute power, and the IT, distribution and dormant companies that only share the word “automation”.

The industry in 60 seconds

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Think of a room with an air-conditioner. A thermometer measures how hot the room is. A little computer compares that to the temperature you want. If it is too hot, it tells the cooler to blow harder. That is automation: measure, decide, act. A factory does the same thing thousands of times a second with heat, pressure, speed and position. Some companies make the thermometers. Some make the little computers. Some put robots, wires and computers together so a whole factory line works. And some companies just have “automation” in their name but do something else entirely.

Say it to a client in one minute

Five of fourteen watchlist names form the price basket. Honeywell Automation India is the anchor: process control and building systems, plus engineering exports to its parent; it earned a 14.3% operating margin in the June quarter and holds large cash. Rishabh Instruments makes electrical measuring instruments in India and owns Poland’s Lumel, which adds aluminium die casting. Marine Electricals builds electrical power-distribution panels and marine automation, and is growing fast on data-centre projects. Affordable Robotic and Aplab are small and currently loss-making: a robot-welding and parking integrator, and a test-and-measurement and power-electronics maker.

The basket is up 31.8% this year against a 10.7% fall in the Nifty, but almost all of that is Rishabh and Marine; the three pure automation and instrument names together are up only 2.3%. The investment distinction is between companies that own the measurement or control technology, those that integrate bought-in robots and controllers into lines, and those that distribute or install electrical equipment. Margins, cash and durability differ sharply between the three.

Factory automation has three layers. The field layer measures and acts: sensors, transmitters, analysers, valves, motors and robots. The control layer decides: programmable logic controllers (PLCs), distributed control systems (DCS) and safety systems. The information layer supervises and records: SCADA, manufacturing execution systems and plant historians. Around those layers sit integrators, who design and commission complete systems, and service teams who calibrate, maintain and upgrade them. Value accrues most reliably to whoever owns the control platform installed at a plant, because changing it is expensive and risky; it accrues least to whoever resells hardware with little engineering attached.

Carry the Engineering family tests forward. From 078 — Castings (see 078): who writes the specification, and does the part need replacing? In automation, replacement comes from drift, obsolescence and recalibration more than from physical wear. From 080 — Forgings (see 080): retained engineering and IP matter more than physical output. From 081 — Fabrication (see 081): who carries input cost — here imported electronics, chips, licences and currency — between quote and delivery. 089 — Machine Tools and 090 — Industrial Machinery cover the machines these systems control.

The roster itself is the first finding. Four of fourteen names fail basic accounting checks on EPS or attribution, two are not industrial businesses at all, and the largest company by revenue growth is an electrical panel builder. Each is placed below with its evidence.

Platforms, instruments and integration

A control platform is sticky. Once a refinery runs on a particular DCS, every new unit, safety upgrade, operator console and spare card tends to come from the same family, because mixing systems adds integration risk to a plant where a stoppage can cost crores a day. The installed base becomes a service and migration annuity. Honeywell Automation India sells and services the Honeywell platform in India; the platform’s technology belongs to its parent.

An instrument is less sticky but more numerous. Meters and sensors drift and must be recalibrated or replaced; process plants hold thousands of them. A maker that owns its designs, manufactures them and supports calibration earns repeat business on reliability and service, not on lock-in. Rishabh and Tempsens are in this category.

A robot integrator usually buys the robot from a global maker and adds fixtures, conveyors, programming and commissioning. Its value added is engineering hours and accountability for the line meeting cycle-time targets. It carries project risk without owning the core technology, much like the machinery integrators in 090.

The margin pools are the platform and the installed-base service. Honeywell Automation India sits closest to both, though its share of the platform’s economics is set by its arrangements with its parent. Instrument makers earn good margins if they own designs and certifications. Integrators of bought-in robots earn engineering margins on projects, which can turn negative when a line is late. Panel builders earn execution margins on bought-in switchgear. Distributors earn trading margins.

Honeywell’s export line deserves its own attention. Its FY26 annual report says export sales were 33% of total sales, down from 35%, and describes the company as a captive service and manufacturing exporter within Honeywell’s global supply chain. They are stable but not market-driven.

Fund-manager lens

Ask each company: whose IP is in the product, who owns the customer’s installed base, and what share of revenue is bought-in hardware passed through. The answers place it on the chain more reliably than its name or its data-vendor classification.

The audit

Nine exclusions, one splice, two mixed-business sensitivities, four unresolved EPS conflicts. All fourteen series end on 18 September 2026. Affordable’s NSE series begins at its October 2024 migration; its same-ISIN BSE SME history from June 2018 was joined at a scale factor of 0.981. Tempsens with 15 sessions, Axis with 113 and Patil with 310 are too short; Patil’s first TradingView bar (20 June 2025) precedes the 23 June listing date in its own result note, an unresolved chronology difference. Aplab traded on only 101–128 days a year in 2018–2020, with gaps of up to 43 days; it is retained because no gap exceeds the 45-day rule, but its 2020 and 2023 gains came on thin trading. Continental Controls has a 71-day gap in 2020 and Universal a 57-day gap in 2026; both are excluded on business grounds anyway.

EPS and attribution failures, not repaired: Axis’s FY26 EPS of 6.12 multiplies to group comprehensive income, not owner profit. PIGL prints FY26 EPS of 8.13 in its annual report and 4.54 in its annual result. Continental’s annual-report EPS note prints a share count ten times its capital. Canarys’ half-year owner-profit rows do not add to the annual owner profit. Each is disclosed in the earnings section.

Two inclusions need defending. Marine Electricals is primarily an electrical-panel builder, not an automation-technology owner; it is included because the watchlist places it here, it has marine automation operations, and it has the longest usable history among the mid-sized names. Rishabh is included although part of its revenue is die casting at Lumel, because its core is measurement instruments. Both effects are isolated: without Marine the basket’s 2026 return is +20.6%; without Rishabh +19.5%; the core three alone +2.3%.

Honeywell: flat revenue, better margin, large cash

Honeywell’s revenue rose only 1.8%, but its operating margin rose from 12.0% to 14.3% and PAT 21%. Other income of ₹46.4 crore — interest on its large cash balance — is about a fifth of pre-tax profit. For FY26 its operating proxy was about ₹605.5 crore on revenue of ₹4,681.9 crore, a 12.9% margin, with other income of ₹179.0 crore. Domestic revenue was ₹3,130.6 crore and exports 33% of sales. The margin is moderate for a technology franchise because much of the revenue is project execution and captive engineering services; the high-value platform IP sits with the parent.

Marine: growth through panels

Marine’s revenue rose 55.2% and its operating margin edged up to 10.9%. Its filings describe the business as power distribution for data centres, industry and large buildings; its October 2025 presentation claims a leadership position in data-centre electrical works and lists data-centre customers. Marine presentation, October 2025 Material costs were ₹221.50 crore against revenue of ₹259.16 crore, with a ₹34.09 crore inventory build: a high bought-in content typical of panel building, and growth that is absorbing working capital.

Rishabh: steady instruments, Polish mix

Rishabh’s revenue rose 4.2% and its margin to 16.8%. Its standalone Indian business (instruments) earned ₹77.60 crore of revenue in the quarter; the rest is mainly Lumel in Poland, whose subsidiaries include Lumel Alucast, an aluminium die-casting business. Group profit of ₹19.36 crore exceeds owner profit of ₹19.03 crore. Currency translation of the Polish business is material in some periods and sits in other comprehensive income.

Aplab and Affordable: small companies, lumpy losses

Aplab’s revenue fell 37.8% and it lost money at the operating level. Its FY26 other income of ₹3.34 crore exceeded its PAT of ₹2.52 crore and included a ₹1.22 crore gain on sale of factory premises in the December quarter, so even the profitable year rested on non-operating items. Affordable’s June-quarter revenue fell 41.2% and its loss widened; its FY26 profit of ₹6.97 crore included a ₹1.51 crore GST provision reversal and ₹1.30 crore of interest accrued on an overseas-subsidiary loan. Its order book stood at about ₹127.16 crore on 31 May 2026, including ₹19.55 crore booked in the quarter, plus a ₹36 crore parking order on a three-year lease model. Affordable presentation, June 2026

The honest read

Honeywell, Rishabh and Tempsens earn mid-teens to twenty per cent operating margins from owned products and service. Marine earns about 11% on fast growth in panels. Aplab and Affordable lose money at the operating level this quarter. The basket’s 2026 return reflects the market rewarding Marine’s growth and Rishabh’s recovery, not a sector-wide margin story.

The table explains the share price. EPS rose 55% from FY22 to FY24 and then stalled: FY26 EPS was only 0.3% above FY25. A company priced for steady compounding delivered two flat years, and the valuation multiple fell; the share is 40% below its July 2024 high even though the June 2026 quarter showed margin recovery. Dividends rose every year and the payout is modest relative to earnings, which leaves cash accumulating on the balance sheet and earning interest — the ₹46.4 crore of June-quarter other income.

Related-party scale

The AGM notice for July 2026 sought approval for material related-party transactions in FY27 of up to ₹9,500 million with Honeywell International (22.7% of FY25 turnover) and up to ₹7,700 million with a second Honeywell group company (18.4%), covering purchases and sales of goods and services, engineering services and contract manufacturing. Together the approved ceilings equal about two-fifths of a year’s turnover. These are ceilings, not actual amounts; actual transactions are reported in the annual report’s related-party note. Their scale shows how much of HAIL’s revenue and cost base is set within the group.

For a minority shareholder the questions are therefore specific. What margin does HAIL earn on engineering and contract-manufacturing exports to the parent, and who sets it? What does HAIL pay for platform technology, and is that changing? How will the parent’s separation into Automation and Aerospace companies, with the Aerospace distribution set for 29 June 2026, affect which group entities HAIL trades with? None of these is answered by Indian capex data. HAIL is best read as an Indian sales, service and engineering arm of a global platform, with a strong balance sheet and a margin that the parent’s arrangements bound above and below.

Fund-manager lens

Value HAIL on its own cash flows after related-party terms, add back excess cash, and require a discount for the fact that the platform IP and pricing power sit outside the listed company. The June-quarter margin recovery is encouraging; two years of flat EPS say it must persist before the old premium returns.

The ladder is roughly what the value-chain analysis predicts. Companies that own certified products and export them (Tempsens) earn the most. Platform service with captive exports (Honeywell) and instrument makers (Rishabh, Axis) sit in the low teens or around ten per cent. Integrators can match that in a good year (Patil) but are volatile (Affordable). Panel builders, electrical contractors and distributors earn mid-single-digit margins on bought-in content. Small companies with one-off income (Aplab, Affordable) look better than their operations.

Margin is only half the test; cash is the other. Of the twelve, only Honeywell, Rishabh and Rexnord produced operating cash close to or above profit in FY26; Tempsens converted about 62% in its pre-IPO year. Several higher-margin names — Axis, Patil — had negative or nil operating cash, which means their reported margins were tied up in receivables and contract assets. For an integrator, a 12% margin that arrives as cash a year later is worth much less than it appears.

Rishabh and the Lumel question

Rishabh’s profile is changing. Its FY26 annual report says Lumel Alucast, its Polish high-pressure die-casting business, is restructuring: exiting large loss-making legacy contracts and moving from a historical 50:50 automotive/non-automotive mix toward 75:25 non-automotive. Rishabh FY26 annual report If that succeeds, the group’s margin and cash should improve as a low-margin automotive casting business shrinks; if it does not, die casting will continue to dilute an instruments business that earns more. The June quarter’s higher margin (16.8% against 14.9%) is consistent with the first path, but one quarter does not establish it.

How to use the ladder

Place every new automation name on this ladder before looking at its share price. A distributor or panel builder priced like a product owner is the most common mispricing in this space; a product owner priced like a distributor is the rarer opportunity.

The cash table sorts the business models. Honeywell converts profit to cash because it is paid on milestones by large customers and by its parent for exports, and holds cash it does not need. Instrument makers convert reasonably because their products are shipped and invoiced quickly. Project businesses growing fast — Marine, Axis — consume cash because panels and systems are built, delivered and then await commissioning and collection. Distribution consumes cash in inventory.

Marine is the case to watch. A company growing revenue 55% while operating cash is negative is financing its customers’ projects. That is normal in a data-centre build-out where developers pay on milestones, but it means reported profit leads cash by months, and a delayed project or customer dispute would land on the balance sheet first.

The test to apply

Compare three-year cumulative operating cash with profit. For integrators and panel builders, read receivable and contract-asset growth against revenue growth. For Honeywell, separate interest income from operating cash. For Affordable, ask why parent and group cash diverged by ₹35 crore.

How integration projects make and lose money

Three of the roster’s names — Affordable, Patil and Axis — are integrators, and Honeywell’s project business works the same way. Their results swing more than product makers’ because of how a project is priced, executed and paid for. Understanding the mechanics explains Affordable’s alternation between large March-quarter profits and June-quarter losses.

The life of a line project

A carmaker or component maker specifies a welding or assembly line: the parts, the cycle time, the quality checks. The integrator quotes a fixed price that includes robots bought from a global maker, fixtures and tooling it designs and builds, conveyors and controls, programming, installation and acceptance trials. It may receive an advance on order, further payments on dispatch and the balance after the customer accepts the line in production, often with a retention held through a warranty period. Patil’s May 2026 presentation describes exactly this offering — intelligent assembly and welding lines integrating robotics, controls and precision engineering — and an order book of more than ₹100 crore from automotive and engineering clients. Patil presentation, May 2026

Margin is made or lost in three places. First, the quote: if the integrator underestimates engineering hours or the robot’s price moves before purchase, the margin shrinks before work begins. Second, execution: fixture redesigns, programming time and site delays add cost that the fixed price does not cover. Third, acceptance: if the line does not meet cycle time, the final payment waits while engineers stay on site at the integrator’s expense. Because most revenue is recognised over the project, a delayed acceptance shows up as rising contract assets and receivables rather than as an immediate loss — which is why integrators’ operating cash often lags profit, as Patil’s nil FY26 operating cash and Axis’s negative cash show.

Why small integrators look seasonal

Customers schedule line installations around model launches and plant shutdowns, and many push for commissioning before the March year-end. Affordable’s consolidated pre-tax profit was ₹7.38 crore in the March 2026 quarter and a pre-tax loss of ₹4.80 crore in June. Its fixed costs — engineers, a factory, subsidiaries — run every quarter, while billable milestones bunch. That pattern makes a single quarter almost meaningless for a small integrator; the full year and the order book matter. Affordable’s book of about ₹127 crore at 31 May 2026, against FY26 revenue of ₹117.67 crore, is more than a year of work, but the June-quarter loss shows the conversion risk.

Lease models change the risk

Affordable’s presentation describes a ₹36 crore robotic-parking order executed on a lease model over three years. Under a lease, the integrator builds and owns or finances the asset and is paid over time, rather than receiving most of the price on completion. Revenue becomes smoother and potentially longer-lived, but the integrator’s balance sheet carries the asset and its funding. For a company whose group operating cash was ₹7.18 crore in FY26 and whose parent and group cash flows differed by about ₹35 crore, the financing of lease projects deserves as much attention as their margin.

The newest listing: Tempsens

Tempsens, outside the basket for lack of history, is the roster’s clearest example of the product-owner model. Its red herring prospectus of August 2026 describes a fresh issue of up to ₹950 million and an offer for sale of up to 1.85 crore shares by promoter-group and other selling shareholders. Tempsens red herring prospectus, August 2026 Its June-quarter revenue grew 33% with a 20% operating margin, and its FY26 owner margin of 15.1% is the highest in the roster. Two cautions apply: the June 2025 and March 2026 quarterly comparisons in its first listed result are management-approved but not audited or reviewed, and June EPS must not be multiplied by post-IPO shares. Once it has a year of trading history, it is the obvious candidate to join a future instrumentation basket.

How to read an integrator

Judge an integrator on full-year margin, order-book coverage, contract assets and operating cash together. A strong year with rising contract assets is a promise; a strong year with operating cash close to profit is a result.

The critical constraint for integrators is engineering capacity, not hardware. A robot cell or a DCS project needs application engineers to design, program and commission it; a team stretched across too many projects delays acceptance and erodes margin. For panel builders it is switchgear supply and working capital. For instrument makers it is certification and component sourcing.

Bought-in content is the hidden variable in margins. When a panel builder or integrator grows by winning larger projects with more bought-in equipment, revenue rises faster than engineering value added and margin percentage falls even if absolute profit rises. Marine’s material cost at 85% of June revenue (before the inventory change) illustrates the point. Comparing its margin with Honeywell’s or Rishabh’s without adjusting for bought-in content would mislead.

The global industry and India’s place

Global automation is dominated by a small number of platform owners — Siemens, ABB, Rockwell, Emerson, Schneider, Honeywell, Yokogawa, Mitsubishi Electric — and robot makers such as Fanuc, Yaskawa, ABB and KUKA. Most operate in India through unlisted subsidiaries; Honeywell, ABB, Siemens and Schneider also have listed Indian companies, of which only Honeywell Automation is in this watchlist. The listed Indian automation universe in this roster is therefore a sample of one multinational subsidiary, two mid-sized product companies and several small integrators.

India’s distinctive role is engineering. Honeywell Automation India describes itself as a captive service and manufacturing exporter in the global supply chain, with exports at a third of sales. Tempsens reports exports at about 28.5% of revenue; Rishabh’s Lumel acquisition gives it a European manufacturing base. Indian instrument makers compete on cost and engineering in segments where certification and reliability matter more than scale.

Robot adoption is rising from a low base. India’s 9,120 installations in 2024 compare with hundreds of thousands a year in China; automotive accounts for about 45% of the Indian total. Integrators like Affordable and Patil serve that adoption, but they buy robots from the global makers and compete on engineering and price for automotive and engineering line projects.

Data-centre electrical infrastructure is the newest demand source. Marine’s and Patil’s presentations both cite data-centre work. The build-out creates demand for switchgear, panels, busways and cooling; for this roster it shows up as panel orders rather than as automation software.

The technology most likely to shift economics is the move toward open, software-defined control and analytics. It can make plants less dependent on a single platform vendor, which threatens the installed-base annuity of incumbents, while creating new subscription revenue for those who own the software. For the Indian listed companies, the question is whether they own any of that software or only implement it.

Affordable’s subsidiaries are building mobile robotics and robots-as-a-service. Its June 2026 presentation describes a parking order executed on a lease model over three years. Lease and service models smooth revenue but require the integrator to fund the assets, which a company with negative group operating cash can do only with outside capital.

Instrument technology moves slowly but steadily toward digital communication and self-diagnosis. Makers that add those features to certified products, as Rishabh and Tempsens claim to, can earn better prices and stronger service ties; makers that do not become commodity suppliers.

Policy, standards and the parent question

Production incentives and electronics policy. Honeywell’s annual report notes production-linked incentive schemes as part of the context for manufacturing in India. Any PLI benefit should be verified at company level by approval and disbursement; the existence of a scheme is not a company catalyst.

Safety, hazardous-area and metrology certification. Instruments used in hazardous areas need certified designs; meters used for billing need metrology approval. These are product-specific requirements and should be checked against the actual product and market, not inferred from a sector label.

Withdrawn machinery order. The 2024 machinery omnibus technical regulation was withdrawn in January 2026, as recorded in 089 — Machine Tools; its timetable is not a pending requirement for automation equipment.

The parent question. For Honeywell Automation India, the policy that matters most is the parent’s. Royalty rates, transfer prices on exports, allocation of new technologies and the treatment of businesses after the global separation are set by agreements between HAIL and its parent, subject to related-party approval rules. Minority shareholders should read the related-party transactions note each year as closely as the revenue line.

SME platform rules. Several names here listed on SME platforms (Affordable before migration, Patil, Canarys). SME trading lots and liquidity limit price discovery; Affordable’s migration to the NSE main board in 2024 is why its NSE price history is short.

Where the cycle stands now

Stocks: the basket is +31.8% this year vs -10.7% for the Nifty (to 18 Sep 2026). Rishabh +91.5% and Marine +75.3% drive the result; Honeywell +8.0%, Aplab +1.0% and Affordable −14.6%. The three pure automation and instrument names together are only +2.3%.

Operating results are mixed. Honeywell’s revenue was flat but margins improved; Rishabh grew modestly with better margins; Marine grew 55% at stable margins while consuming cash; Tempsens, outside the basket, grew 33%. Aplab and Affordable lost money in the seasonally weak June quarter. The broad picture is selective strength in electrical infrastructure and instruments, and weakness at small integrators.

A second debate is about the parent discount. Investors have historically paid a premium for HAIL’s scarcity, cash and multinational governance. The counter-argument is that the platform economics are set by related-party terms the minority does not control, that a large share of revenue is captive, and that two years of flat EPS show the limits of that model. The share’s 40% fall from its 2024 high suggests the market has moved toward the second view; the June-quarter margin recovery is the first evidence against it. Neither view is settled by one quarter.

A third is about data centres. The theme has lifted Marine sharply, and Patil’s presentation also cites data-centre infrastructure. For investors seeking automation exposure, the risk is buying electrical contracting at an automation multiple. For investors seeking data-centre exposure, the risk is buying a panel builder whose cash conversion has not yet caught up with its growth.

The central debate is whether the Indian listed universe gives investors real exposure to factory automation. The honest answer from this roster is: partially, through Honeywell (with parent constraints) and through instrument makers. Most other names are adjacent — electrical, integration, distribution or IT — and the market has recently rewarded the adjacent names more than the core.

The decision rule

Prefer companies that own their products and certifications and convert profit to cash; in this roster that is Honeywell, Rishabh and, once it has history, Tempsens. Treat panel builders as electrical-infrastructure plays and small integrators as project businesses that must prove profitability through a full year.

It comes from Rishabh (instruments plus Polish die casting) and Marine (electrical panels). The three core automation names returned 2.3%.

What limits Honeywell India’s share of Honeywell’s technology?

The platform IP belongs to the parent; HAIL sells, services and exports under related-party arrangements.

Why is Marine’s cash negative while profit grows?

Panel projects absorb receivables, inventory and advances before customers pay on completion.

What is wrong with Axis’s EPS?

It multiplies to group comprehensive income, not owner profit.

Why is Affordable’s price history only partly NSE?

It listed on the BSE SME platform in 2018 and migrated to the NSE main board in 2024; the same-ISIN histories were spliced.

Why are Tempsens and Patil excluded?

They listed in August 2026 and June 2025 respectively and lack usable history, though both are relevant businesses.

Sources and method

Primary result filings govern revenue, other income, profit, EPS and capital. Annual reports govern cash flows, share counts and business descriptions. Presentations supply management statements and plans, dated. All evidence is dated no later than 18 September 2026; Rexnord announcements from 19 September onward are excluded.

Units: Honeywell, Rishabh and Tempsens file in ₹ million; others in ₹ lakh; all converted to crore. Where text extraction was ambiguous, the PDF page was rendered and read (Marine’s consolidated statement, Aplab’s statement). The five price series were checked for the fixed endpoint; sensitivities and member returns are reproduced by audit_093.py after compute_stats.py 093. The research package that preceded this page is preserved in the repository’s pilot folder with its source manifest.
