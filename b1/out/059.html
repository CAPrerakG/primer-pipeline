Industry primer · 59 of 278 · Defence family, part 3

Shipbuilding & Ship Repair

India's listed shipyards build destroyers, frigates, corvettes and submarines for the Navy, and — increasingly — tankers, bulk carriers and hybrid ferries for foreign owners. Warship building is a protected, government-funded business; commercial shipbuilding is one of the most competitive industries in the world, dominated by China, South Korea and Japan. This primer explains how a shipyard makes money, why the three state yards' basket rose about thirteen-fold between 2022 and 2025, why India's private yards failed in the 2010s, and what the next wave of submarine contracts and the government's shipbuilding package could change.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Protected warships, competitive merchant ships

Explain it to a 10-year-old

A shipyard is like a giant garage where huge boats are built piece by piece, a bit like assembling a model kit the size of three football fields. Building one big ship takes years. The Navy is like a customer who orders a few very special, very expensive boats — warships and submarines — and pays in instalments while they are being built. Ordinary boats, like oil tankers and cargo ships, are ordered by companies all over the world, and most of them go to shipyards in China, Korea and Japan because those garages are the biggest and fastest. Indian shipyards are good at the Navy's special boats and are now trying to win more ordinary ones too.

Say it to a client in one minute

Indian shipbuilding is a warship business looking for a second leg.

  • Warships: Mazagon Dock (submarines, destroyers, frigates), GRSE (frigates, corvettes, patrol vessels) and Cochin Shipyard (aircraft carrier, anti-submarine craft, repair) are funded by the Navy's capital budget and paid in milestones.
  • The cliff: Mazagon's order book has fallen to ₹18,218 cr as big projects deliver; it expects P75(I) and three more Scorpènes to add over ₹1 lakh cr in FY27.
  • Commercial: Cochin has ₹7,200 cr of export commercial orders; the government's ₹69,725 cr package supports new yards and finance, but China, Korea and Japan take over 90% of world orders.
  • Stocks: the basket rose about thirteen-fold over 2022–2025, then fell; all three yards are 31–51% below their highs.
  • Caution: Swan Defence, the relisted former Reliance Naval, has risen 73-fold on upper circuits with a tiny free float.

A warship is roughly one-third hull and machinery and two-thirds weapons, sensors and electronics, much of which is supplied to the yard by BEL (058), BDL (057) and foreign vendors. The yard designs (often with the Navy's own design bureau), builds the hull, installs and integrates everything, and runs sea trials. That is why a shipyard's revenue includes large pass-through items and why its margin is lower than a defence-electronics company's.

The chain in one breath

  • Designers draw the ship.
  • Steel makers and equipment suppliers provide the materials and machinery.
  • The shipyard welds, fits out and tests it over several years.
  • Weapons and electronics makers fit the teeth and eyes.
  • The Navy pays in stages and returns the ship for refits for 30 years.
Fund-manager lens

The critical asset is dock capacity plus a nominated position in the Navy's building plan. India has only a handful of yards that can build large warships, and the Navy spreads its orders among them. That gives each state yard a quasi-guaranteed pipeline — but also makes its revenue lumpy, tied to when the next big programme is signed. Mazagon's order book fell from ₹20,535 cr to ₹18,218 cr in one quarter as projects delivered; the next step-up depends almost entirely on the submarine contracts.

The rest of the industry

Goa Shipyard and Hindustan Shipyard are state-owned but unlisted; L&T builds warships at Kattupalli; Reliance Naval (now Swan), ABG Shipyard and Bharati Shipyard were the private yards of the 2000s boom, and all three ended in insolvency. Equipment suppliers sit elsewhere: CFF Fluid Control (057), BEL (058), cable makers (044). The basket uses the three state yards with continuous trading histories.

Treasury incomeInterest on customer advancesAdds materially to profit at state yards—

A warship contract is paid in stages — keel laying, launch, trials, delivery — and the yard recognises revenue as work progresses. Because the Navy pays advances, the state yards hold large cash balances and earn interest on them, which adds to profit. Mazagon Dock's EBITDA margin of 15.2% in Q1 FY27 is modest by defence standards, reflecting the large share of pass-through equipment; its profit margin is flattered by other income.

Commercial shipbuilding is a different business. Prices are fixed in foreign currency years before delivery, steel and equipment costs can move against the yard, and delays trigger penalties. India's private yards learned this painfully in the 2010s. The state yards have entered it carefully: Cochin's ₹7,200 cr of export commercial orders — hybrid and specialised vessels for European owners — is the largest such book among the listed yards.

Why repair matters

Ship repair has short cycles, quick cash and good margins. Cochin's repair revenue fell 37% in Q1 FY27 only because the year-ago quarter included the refit of the aircraft carrier INS Vikramaditya. For Cochin, repair can swing profit by a fifth; for investors, the quarterly mix between building and repair explains much of its earnings volatility.

P75(I) is the swing factor. After years of trials and negotiation, the Mazagon Dock–TKMS team was selected, cost negotiations were completed by March 2026 and the proposal went for Cabinet Committee on Security approval; by August 2026 a decision was reported to be close, at a headline value of about ₹70,000 cr. The first boat is due within seven years of signing, with indigenous content rising from 45% on the first hull to 60% on the last. Once signed, Mazagon's order book would rise to well over ₹1 lakh cr — more than six times its present level — giving revenue visibility into the 2030s — but revenue would build slowly, because submarine construction takes years to reach full pace.

How to read it

For a shipyard, the order book tells you revenue for the next five years, not the next five quarters. Mazagon's near-term revenue will come from finishing existing ships, and its growth guidance of 15–17% for FY27 does not depend on P75(I). The contract matters for FY29 onward and for the valuation multiple today.

Steel is no longer the constraint it once was — DMR-249 grade warship steel is made in India. The binding limits are engines and dock space. Almost every Indian warship runs on foreign-designed engines, and a yard can only work on as many hulls as its docks and slipways allow. That is why the government's package includes a scheme to build new shipbuilding clusters and why Mazagon Dock has pursued extra capacity, including the Colombo Dockyard stake.

7 · The business calendar

Launches, deliveries and the fiscal year-end

  • January–March: the Ministry's capital spending peaks; milestone billing is heaviest; deliveries are timed before 31 March where possible.
  • April–May: fourth-quarter results and dividends; new-year orders.
  • June–September: the monsoon slows outdoor work and sea trials on the west coast.
  • Throughout: keel-layings, launches and commissionings are public events that often move the stocks, though they rarely change revenue timing.

Large contract signings — P75(I), additional Scorpènes, next-generation corvettes — do not follow a calendar. They depend on Cabinet approvals, which can slip by quarters. The stocks tend to rally on reports of an imminent approval and fade if it is delayed.

  • Windows: Feb–Mar beat the Nifty in 3 of 9 years (median -4.2 pts); Apr–Jul beat the Nifty in 8 of 9 years (median +16.4 pts); Aug–Sep beat the Nifty in 4 of 8 years (median +2.5 pts); Oct–Dec beat the Nifty in 5 of 9 years (median +0.3 pts); Sep–Nov beat the Nifty in 7 of 9 years (median +7.8 pts); Jan–Mar beat the Nifty in 1 of 9 years (median -5.1 pts).

Control: small caps as a whole (Nifty Smallcap 100) beat the Nifty in April in 11 of 15 years, by an average of 3.0 points, and lag in January–February. Part of any April strength is this market-wide effect. After removing it, this industry's own April edge is about +6.2 points.

Reading it: only nine years (2018–2026) and three stocks — Cochin Shipyard from 2017, GRSE from late 2018 and Mazagon Dock from late 2020 — so treat every number as indicative. April–July has beaten the Nifty in 8 of 9 years (median +16.4 points; only 2018 failed). January–March has worked in 1 of 9 and February alone in 1 of 9 (average −7.1). The shape matches the rest of the Defence family: budgets priced in before February; fourth-quarter results, dividends and new-year orders in April–May.

A tendency, not a rule: small samples, survivorship bias (only names still listed) and equal weighting. Use it for timing context, not as a signal.

The exceptions, and why

  • April–July failed only in 2018 (−22.5), when Cochin was the only stock and small caps crashed. Its best runs were 2024 (+187.7) and 2023 (+63.3), the re-rating years.
  • January–March worked only in 2025 (+5.3); every other year from 2018 to 2026 lost to the Nifty.
  • August–September failed badly in 2024 (−30.7), after the July 2024 peak.
How to use this

Nine years and three stocks make any calendar fragile, and the 2022–2024 re-rating inflates the April–July numbers. But the pattern agrees with 057 and 058 across a larger sample, which makes it more credible: the Defence family as a whole tends to lag in January–March and lead in April–July.

9 · What moves margins

Eight drivers

  1. Contract signings. A single submarine programme can multiply an order book.
  2. Execution pace. Revenue follows physical progress; delays in equipment push it out.
  3. Pass-through share. More supplied equipment means more revenue but lower margin percentage.
  4. Repair mix. Refits lift margins; a big refit in one year creates a high base for the next.
  5. Cost escalation clauses. Protect warship margins; commercial contracts usually have none.
  6. Treasury income. Interest on advances adds to profit; falling cash balances reduce it.
  7. Commercial pricing. Newbuilding prices and currency affect export orders.
  8. Government support. Shipbuilding financial assistance offsets part of the cost gap with Asian yards.

The anatomy of the cycles

  1. The supercycle and bust (2003–2016, global): China's growth drove record orders; after 2008 orders collapsed and yards worldwide lost money for a decade. India's private yards — ABG, Bharati and Pipavav — expanded into this bust and failed; ABG later became the subject of one of India's largest bank-fraud cases.
  2. Warship PSUs as bonds (2017–2021): Cochin, GRSE and Mazagon listed and traded as dividend-paying government companies with little growth priced in.
  3. The re-rating (2022–2024): indigenisation, warship deliveries and the global rearmament theme lifted the basket 114%, 142% and 105% in successive years.
  4. The pause (2025–2026): peaks in July 2024 (Cochin) and May–June 2025 (Mazagon, GRSE); order books running down ahead of the next big contracts.

Patterns that repeat

  • Warship stocks follow order visibility, not the global shipping cycle. The Indian yards re-rated while Korean yards were still losing money.
  • Commercial shipbuilding punishes over-expansion. Every Indian private yard that bet on the 2000s boom failed.
  • Low-float relistings run far ahead of fundamentals. Swan's 73-fold rise mirrors the pattern seen elsewhere in this project.

The commercial market is in a strong phase: fleet renewal for decarbonisation, the container-ship boom and LNG demand have pushed newbuilding prices close to their 2008 records (the Clarksons index was about 182 in February 2026). Geopolitics is reshaping it too — the US announced port fees on Chinese-built ships in 2025 (later suspended for a year under a US–China truce) and is courting Korean yards for naval work — which creates an opening for non-Chinese builders. India's ambition, set out in the Maritime India Vision 2030 and Amrit Kaal Vision 2047, is to become a top-ten shipbuilder by 2030 and top-five by 2047; the gap to close is enormous.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Air-independent propulsion. Lets conventional submarines stay submerged for weeks; the core of P75(I).
  2. Green propulsion. Hybrid, battery-electric, LNG, methanol and ammonia-ready ships; Cochin has a green-propulsion joint venture with HBL (046).
  3. Modular block construction. Building ships in large pre-outfitted blocks cuts build time.
  4. Unmanned surface and underwater vessels. A growing naval category (see 061).
  5. Digital shipyards. 3D design, digital twins and automation raise productivity.
  6. Indigenous design. The Navy's own designs for destroyers, frigates and corvettes, and a planned indigenous submarine after P75(I).

What is priced in

After falling 31–51% from their highs, the state yards are priced for their existing execution plus a reasonable chance of the submarine contracts. Mazagon at about ₹89,815 cr is roughly 41 times its Q1 FY27 profit annualised — a full multiple for a company whose order book is shrinking, justified only if P75(I) and the Scorpènes arrive. GRSE, with the fastest growth, has corrected least. Cochin's fall reflects its weaker margin outlook and the difficulty of making commercial shipbuilding profitable. Swan's valuation, by contrast, is the product of a low-float relisting, not of earnings.

The honest read

The warship business is sound and protected, and the Navy's plan provides decades of work. But shipyard earnings are lumpy and order books cyclical; the easy re-rating of 2022–2024 is over. The next leg depends on signing the submarine contracts and on whether commercial shipbuilding — supported by the government package — can become profitable at scale. History, including India's own private-yard failures, argues for patience on the second.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What is the expected timeline for the next major contract, and what is its value?
  2. How much revenue will the existing order book deliver this year and next?
  3. What share of contract value is supplied equipment passed through at low margin?
  4. What cost-escalation protection do your warship contracts have?
  5. What margins are you earning on commercial ships, and what penalties apply for delay?
  6. How much of profit comes from treasury income, and how will it change as advances are used?
  7. What is your dock and slipway capacity, and how much is free for new orders?
  8. How will the shipbuilding package benefit you directly?
  9. How is the ship-repair pipeline for the coming year?
  10. What progress is there on indigenous content in engines and submarine systems?

19 · Glossary

Every term you'll meet

Ships and programmes

P75
The six Scorpène (Kalvari-class) submarines built by Mazagon Dock with France's Naval Group.
P75(I)
Six air-independent-propulsion submarines, Mazagon Dock with Germany's TKMS.
P15B
Visakhapatnam-class destroyers built by Mazagon Dock.
P17A
Nilgiri-class frigates built by Mazagon Dock and GRSE.
ASW SWC
Anti-submarine warfare shallow-water craft built by GRSE and Cochin.
NGC / NGOPV
Next-generation corvettes and offshore patrol vessels.
INS Vikrant
India's first indigenous aircraft carrier, built by Cochin Shipyard.
AIP
Air-independent propulsion — lets a conventional submarine stay submerged longer.
Scorpène
A French-designed conventional submarine; the Kalvari class in India.
Corvette
A small warship for coastal defence and anti-submarine work.
Frigate
A medium warship, often focused on escort and anti-submarine roles.
Destroyer
A large, heavily armed surface warship.
Hull
The main watertight body of a ship.
Bulk carrier
A merchant ship that carries unpackaged cargo such as coal, ore or grain.
IMO Type II chemical tanker
A tanker built to carry hazardous liquid chemicals, the type Swan's letter of intent covers.
LNG carrier
A ship that carries liquefied natural gas at −162°C; a Korean speciality.

Shipbuilding terms

Keel laying
The ceremonial start of a ship's construction, often a payment milestone.
Launch
Floating the hull for the first time.
Outfitting
Installing machinery, piping, cabling and systems after launch.
Sea trials
Testing the ship at sea before delivery.
Commissioning
Formal entry of a warship into naval service.
Dry dock
A basin that can be drained to build or repair ships.
Slipway
An inclined ramp on which ships are built and launched.
Block construction
Building a ship in large modules that are joined together.
Refit
A major overhaul of a ship during its service life.
DMR-249
Indian warship-grade steel made by SAIL.
CGT
Compensated gross tonnage — a measure of shipbuilding work that adjusts for vessel complexity.
Gross tonnage (GT)
A measure of a ship's internal volume.
Newbuilding price index
Clarksons' index of new-ship prices.
Refund guarantee
A bank guarantee that repays a ship owner's instalments if the yard fails to deliver.

Finance and policy

Milestone payment
A payment triggered by a construction stage.
Cost escalation clause
A contract term that adjusts price for inflation in inputs.
Pass-through
Equipment bought and billed on with little margin.
Treasury income
Interest earned on cash, including customer advances.
SBFAS
Shipbuilding Financial Assistance Scheme — subsidy to Indian yards, extended to 2036.
Maritime Development Fund
A ₹25,000 cr fund for long-term maritime finance.
Shipbuilding cluster
A new integrated shipbuilding zone with yards and suppliers.
Navratna
A status giving large state companies more financial autonomy; GRSE received it in June 2026.
T group
An exchange category where every trade must be settled by delivery, used for relisted or surveillance stocks.
Upper circuit
The maximum price rise allowed in a day; low-float stocks can hit it repeatedly.
Minimum public shareholding
The rule that at least 25% of a listed company's shares be held by the public.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. Why is a shipyard's EBITDA margin lower than a defence-electronics company's?

A large share of contract value is supplied equipment passed through at low margin.

2. What is P75(I), and why does it matter to Mazagon Dock?

Six air-independent-propulsion submarines with TKMS, about ₹70,000 cr; with three more Scorpènes it would add over ₹1 lakh cr to an order book of ₹18,218 cr.

3. What did GRSE report in Q1 FY27?

Revenue ₹1,815 cr (+38.5%), profit ₹173 cr (+43.8%); order book ₹13,596 cr.

4. Why did Cochin's profit fall in Q1 FY27?

Ship repair revenue fell 37% against a high base that included the INS Vikramaditya refit, and margin guidance disappointed.

5. What are the three parts of the ₹69,725 cr package?

SBFAS ₹24,736 cr, Maritime Development Fund ₹25,000 cr and Shipbuilding Development Scheme ₹19,989 cr.

6. What share of 2025 ship orders went to China?

63%; Korea 21%, Japan about 9%.

7. Which Indian private yards failed in the 2010s?

ABG Shipyard, Bharati Shipyard and Pipavav (later Reliance Naval, now Swan Defence).

8. Why is Swan Defence excluded from the basket?

It relisted in January 2025 with a tiny free float and rose 73-fold, much of it on consecutive upper circuits.

9. Which window has been strongest for the shipyards?

April–July, which beat the Nifty in 8 of 9 years.

10. Why do Indian shipyard stocks not follow the global shipping cycle?

Their revenue comes mainly from Navy orders, which follow the defence budget, not freight rates.
