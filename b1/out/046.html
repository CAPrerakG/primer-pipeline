Batteries & Energy Storage

Batteries store electricity as chemical energy and give it back when needed — to start a car, keep a phone tower running during a power cut, power a torch, or hold solar power from noon until evening. India's listed battery industry is still mostly the old chemistry, lead-acid; its two leaders are spending heavily to become lithium cell makers just as grid-scale storage takes off.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

An old cash machine paying for a new, harder business

Explain it to a 10-year-old

A battery is a box that stores electricity to use later, like a water tank on a roof stores water. The oldest kind, lead-acid, is heavy and cheap and sits under every car bonnet; when it wears out after a few years, you buy a new one, so the makers sell millions every year. The newer kind, lithium, is light and stores much more, which is why phones, electric scooters and big "power tanks" for solar farms use it. India's biggest battery companies are very good at the old kind. Now they are building expensive new factories to make lithium cells — but China already makes them in huge numbers very cheaply, so it is a hard race.

Say it to a client in one minute

The listed battery industry is a mature lead-acid business funding an uncertain lithium bet.

  • Lead-acid is still the profit engine: Exide's revenue +17.6% to ₹5,305 cr and profit +27% to ₹407 cr; Amara Raja revenue +24% but EBITDA margin 9.6% against 10.7%.
  • The lithium bet is large: Exide has put ₹4,902 cr of equity into a 6 GWh Bengaluru plant expected to earn revenue from Q3 FY27; Amara Raja commissioned a ₹500 cr pilot line and targets 16 GWh by FY30.
  • Storage demand is booming: over 130 GWh tendered in 2025, 9.2 GWh expected to be commissioned in 2026, tariffs down 86% since 2022.
  • But the cells come from China: LFP cells cost about $75–84/kWh from Chinese makers, and India's cell-incentive scheme had commissioned only 1.4 GWh of 50 GWh by late 2025.
  • The stocks have de-rated: Amara Raja is 54% below its July 2024 peak, Exide 25% below its June 2024 peak.

Analogy

Lead-acid is the bicycle of batteries: heavy, cheap, reliable, recyclable, and still what most people need for short trips — starting an engine takes a burst of power for a few seconds. Lithium is the motorcycle: it goes much further on the same weight, which is essential for electric vehicles and for storing hours of solar power, but it costs more to make and the best factories are abroad. A car still needs its bicycle-battery to start; the motorcycle-battery is what powers the electric future.

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Ingredients: old batteries are melted down for their lead; lithium has to be bought from other countries.
  • Parts: metal plates and chemicals are prepared.
  • Cells: the parts are assembled into the small units that actually store energy.
  • Packs: many cells are joined with a small computer that keeps them safe.
  • Shops: mechanics and dealers sell and fit batteries.
  • Recycling: old lead batteries go back to the start of the loop.
Fund-manager lens

The critical question is what the lithium investment will earn. Lead-acid is a well-understood oligopoly: two brands, a replacement cycle, recycled raw material, and EBITDA margins of about 10–12% (Exide 12.3%, Amara Raja 9.6% this quarter). Lithium cell manufacturing is a global commodity business in which Chinese makers sell LFP cells at $75–84/kWh and run their plants at enormous scale. Exide's ₹4,902 cr and Amara Raja's multi-year programme are reasonable defences against the long-term decline of lead-acid in vehicles, but the market has refused to value them until they show utilisation, yields and customer contracts. That is why both stocks are well below their 2024 peaks even as their lead-acid profits grow.

Why lead-acid is a good business

Almost every vehicle needs a lead-acid battery to start, and the battery wears out every few years, so the replacement market grows with the number of vehicles on the road rather than with new sales. Lead is one of the most recycled materials in the world, so the key raw material comes back in the form of old batteries. Two brands dominate, with deep dealer networks. The result is steady volumes and margins, with price increases passed on when lead rises. The threat is slow, not sudden: electric two-wheelers and cars need a large lithium traction battery, and inverter customers are beginning to switch to lithium.

Analogy

Lead-acid batteries are like tyres: every vehicle has them, they wear out, and the replacement business is steadier and more profitable than selling to carmakers. The question is what happens when vehicles start to need a completely different kind of "tyre".

7 · The business calendar

Summer is battery season

  • April–June: heat shortens battery life and power cuts drive inverter sales; peak replacement season for auto and inverter batteries.
  • July–September: monsoon; replacement demand moderates, inverter demand stays high where outages persist.
  • October–December: festive vehicle sales lift OEM volumes.
  • January–March: the quietest quarter for replacement batteries; dealers destock before the financial year ends.
  • Storage tenders: SECI, NTPC, NHPC and state utilities issue BESS tenders throughout the year, with VGF tranches setting the pace.

8 · What the stocks do

Seasonality test on TradingView prices

Basket: 6 names (Exide Industries Limited, HBL Engineering Limited, Amara Raja Energy & Mobility Ltd, Eveready Industries India Ltd, High Energy Batteries Ltd., Maxvolt Energy Industries Limited), equal-weighted, monthly from Jul 2011, compared with the Nifty 50.

  • Strongest months vs the Nifty: Apr +6.1 pts (beat in 11 of 15 years); Dec +3.2 pts (beat in 9 of 15 years); Nov +3.1 pts (beat in 9 of 15 years).
  • Weakest months: Mar -4.0 pts (beat in 1 of 15 years); Feb -0.4 pts (beat in 6 of 15 years); Jan 0.0 pts (beat in 8 of 15 years).
  • Windows: Feb–Mar beat the Nifty in 2 of 15 years (median -4.2 pts); Apr–Jul beat the Nifty in 11 of 15 years (median +10.8 pts); Aug–Sep beat the Nifty in 10 of 14 years (median +3.8 pts); Oct–Dec beat the Nifty in 9 of 14 years (median +3.9 pts); Sep–Nov beat the Nifty in 10 of 14 years (median +3.9 pts); Jan–Mar beat the Nifty in 4 of 15 years (median -4.3 pts).

Control: small caps as a whole (Nifty Smallcap 100) beat the Nifty in April in 11 of 15 years, by an average of 3.0 points, and lag in January–February. Part of any April strength is this market-wide effect. After removing it, this industry's own April edge is about +3.1 points.

Reading it: one of the clearest seasonal patterns in the library, and it has a physical cause. March has beaten the Nifty in only 1 of 15 years (average −4.0 points) and February–March in 2 of 15; January–March works in only 4 of 15 (median −4.3). Then April beats the Nifty in 11 of 15 years (+6.1 points) and April–July in 11 of 15 with a median of +10.8. Heat shortens battery life, so replacement demand for car, two-wheeler and inverter batteries peaks in the Indian summer; the stocks rise into it and drift before it.

A tendency, not a rule: small samples, survivorship bias (only names still listed) and equal weighting. Use it for timing context, not as a signal.

The exceptions, and why

  • April–July failed in 4 of 15 years: 2019 (−25.5) and 2018 (−21.6), when HBL and Eveready collapsed; 2013 (−5.9) and 2012 (−0.4). Its best runs were 2024 (+43.8) and 2014 (+41.3).
  • March beat the Nifty only in 2015 (+4.3); even its "best" other years, 2020 (−1.4) and 2016 (−0.8), were small losses. The worst were 2021 (−11.4) and 2019 (−8.9).
  • February–March succeeded only in 2015 (+10.3) and 2012 (+3.2) — 13 failures in 15 years, including every year since 2016.
  • January–March 2026 (−11.7) and 2025 (−18.9) kept the pattern going.
How to use this

This is a pattern with a mechanism: replacement battery demand follows temperature, and the dealer channel destocks before March. A practical rule is to be underweight or patient through February and March and to hold through April–July. The risk to the pattern is structural: as more of these companies' value comes from lithium and storage, which do not follow the Indian summer, the seasonality should weaken.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Eight drivers

  1. The vehicle parc: the number of vehicles on the road sets replacement demand.
  2. Summer temperatures and power cuts: replacement and inverter demand.
  3. Lead prices: passed on with a lag; stable in 2026.
  4. Electric-vehicle adoption: lithium traction batteries for two-, three- and four-wheelers (see 017, 018).
  5. Grid storage tenders and VGF: 130+ GWh tendered in 2025; ₹9,100 cr or so of VGF for 43.2 GWh.
  6. Telecom and data centres: backup power, increasingly lithium.
  7. Chinese cell prices: the benchmark for every Indian lithium plan.
  8. Execution of the lithium plants: yields, utilisation and customer approvals at Exide and Amara Raja.

The anatomy of the cycles

  1. The lead-acid duopoly years (2012–2017): steady growth in vehicles and inverters, with the small caps (Eveready, HBL, High Energy) making the big moves.
  2. The small-cap bust and EV fear (2018–2019): two years of −30% as small caps crashed and investors began to price in electric vehicles.
  3. Specialty and consumer rebounds (2020–2022): Eveready's change of control, High Energy's defence orders, HBL's railway business; the two leaders lagged.
  4. The lithium re-rating (2023–2024): gigafactory plans, EV growth and HBL's Kavach orders; the basket +87% and +32%.
  5. Show me the cells (2025–2026): the leaders de-rated while their lithium plants were built; returns came from specific stories (HBL in 2025, Exide's profits in 2026).

Patterns that repeat

  • The leaders move slowly; the small caps make the extremes — Eveready +420% (2014) and −71% (2019).
  • The market pays for lithium stories before they produce revenue, then withdraws the premium while it waits.
  • Lead-acid profits have been steadier than the share prices suggest.

11 · The global picture

China makes most of the world's lithium batteries

Chinese companies — CATL and BYD above all — make the large majority of the world's lithium-ion cells and dominate every upstream step: cathode, anode, electrolyte and separator materials, and lithium refining. Their scale has pushed LFP cell prices below $100/kWh, which made grid-scale storage economic almost everywhere. Korea and Japan lead in some high-performance chemistries; the US and Europe are building plants with heavy subsidies and trade protection. India's position is typical of a late entrant: strong demand, lots of assembly of packs and storage systems, and a handful of cell projects that are only now starting to produce.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • From Japan to Korea to China in lithium cells, on scale and supply-chain control.
  • From NMC to LFP for storage and many EVs, on cost and safety — China's strength.
  • From lead-acid to lithium in inverters and telecom backup, gradually.
  • The next possible shift: sodium-ion, which avoids lithium and could suit storage and cheap vehicles.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. LFP dominance in storage: cheaper, safer, longer-lasting; 280–314 Ah prismatic cells are the storage standard.
  2. Larger storage cells and systems: fewer parts per MWh, lower cost.
  3. Sodium-ion: no lithium; early commercial use in China.
  4. Enhanced lead-acid: EFB and AGM batteries for start-stop vehicles.
  5. Lithium in inverters and telecom: replacing lead-acid where cycling is frequent.
  6. Battery recycling: recovering lithium, nickel and cobalt — Nile's pilot is an early example.

The honest read

The core lead-acid business is doing well, and in Exide's case profits are growing faster than the share price. The discount on both leaders is the market's price for their lithium plans: large, early, and up against Chinese cells that are cheap and getting cheaper. The next four quarters are the test — Exide's first commercial revenue from Bengaluru, Amara Raja's qualification results, and whether either wins storage or EV contracts at prices that cover their costs. Grid storage is a genuine boom, but at record-low tariffs and on imported cells, much of its value will go to Chinese suppliers and to developers rather than to Indian manufacturers. Investors should treat this section as a lead-acid franchise with a lithium option, and value the option cautiously until it produces.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What are cell yields and line utilisation at the lithium plant, and when do you expect EBITDA break-even?
  2. What is your cost per kWh against landed Chinese cells, including duties?
  3. Which customers have approved your cells, and for what volumes?
  4. How much more capital will the lithium business need before it funds itself?
  5. How is replacement demand growing, separately from OEM?
  6. How quickly are inverter and telecom customers switching to lithium?
  7. What share of storage-system cost is the cell, and where do you source it?
  8. How are lead price changes passed through, and with what lag?

Red flags

  • Rising capital commitments without customer contracts.
  • Storage bids at tariffs that imply losses.
  • Falling replacement-market share to new entrants.
  • Delays in PLI milestones leading to penalties.

19 · Glossary

Every term you'll meet

Battery technology

Lead-acid battery
Lead plates in sulphuric acid; cheap, heavy, highly recyclable.
SLI battery
Starting, lighting and ignition — the battery that starts a vehicle.
Flooded battery
A conventional lead-acid battery with liquid electrolyte.
VRLA
Valve-regulated lead-acid — sealed, maintenance-free batteries used in UPS and telecom.
Tubular battery
A lead-acid design with tube-shaped plates for deep cycling; the standard inverter battery.
EFB and AGM
Enhanced flooded and absorbent glass-mat batteries for start-stop vehicles.
Lithium-ion battery
A battery in which lithium ions move between cathode and anode; light and energy-dense.
Cathode and anode
The positive and negative electrodes; cathode chemistry defines the battery type.
Electrolyte and separator
The medium ions move through, and the film that keeps electrodes apart.
Cylindrical, prismatic and pouch cells
The three lithium cell formats.
Cell, module and pack
The single unit, a group of cells, and the finished battery with electronics.
Battery management system (BMS)
Electronics that monitor and protect cells in a pack.
Energy density
Energy stored per kilogram or litre.
Cycle life
How many charge-discharge cycles a battery lasts.
Depth of discharge
How much of a battery's capacity is used each cycle.
C-rate
How fast a battery charges or discharges relative to its capacity.
Round-trip efficiency
The share of stored energy recovered on discharge.
Thermal runaway
A self-heating failure that can cause lithium battery fires.
Sodium-ion battery
A lithium-free chemistry using sodium; cheaper materials, lower density.
Zinc-carbon and alkaline cells
Primary (non-rechargeable) dry cells for household devices.
Silver-zinc battery
A high-power specialty battery used in missiles and torpedoes.
Thermal battery
A reserve battery activated by heat, used in defence systems.

Industry and market

Gigafactory
A cell plant with capacity in the GWh range.
GWh and MWh
Units of stored energy; 1 GWh = 1,000 MWh.
OEM and replacement markets
Batteries sold to vehicle makers versus to vehicle owners.
Vehicle parc
The total number of vehicles in use, which drives replacement demand.
Secondary lead
Lead recovered by recycling used batteries.
Extended producer responsibility (EPR)
The obligation on battery makers to ensure collection and recycling.
Lithium carbonate
The refined lithium compound used to make LFP cathodes.
Spodumene
The hard-rock lithium ore mined in Australia, Africa and China.
ACC PLI
India's ₹18,100 cr production-linked incentive for 50 GWh of advanced-chemistry cells.
Domestic value addition (DVA)
The share of a product's value made in India, required under the PLI.
Customer qualification plant (CQP)
A small line producing cells for customers to test before large orders.
Viability gap funding (VGF)
A government grant covering part of a storage project's capital cost.
Tariff per MW per month
How storage capacity is priced in Indian tenders — ₹1.48 lakh at the record low.
Power conversion system (PCS)
The inverter that connects a storage system to the grid.
Augmentation
Adding batteries over time to offset degradation in a storage project.
Kavach
India's indigenous automatic train-protection system, HBL's largest business.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. Why is replacement demand the heart of the lead-acid business?

Every vehicle needs a starter battery that wears out every few years, so demand follows the fleet on the road and is steadier and more profitable than OEM supply.

2. Why is March so weak for these stocks?

Replacement demand is lowest before summer and dealers destock before the financial year ends; the basket beat the Nifty in March in only 1 of 15 years.

3. How much has Exide invested in its lithium subsidiary?

₹4,902 cr of equity by July 2026, with ₹1,400 cr more approved for FY27; commercial revenue is expected from Q3 FY27.

4. Why did Amara Raja's margin fall?

Higher material costs and brand investments, including IPL sponsorship: EBITDA margin 9.6% against 10.7%.

5. How far have Indian storage tariffs fallen?

From ₹10.83 lakh/MW/month in 2022 to ₹1.48 lakh in December 2025 — about 86%.

6. How far has the ACC PLI scheme progressed?

Only 1.4 GWh of 50 GWh (2.8%) had been commissioned by October 2025, all by Ola Electric.

7. What is the competitive benchmark for an Indian lithium cell?

Chinese LFP cells at about $75–84/kWh.

8. What does HBL Engineering mostly sell now?

Kavach railway safety systems, about half of FY26 sales, alongside specialty batteries.

9. Why is lead a stable raw material?

Most of it is recycled from used batteries; its price averaged about $1,936/t in 2026, close to recent years.

10. What is Eveready's market position?

Over half of India's dry-cell market: 58% of zinc-carbon and 18% of alkaline.
