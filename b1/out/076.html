Industry primer · 76 of 278 · Metals family, part 13

Metal Recycling

Every primer before this one started with a hole in the ground. This one starts with a scrap heap — and it is the better business. Recycling aluminium uses about a twentieth of the energy of smelting it from ore; recycling lead recovers 98% of the metal; and neither requires a mine, a mining lease or a decade of permitting. India is the world's largest importer of some metal scrap and the most under-collected market for its own, and a set of environmental rules is now dragging an enormous cash trade into the formal sector. The catch, which 2026 has made painfully clear, is that the binding constraint on this industry is not demand for recycled metal. It is getting hold of the scrap.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

A mine with no mining risk, and a sourcing problem

Explain it to a 10-year-old

When a car battery dies, there is still lead inside it — good lead, exactly the same as the lead that came out of a mine. Melting it down again takes almost no energy compared with digging up rock. So a recycler is really a miner whose mine is the pile of old batteries, wires and window frames that everyone throws away. The hard part is not melting it. The hard part is collecting enough of it.

Say it to a client in one minute

A genuine structural story having a bad year for a good reason.

  • Gravita India: Q1 FY27 revenue ₹1,475 cr (+42%), EBITDA ₹145 cr at a 9.8% margin, profit ₹106 cr (+14%) — but volumes grew only 4%, with 15–20% of Gulf scrap imports disrupted. Entered copper via the RMIL acquisition and commissioned a pilot lithium-ion line.
  • Pondy Oxides: total income ₹935 cr (+54.8%), profit ₹35.9 cr (+42.6%); secondary copper volumes more than tripled, and lead EBITDA per tonne hit a record ₹21,595 with value-added products at 85% of segment revenue.
  • The formalisation driver: the Battery Waste Management Rules 2022 set a 90% collection target for lead-acid batteries by 2026 and require registered recyclers to recover at least 98% of the lead.
  • The distortion: 18% GST on retired batteries in the formal channel still pushes volume to the informal one, and about 5,000 fake EPR certificates were reported in 2024.
  • New listings: CMR Green — India's largest aluminium recycler, about four times its nearest competitor — listed in June 2026 at a 43% premium; Jain Resource Recycling's revenue rose 76%.

That last row is the one that makes this industry different from ordinary metal conversion. A registered recycler does not only sell metal; it sells proof that metal was recycled, because the law obliges battery producers to buy that proof. Two revenue lines, one physical process — which is exactly why formalisation is the investment thesis, and why the integrity of the certificate market matters so much.

The chain in one breath

  • Somebody collects a dead battery or a bundle of scrap wire.
  • Aggregators and importers gather it into industrial quantities.
  • Plants break it down and separate the metal from everything else.
  • Furnaces remelt it using a fraction of the energy a mine-to-metal route needs.
  • Refining and alloying turn a commodity ingot into a specified product.
  • The metal and the certificate are both sold.
Fund-manager lens

Read this chain backwards and the investment case becomes obvious. Stages four and five — melting and refining — are ordinary industrial processes that anybody with capital can build. Stage one, collection, is the hard part: thousands of tiny, cash-based, geographically scattered sellers who have to be organised into a reliable supply. A recycler's real asset is its collection network, not its furnaces, which is why Gravita's disclosure that volumes grew 4% while revenue grew 42% is far more informative than the revenue line. Anyone underwriting capacity additions in this sector should first ask where the extra feed is coming from.

The audit

Five of these eight companies listed in 2022 or later, and two of those in 2026. That is not a data defect — it is the story. Recycling was a cash-based, informal trade for decades; the arrival of environmental rules with enforceable targets made it worth being a listed, audited company, and a wave of recyclers came to market. The consequence for analysis is that a fifteen-year backtest of this sector can only be run on Gravita and Pondy Oxides, and we run it on exactly those two rather than stitching in three years of a 2023 listing and calling it history.

Pondy Oxides is spliced to its BSE record back to 2006 because its NSE series begins only in March 2023. One artefact to note: the spliced series contains a single implausible move on 21 February 2007 (₹0.6 to ₹3.0). It sits well before the 2011 start of our basket and so affects nothing here, but anyone charting the full history should treat that day as a data error rather than a return.

Hariyana Ship Breakers does not belong in this section. Ship breaking recovers structural steel from decommissioned vessels — a different feedstock, different customers and a different regulatory regime (the Hong Kong Convention) from non-ferrous battery and scrap recycling. Recommend reclassifying it.

5 · How money is made

Margin per tonne, and nothing else

A recycler buys scrap at a discount to the metal price and sells metal at or near it. Revenue therefore rises and falls with the metal price whether or not the business grows at all, which makes the reported top line close to useless as a measure of progress. The three numbers that matter are:

MetricWhat it tells youWhere it stands
Volume in tonnesWhether the business is actually growingGravita +4% — the warning signal of 2026
EBITDA per tonneWhether mix and buying are improvingPondy's lead vertical at a record ₹21,595
Value-added shareWhether it sells alloys or plain ingot85% of Pondy's lead segment revenue

Why the scrap discount is the whole game

The spread between what a recycler pays for scrap and what it receives for refined metal is set by competition for feed, not by the metal price. When scrap is plentiful the discount widens and margins expand; when everybody is chasing the same containers, the discount narrows and margins compress — even as revenue looks spectacular because the metal price is high. That is precisely the 2026 configuration, and it explains an apparent contradiction on this page: Gravita's revenue rose 42% while its profit rose 14% and its EBITDA margin sat at 9.8%.

The second revenue line

EPR certificates change the arithmetic for a registered recycler. Because battery producers are legally obliged to buy documented recycling, a compliant recycler earns a fee — reported at roughly ₹5–20 per kilogram depending on chemistry and market conditions — on top of the metal. This is why formal capacity can out-earn an informal yard that pays more for the same battery: the informal operator cannot issue a certificate. It is also why the reported existence of about 5,000 fake certificates in 2024 is a genuine risk to the thesis rather than a footnote, since counterfeit paper undercuts the price of the real thing.

5b · The formalisation thesis

A cash trade being dragged into the light

India has recycled lead for decades, and has done it largely in unregistered backyard furnaces — smelting batteries with no emission control, dumping acid, and exposing workers to lead. The formal sector's opportunity is not to create a market that does not exist; it is to take share from an informal sector that already handles most of the volume. Four regulatory facts drive that shift.

  1. The Battery Waste Management Rules, 2022, notified on 22 August 2022, replaced rules that had stood since 2001 and put extended producer responsibility at the centre: producers must ensure their batteries are collected and recycled, and they demonstrate it by buying certificates from registered recyclers.
  2. The targets are specific and near. Lead-acid batteries carry a 90% collection target by 2026, and automotive and industrial lead-acid batteries a 60% material-recovery target by FY 2026–27. Registered recyclers must achieve a minimum 98% lead recovery by weight — a standard a backyard furnace cannot meet.
  3. Certificates have a price. At ₹5–20 per kilogram, the compliance stream is large enough to change the economics of paying up for feed.
  4. But the tax code works against it. Retired batteries attract 18% GST in the formal channel, which hands the informal collector an immediate price advantage on every battery it buys. This single distortion is the biggest obstacle to the whole thesis, and it is fixable with a stroke of a pen.
How to use this

Treat the regulation as a share-shift thesis, not a growth thesis. The tonnage exists already; the question is what proportion flows through audited plants that can issue certificates. That makes two things worth tracking closely: enforcement action against unregistered smelters, and any change to the GST treatment of retired batteries. Either would move the sector's volumes far more than a metal price would. And note the asymmetry with 072: India's aluminium recyclers depend on imported scrap because domestic collection is weak, while its lead recyclers compete with domestic informal collection. Same industry heading, opposite constraints.

7 · The business calendar

The scrap trade's own year

  • January–March: importers hold back before the fiscal year end, working capital is squeezed and collection slows; the weakest window at 4 wins in 15 years.
  • April: new limits, new budgets and fresh buying; 13 wins in 15 years, averaging +9.6 points — the most consistent month in this project.
  • April–July: peak import and collection season; 11 wins in 15, median +18.5.
  • August–September: still strong at 9 of 14, median +10.7, as battery replacement demand picks up before winter.
  • October–December: 8 of 14, median +8.8; battery replacement peaks in cold weather, which lifts collection a season later.

Reading it: two companies with long histories — Gravita India (from 2010) and Pondy Oxides (spliced to its BSE record back to 2006). Five of the eight names in this section listed in 2022 or later, so the basket is deliberately small. April has beaten the Nifty in 13 of 15 years — the most consistent single month found anywhere in this project — averaging +9.6 points. April–July has worked in 11 of 15 with a median of +18.5. February–March has worked 4 times in 15 (median −8.4). The mechanism is the scrap trade's own year: collection and imports pick up from April, and the January–March quarter is when working capital is squeezed and importers stop buying.

A tendency, not a rule: small samples, survivorship bias (only names still listed) and equal weighting. Use it for timing context, not as a signal.

The exceptions, and why

  • April has failed only twice in fifteen years — 2019 (−2.0) and 2020 (−0.3) — and both misses were negligible. Its best years were 2013 (+25.3), 2026 (+22.5) and 2017 (+17.9).
  • April–July's four failures were 2019 (−49.4), 2018 (−31.3), 2013 (−11.5) and 2022 (−4.0) — the two large ones are the small-cap liquidity crisis, not a seasonal effect.
  • February–March has been negative in eleven of fifteen years, with the worst clustered in 2013–2015 (−35.3, −31.2, −30.6) when imported scrap became expensive as the rupee fell.
How to use this — with one caveat

April at 13 of 15 is the strongest monthly record in the project, and unlike the April signal in 075 it has a mechanism you can name: the scrap trade restocks after the fiscal year end, and importers who stopped buying in February resume. But the basket is two companies, both of which compounded enormously over the period, so part of what looks like seasonality is a strongly trending pair being measured monthly. The safest reading is directional — the first calendar quarter is when this industry's working capital hurts and the rest of the year is when it earns — and note that the same first-quarter weakness appears in every basket across the Metals family.

9 · What moves margins

Eight drivers

  1. Scrap availability. The binding constraint, and the 2026 story.
  2. The scrap discount. What feed costs relative to refined metal — the actual margin.
  3. Metal prices. Lead, aluminium and copper; they move revenue more than profit.
  4. Value-added mix. Specified alloys against plain ingots.
  5. EPR certificate prices. The compliance revenue stream, and its integrity.
  6. Enforcement against informal smelting. The share-shift mechanism.
  7. GST treatment of retired batteries. An 18% wedge favouring the informal channel.
  8. Working capital and the cost of it. Scrap is paid for in cash, up front.

The anatomy of the cycles

  1. Discovery (2012–2017): a small, misunderstood business model was found by the market; the basket beat the index in five of six years and returned 230% in 2017 alone.
  2. The liquidity crisis (2018–2019): the IL&FS episode drained small-cap liquidity and exposed how working-capital-hungry scrap buying is; two years of −43% and −46%.
  3. The circular-economy re-rating (2020–2024): the Battery Waste Management Rules, an ESG bid and genuine capacity growth produced +192%, +68%, +88% and +172%.
  4. The supply wall (2025–2026): demand and rules intact, but scrap became the constraint; Gravita fell 15% in 2025 and the basket is down 16% this year.

Patterns that repeat

  • Bad years here are working-capital events, not demand events — 2013 (rupee), 2018–19 (liquidity), 2026 (feed supply).
  • The two names rarely fall together for the same reason: in 2015 Pondy rose 163% while Gravita fell 49%; in 2025 Pondy rose 72% while Gravita fell 15%.
  • Regulation has been a reliable tailwind, but it shows up in valuation long before it shows up in volumes.

The structural logic of the global scrap trade is that rich countries generate it and lower-cost countries process it — which has made India a natural destination, especially after China tightened its own import rules. But that flow is a privilege rather than a right: it depends on freight routes staying open, on exporting countries not restricting shipments (as the UAE and Saudi Arabia did, per 072), and on India's duty structure. All three moved against the industry in 2026 at once.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Lithium-ion recycling. The next chemistry, with a very different process — hydrometallurgy rather than smelting. Gravita has commissioned a pilot line.
  2. Copper as the growth metal. Every listed recycler is moving into it, because copper's scrap discount and end demand are both attractive; see 073.
  3. Automated sorting. Sensors and X-ray sorting to separate alloys, which raises the value of mixed scrap.
  4. Traceability systems. Digital chain-of-custody, partly to address the fake-certificate problem.
  5. Closed-loop contracts. Recyclers taking back their customers' own scrap under long-term agreements — the surest way to secure feed.
  6. Cleaner smelting. Emission control and slag treatment, which is both a compliance cost and the moat against informal competition.

What is priced in

A de-rating of the volume assumption, which is the right thing to re-price. Gravita is 40% below its September 2024 high and Jain Resource 52% below a high set only in May — and both now trade on a market that has understood the difference between 42% revenue growth and 4% volume growth. Gravita's Vision 2030 targets 20–25% annual volume growth and over 8 lakh tonnes of capacity by FY29; the June quarter delivered 4%. That gap is the entire debate, and it is a sourcing debate, not a demand one.

What the prices arguably do not reflect is the mix lever, which is working quietly. Pondy earning a record ₹21,595 per tonne in lead with 85% of that segment's revenue in value-added products shows that a recycler short of feed can still grow profit by selling better products from the same tonnes — and its copper line reaching 75% utilisation within months says the demand for secondary copper is there whenever the metal can be sourced. A business that can raise margin per tonne while volumes stall is more resilient than the share prices currently assume.

The honest read

This is the best structural story in the Metals family — energy advantage, regulatory tailwind, no mining risk, no lease queue — attached to the most fragile supply chain. Underwrite it on tonnes and EBITDA per tonne, never on revenue. The two catalysts worth waiting for are external: removal of the 18% GST wedge on retired batteries, and either the reopening of Gulf scrap flows or credible progress on domestic collection. Until one of those arrives, capacity announcements in this sector deserve scepticism rather than credit.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What were volumes in tonnes by metal, and how do they compare with plan?
  2. What was EBITDA per tonne, and what drove the change?
  3. What proportion of feed is imported, and from which regions?
  4. What is the scrap discount to the metal price, and how has it moved?
  5. What share of revenue comes from value-added alloys rather than plain ingot?
  6. How much revenue comes from EPR certificates, and at what realised price?
  7. For announced capacity: where will the incremental feed come from, and is it contracted?
  8. How many days of working capital does the business carry, and at what cost?
  9. What is the status of environmental consents at each plant?
  10. For lithium-ion: what is the pilot's recovery rate, and what would commercial scale require?

Secondary aluminium alloy
Recycled aluminium cast to an alloy specification for die casters.
Used beverage cans (UBC)
A clean, high-value aluminium scrap stream.
Dross and slag
Residues from melting, which still contain recoverable metal.
Recovery rate
The share of contained metal actually recovered — at least 98% for registered lead recyclers.
Yield
Saleable metal as a proportion of scrap charged into the furnace.

The process

Collection network
The system of dealers and aggregators that gathers scrap; the real competitive asset.
Aggregator
A trader who consolidates small lots into industrial quantities.
Battery breaking
Draining and cutting batteries to separate lead, plastic and acid.
Rotary furnace
A common furnace type for secondary lead.
Refining
Removing impurities from remelted metal.
Alloying
Adding elements to hit a customer's specification.
Hydrometallurgy
Recovering metal chemically in solution — the route for lithium-ion recycling.
Closed loop
Taking back a customer's own scrap and returning it as metal.
Informal sector
Unregistered recyclers operating without emission control or certification.
Ship breaking
Dismantling vessels for steel — a different business from non-ferrous recycling.

Regulation and economics

Extended producer responsibility (EPR)
The principle that a product's maker is responsible for its end-of-life recycling.
EPR certificate
Documentary proof of recycling that producers buy from registered recyclers.
EPR credit price
What a certificate fetches — roughly ₹5–20 per kg.
Battery Waste Management Rules, 2022
The Indian framework, notified 22 August 2022, replacing the 2001 rules.
Collection target
The share of batteries a producer must ensure is collected — 90% for lead-acid by 2026.
Material recovery target
The share of material that must be recovered — 60% for automotive and industrial lead-acid by FY 2026–27.
Registered recycler
A recycler authorised to issue EPR certificates.
CPCB
The Central Pollution Control Board, which runs the EPR portal.
Scrap discount
The gap between the scrap purchase price and the refined metal price — the true margin.
EBITDA per tonne
The industry's key profitability measure, independent of the metal price.
Value-added share
The proportion of revenue from specified alloys rather than plain ingot.
Working capital cycle
The cash gap between paying for scrap and being paid for metal.
Basic customs duty
The 2.5% import duty on scrap, plus IGST and surcharge; see 072.
Circular economy
An economy in which materials are recovered and reused rather than discarded.
Vision 2030
Gravita's plan: 20–25% volume growth a year and over 8 lakh tonnes of capacity by FY29.
Formalisation
Volume shifting from unregistered yards to audited, certified plants — the sector's thesis.
Share shift
Growth that comes from taking existing volume, not from new demand.
Chain of custody
The documented trail proving where scrap came from and what became of it.
Trans-shipment hub
A port where scrap is consolidated before onward export — the Gulf's role.
Feed security
Whether a plant has contracted scrap to run at its rated capacity.
Realisation
The price actually received per tonne, after discounts and freight.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. Why is recycled metal structurally advantaged?

It needs a fraction of the energy — about a twentieth for aluminium — and requires no mine, mining lease or decade of permitting.

2. What is a recycler's real asset?

Its collection network. Furnaces can be bought; organising thousands of small, cash-based scrap sellers cannot.

3. Why is revenue a poor measure here?

It moves with the metal price. Gravita's revenue rose 42% while volumes rose 4%.

4. What constrained 2026?

Feed. Middle East disruption cut roughly 15–20% of Gravita's normal Gulf scrap imports.

5. What are EPR certificates?

Documentary proof of recycling that battery producers must buy from registered recyclers to meet legal targets — worth roughly ₹5–20 per kg.

6. What targets do the 2022 rules set?

90% collection for lead-acid batteries by 2026, 60% material recovery for automotive and industrial lead-acid by FY 2026–27, and a minimum 98% lead recovery for registered recyclers.

7. What works against formalisation?

18% GST on retired batteries in the formal channel, which hands informal collectors a price advantage on every battery.

8. How is Pondy Oxides growing despite the feed shortage?

Mix — a record ₹21,595 EBITDA per tonne in lead with 85% of that segment in value-added products, plus tripling secondary copper volumes.

9. Why can only two companies be back-tested?

Five of the eight listed in 2022 or later; only Gravita (2010) and Pondy Oxides (BSE from 2006) have a usable long record.

10. What is the strongest calendar pattern, and why?

April — 13 wins in 15 years — because importers and collectors resume buying after the fiscal year end squeezes working capital in January–March.
