Stock Exchanges & Depositories

Every share bought in India is traded on an exchange, cleared by a clearing corporation and held in a demat account at a depository. The four listed companies in this section — BSE, MCX, CDSL and NSDL — collect a small fee at each of those steps, on a volume of activity that has multiplied as Indian households moved savings from gold, property and bank deposits into markets. They are among the most profitable businesses in the country. This primer explains how they earn, why their margins exceed 50%, how a single regulatory circular can cut their volumes, and what the listing of NSE — the largest of them all — on 24 September 2026 changes.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

A toll road on the financialisation of savings

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Think of a huge marketplace where people swap shares of companies instead of vegetables. The exchange is the market ground: it sets the rules, matches every buyer with a seller and charges a tiny fee for each swap. The depository is like a giant, very safe locker room where everyone's shares are kept, not as paper certificates but as entries in a computer, and it charges a small rent for each locker and a fee each time something leaves it. As more families in India start buying shares and mutual funds, more people use the market and the lockers, and these companies earn more — without needing to build much more.

Say it to a client in one minute

Market infrastructure is the purest listed way to own the shift of Indian savings into financial assets — with one big regulatory risk.

  • Scale: 23 crore demat accounts; 13.2 crore unique investors at NSE; NSE is the world's largest derivatives exchange by contracts traded.
  • Economics: costs are mostly fixed, so extra volume falls to profit — MCX's EBITDA margin was 72% in Q1 FY27; BSE's profit rose 62% on revenue up 63%.
  • Leaders now: MCX (bullion options +541%) and BSE (Sensex options premium +96%) are growing fastest.
  • Laggards: the depositories — CDSL profit +16%, NSDL +10% — as account openings slowed and SEBI's curbs cut derivatives trading.
  • Risk: SEBI's measures since November 2024 cut retail F&O traders from 98 lakh to 79 lakh; removing weekly expiries is reportedly under consideration.
  • Event: NSE lists on 24 September at up to ₹1,785 a share — about ₹4.4 lakh cr, 43 times FY26 profit.

The exchanges and depositories do not buy or sell anything themselves and take almost no market risk. They are platforms: the more trades, accounts and listings pass through them, the more they earn. That makes them, economically, closer to payment networks or software companies than to banks or brokers.

The chain in one breath

  • Investors place orders through brokers.
  • Exchanges match them and charge a fee.
  • Clearing corporations guarantee them.
  • Depositories move the shares into the buyer's demat account.
  • Issuers pay the depositories and registrars to keep their shareholder records.
Fund-manager lens

The critical economic feature is the network effect. Traders go where liquidity is deepest, so the leading exchange in each product tends to keep its lead: NSE in equity derivatives, MCX in commodities. Depositories are a near-duopoly protected by regulation and switching costs. Competition arises mainly when a regulator or a product change resets the game — as when BSE relaunched Sensex options in 2023 and won a meaningful share of options trading. That is why regulatory decisions matter more to these stocks than almost anything their managements do.

NSE, and the rest of the industry

The National Stock Exchange — by far the largest Indian exchange — is listing on 24 September 2026 after a ₹22,562 cr offer for sale at ₹1,700–1,785 a share, subscribed 5.7 times (institutions 12.7 times, retail 1.4 times). In FY26 it earned ₹10,302 cr on revenue from operations of ₹16,601 cr, 79% of it transaction charges; at the top of the band it is valued at about ₹4.4 lakh cr, more than three times BSE. NCDEX (agricultural commodities) and the GIFT City exchanges are unlisted. Brokers, registrars (CAMS, KFin) and asset managers sit in their own sections of the Capital Markets family.

The cost base — technology, staff, regulatory contributions and a settlement guarantee fund — changes slowly, while revenue moves with activity. So when volumes jump, margins jump: MCX's EBITDA margin rose to 72% in Q1 FY27 from 68% a year earlier, and BSE's profit is now more than half its revenue. The reverse is also true. In 2022, when IPOs dried up and trading slowed, the basket fell 12% and CDSL 26%.

Why options changed everything

Options trading is where Indian exchanges have grown fastest. A retail trader can buy a short-dated index option for a few thousand rupees, and millions did so every week after 2020. Exchanges charge on the option premium, so option volumes now dominate their revenue. That has made exchange earnings highly sensitive to the regulator's view of retail speculation — which is where the biggest risk to the sector lies.

Depositories: steadier, slower

Depositories earn from accounts and from transactions that move securities out of them. Account growth drove CDSL in 2020–2024, when crore after crore of new investors opened demat accounts; as openings slowed and trading activity cooled after SEBI's curbs, its growth fell to the mid-teens. NSDL's strength is custody value — it holds the securities of banks, funds and foreign investors — which is stickier but grows with market value, not with new retail accounts.

SEBI's study of FY26 found that retail investors' aggregate losses in equity derivatives fell to ₹91,685 cr from about ₹1.12 lakh cr a year earlier, and the number of individual traders fell from 98.1 lakh to 78.6 lakh — but the average loss per trader rose to ₹1.17 lakh. From the regulator's point of view the job is unfinished; from the exchanges' point of view, every further step is a potential cut to revenue. Yet BSE's options premium still rose 96% year on year in Q1 FY27, showing how strong underlying demand remains.

How to read it

The structural tailwind — more Indians investing — is real and durable. The cyclical-regulatory overlay — how much speculative options trading the regulator tolerates — is not. Valuing these companies means separating the two: account growth, mutual-fund flows, listings and cash trading on one side, short-dated index options on the other.

Unlike most industries in this project, there are no physical raw materials. The critical inputs are permission and trust: a licence from SEBI, the confidence of investors that trades will settle, and technology that never fails on a busy day. MCX's history shows the cost of getting technology wrong: for years it paid its former promoter's software company to run its trading system and struggled to replace it, until its new platform went live in October 2024 — after which its margins rose sharply.

7 · The business calendar

Volatility, IPO waves and expiry days

  • Weekly: expiry days (NSE Tuesday, BSE Thursday) carry a large share of options volume.
  • Event days: the Union Budget, elections, RBI and US Federal Reserve decisions, and global shocks spike volumes.
  • IPO seasons: listings cluster in September–December and February–March, lifting account openings and depository and listing fees.
  • Commodities: MCX trades until late evening, following US markets; gold, silver and crude volatility drive its volumes regardless of the Indian calendar.
  • Fiscal year start: depositories bill annual issuer charges to companies at the start of each fiscal year, based on their shareholder folios.

Because activity follows volatility and sentiment rather than the calendar, the stocks' seasonality is weaker than in most industries. The useful monthly data are operational: exchanges publish daily turnover, and depositories monthly account additions.

Seasonality test on TradingView prices

Basket: 4 names (BSE Ltd., Central Depository Services (India) Limited, Multi Commodity Exchange of India Limited, National Securities Depository Limited), equal-weighted, monthly from Mar 2017, compared with the Nifty 50.

  • Strongest months vs the Nifty: Oct +6.7 pts (beat in 7 of 9 years); Apr +5.2 pts (beat in 8 of 10 years); Sep +4.5 pts (beat in 5 of 9 years).
  • Weakest months: Feb -2.1 pts (beat in 4 of 9 years); Jan -1.5 pts (beat in 3 of 9 years); Dec -1.2 pts (beat in 3 of 9 years).
  • Windows: Feb–Mar beat the Nifty in 5 of 9 years (median +0.2 pts); Apr–Jul beat the Nifty in 7 of 10 years (median +10.9 pts); Aug–Sep beat the Nifty in 4 of 9 years (median -2.7 pts); Oct–Dec beat the Nifty in 4 of 9 years (median -0.0 pts); Sep–Nov beat the Nifty in 5 of 9 years (median +1.2 pts); Jan–Mar beat the Nifty in 5 of 9 years (median 0.0 pts).

Control: small caps as a whole (Nifty Smallcap 100) beat the Nifty in April in 11 of 15 years, by an average of 3.0 points, and lag in January–February. Part of any April strength is this market-wide effect. After removing it, this industry's own April edge is about +2.2 points.

Reading it: the basket runs from March 2017 (BSE and CDSL listed that year; MCX has traded since 2012) and adds NSDL from August 2025, so there are nine full years. April has beaten the Nifty in 8 of 10 years (average +5.2 points) and October in 7 of 9 (+6.7); April–July has worked in 7 of 10 (median +10.9). January and December have been weak (3 of 9 each). The first quarter of the year is a coin toss (January–March 5 of 9, median 0.0). The pattern is weaker than in cyclical industries because these stocks follow market activity, which follows volatility and IPO waves more than the calendar.

A tendency, not a rule: small samples, survivorship bias (only names still listed) and equal weighting. Use it for timing context, not as a signal.

The exceptions, and why

  • April–July failed in 2022 (−16.3), 2019 (−8.2) and 2018 (−2.1) — the years when IPOs and trading slowed. The best runs were 2021 (+60.5) and 2020 (+36.2), the retail-boom years.
  • October–December was strongest in 2023 (+43.8) and 2024 (+33.7) and weakest in 2020 (−15.9) and 2017 (−12.6).
  • January–March failed in 2018 (−17.4) and 2025 (−14.0), both periods of broad small- and mid-cap selling.
How to use this

For these stocks, the operating data — monthly demat additions, daily options premium, IPO pipeline — lead the share prices far better than the calendar. The April and October strength is worth knowing, but it reflects the booms of 2020–2024 more than a durable season.

9 · What moves margins

Eight drivers

  1. Options volumes. The largest single revenue driver for exchanges.
  2. SEBI's product and fee rules. Expiries, contract sizes and fee structures.
  3. Market share. Between NSE and BSE in index options; MCX's dominance in commodities.
  4. Retail participation. New accounts and active traders.
  5. IPOs and listings. Listing fees and account openings.
  6. Commodity volatility. Gold, silver and crude drive MCX.
  7. Technology costs. Platform upgrades and outages.
  8. Treasury income. Interest rates on large cash balances.

The anatomy of the cycles

  1. Crisis and reset (2013–2016): the NSEL scandal, MCX's change of ownership and stricter rules for market infrastructure ownership.
  2. Listing and the first retail wave (2017–2019): BSE and CDSL listed; the 2018 small-cap crash cut them hard.
  3. The retail boom (2020–2021): pandemic-era account openings and IPOs; CDSL +139% and +181%, BSE +210% in 2021.
  4. The options era (2023–2025): Sensex options, commodity options and MCX's new platform; the basket rose 148% and 113% in 2023–24.
  5. Regulation bites (2024–2026): SEBI's curbs slow retail trading; depositories lag; NSE comes to market.

Patterns that repeat

  • Operating leverage works both ways: 2022 (−12%) and 2018 (−29%) show how fast earnings expectations fall when activity slows.
  • Product launches re-rate exchanges: Sensex options for BSE, bullion options for MCX.
  • Governance shocks are existential: MCX lost two-thirds of its value in 2013 over its promoter's other business.

By contracts traded, NSE has been the world's largest derivatives exchange for several years, driven by retail index options. That scale is also the concern: in most developed markets, options are a smaller share of activity and retail speculation is less concentrated. Over time, global exchanges have diversified into data, indices and post-trade services, which are steadier than trading fees. BSE's index business, NSE's indices and data, and the depositories' account-based fees are the Indian equivalents — still small relative to transaction charges.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Faster settlement. India moved to T+1 settlement in 2023 and has piloted same-day settlement.
  2. Mobile-first investing. Discount-broker apps brought millions of first-time investors.
  3. Electronic everything. e-KYC, e-voting and consolidated account statements reduce costs.
  4. Cloud and resilience. Regulators demand disaster recovery and near-zero downtime.
  5. Surveillance and AI. Detecting manipulation, especially around expiries.
  6. GIFT City. International exchanges for dollar-denominated trading.

What is priced in

On Q1 FY27 profit annualised, BSE trades at about 38 times, MCX about 50 times, CDSL about 58 times and NSDL about 40 times; NSE is coming at about 43 times FY26 profit. Those multiples assume that the structural growth in investors continues and that regulation does not cut deeper into options. The market is already discriminating: MCX, whose growth comes from commodities and is less exposed to the equity-options debate, has held near its high; BSE is 26% below its May peak as weekly-expiry risks were discussed; the depositories, whose growth has slowed, have de-rated. NSE's listing also gives investors a larger, more liquid alternative, which may pull money away from BSE.

The honest read

These are exceptional businesses — near-monopolies with high margins, little capital need and a decades-long tailwind. But their current earnings are unusually dependent on short-dated options trading that the regulator is actively trying to shrink. The durable parts — accounts, mutual-fund flows, listings, custody, data — grow steadily; the volatile part — options — can change with one circular. Owning them well means paying a multiple that reflects both.

16 · Adjacent industries

Where this connects

  • Stockbroking: the brokers who bring investors to the exchanges; their revenue is even more options-dependent.
  • Asset management: mutual-fund flows drive depository and registrar activity.
  • Registrars and fund services: CAMS and KFin, the record-keepers for mutual funds and companies.
  • Wealth management and insurance distribution: the other routes into financial savings.
  • Payments and fintech: NSDL's payments bank; app-based distribution.
  • Global proxies: CME, ICE and HKEX.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What share of revenue comes from index options, and from weekly expiries in particular?
  2. What would a removal of weekly expiries mean for volumes and revenue?
  3. How is market share in index options moving, and why?
  4. What is the cost base's fixed and variable split?
  5. How much revenue comes from non-trading sources — data, indices, listing, platforms?
  6. How fast are new accounts growing, and how many are active?
  7. What regulatory fees and settlement-fund contributions are expected this year?
  8. What technology investment is planned, and what are the resilience targets?
  9. For MCX: how concentrated are volumes in gold and silver, and what new products are planned?
  10. For NSDL: how much of growth and profit comes from the payments bank?

Every term you'll meet

Market structure

Exchange
A regulated marketplace that matches buy and sell orders.
Clearing corporation
The entity that guarantees trades by standing between buyer and seller.
Depository
An institution that holds securities electronically.
Depository participant (DP)
A broker or bank that opens demat accounts on behalf of a depository.
Demat account
An electronic account holding shares, bonds and fund units.
Beneficial owner (BO) account
The depositories' term for a demat account.
Custody value
The market value of securities held in a depository.
Market infrastructure institution (MII)
SEBI's term for exchanges, clearing corporations and depositories.
Registrar and transfer agent (RTA)
Keeps shareholder or unitholder records for companies and funds.
KRA
KYC registration agency that stores investors' identity records.
Unique client code (UCC)
An investor's trading-account identifier at an exchange.
GIFT City
India's international financial services centre in Gujarat.

Trading and products

Cash segment
Buying and selling shares for delivery.
Futures
Contracts to buy or sell at a set price on a future date.
Options
Contracts giving the right, not the obligation, to buy or sell.
Option premium
The price paid for an option; exchanges charge on it.
Notional turnover
The full contract value of options traded, far larger than premium.
Expiry
The day a derivative contract ends.

Options that expire every week, heavily traded by retail.
Index derivatives
Futures and options on indices such as the Nifty or Sensex.
Average daily turnover (ADTV)

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. What does a depository do?

It holds securities electronically in demat accounts and records changes in ownership.

2. Why are exchange margins so high?

Costs are largely fixed while revenue rises with volume.

3. What did BSE report in Q1 FY27?

Revenue ₹1,566 cr (+63%), profit ₹873 cr (+62%); options premium +96%.

4. What drove MCX's Q1 FY27 growth?

Options, especially bullion options (+541%); profit +103% at a 72% EBITDA margin.

5. What happened to MCX in 2013?

The NSEL payment crisis, linked to its promoter, cut the stock by two-thirds.

6. What were SEBI's main derivatives measures from November 2024?

One weekly expiry per exchange, larger contract sizes, upfront premium collection, extra expiry-day margin and no calendar-spread benefit on expiry.

7. How did retail F&O participation change in FY26?

Individual traders fell from 98.1 lakh to 78.6 lakh and aggregate losses to ₹91,685 cr.

8. How many demat accounts are there, and how are they split?

About 23 crore — 18.6 crore at CDSL and 4.6 crore at NSDL.

9. What are the terms of NSE's IPO?

A ₹22,562 cr offer for sale at ₹1,700–1,785, 5.7 times subscribed, listing on 24 September 2026.

10. What is the biggest open risk to exchange earnings?

Further SEBI curbs on short-dated options, such as removing weekly expiries.
