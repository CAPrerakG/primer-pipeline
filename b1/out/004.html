Inorganic & Commodity Chemicals

Chemicals made from minerals and the sea rather than oil: sulphuric acid, bromine, boric acid, zinc oxide, aluminium fluoride, copper salts, phosphorus chemicals, industrial salt. Each is its own small world, tied to one mineral and a few customer industries.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Rocks and sea water turned into ingredients

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Some chemicals don't come from oil at all. They come from rocks, metals, salt and sea water. Yellow sulphur becomes sulphuric acid, the most-used chemical in the world, needed for fertiliser and batteries. Sea water from the Rann of Kutch gives bromine, which stops things catching fire. A white rock from Turkey gives boric acid, used in glass and medicine. Zinc metal gets burnt into zinc oxide, which helps tyres last. Copper becomes copper sulphate for farms. Each company usually makes one or two of these and sells them to a few big customers. When a war or a Chinese factory shutdown cuts supply of one mineral, that company's price can jump overnight.

Say it to a client in one minute

Inorganics are a collection of mineral-based niches. Each company's economics depend on access to a specific resource (brine in Kutch, Turkish borates, sulphur from Gulf refineries, zinc and copper metal) and a conversion margin over it. Supply shocks matter more than demand. Sulphur more than doubled in 2025 and hit $1,000+/t CFR India in 2026 as the Hormuz crisis cut Gulf supply; India banned sulphur exports in July and is weighing a subsidy for fertiliser makers. Bromine realisations jumped ~50% for Archean. The best businesses control a scarce resource (Archean's Kutch brine) or are the dominant domestic converter (JG Chemicals in zinc oxide, Indo Borax in boric acid). The weakest are traders and small acid makers with no feedstock edge. The stocks are small and speculative: Sanginita rose about seven-fold this year without matching news.

Everyday examples

The car battery under your bonnet is filled with sulphuric acid; the fire-resistant plastic around your laptop's circuit board uses bromine; the Borosil glass in your kitchen needs boric acid; the white powder that helps your tyres cure is zinc oxide; the blue powder a farmer sprays on grapes is copper sulphate; the aluminium in a soft-drink can was smelted with aluminium fluoride.

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Resource: like owning the only well in a desert village: whoever owns the brine or the mine can sell water at a good price when others run dry.
  • Primary chemical: the well owner (or a buyer of imported rock) turns the raw stuff into a usable powder or liquid.
  • Derivatives: cooking that powder into a more specific ingredient a medicine or fire-retardant maker needs; harder, so better paid.
  • Customers: a handful of big factories (tyres, fertiliser, aluminium) that buy in bulk and bargain hard.

Fund-manager lens

In inorganics, ask one question first: does this company own or control its key raw material, or does it buy it at world prices? Resource owners earn rents in tight markets. Converters earn a spread that competition and dumping erode. The 2025–26 sulphur shock is a live example: acid buyers were squeezed, smelters producing acid as a by-product benefited, and a sulphur burner with pricing power (Amal) did well because acid itself was scarce.

Resource rent or conversion spread

Two mechanics: pass-through lag (contract prices reset monthly or quarterly, so fast raw-material moves hurt or help for a quarter), and working capital tied to metal prices (a zinc or copper rally inflates receivables and inventory without raising margins).

Analogy

A resource owner is like a farmer who owns a mango orchard: in a bad year for everyone else, his mangoes fetch double. A converter is like a juice shop buying mangoes at the market: when mangoes get expensive, his margin shrinks unless customers accept a higher price for juice.

6 · The business calendar

Few seasonal patterns; the monsoon matters for sea salt

  • Solar evaporation (salt and bromine from brine) runs October to June; the monsoon halts pans. Heavy rain years hurt output.
  • Fertiliser demand for sulphuric and phosphoric acid peaks before kharif and rabi sowing.
  • Farm fungicides (copper salts) peak before and during the monsoon.
  • Tyre production follows auto cycles and festive demand.
  • Contract resets: quarterly for many inorganic contracts (phosphoric acid CFR India, borates); monthly Gulf sulphur prices (ADNOC, Qatar).

7 · What the stocks do

Seasonality test on TradingView prices

Basket: 6 names (Archean Chemical Industries Ltd., JG Chemicals Limited, Indo Borax & Chemicals Limited, Amal Ltd., Sanginita Chemicals Ltd., Alufluoride Ltd), equal-weighted, monthly from Jul 2011, compared with the Nifty 50.

  • Strongest months vs the Nifty: Apr +9.2 pts (beat in 13 of 15 years); Jul +6.2 pts (beat in 13 of 16 years); Aug +6.0 pts (beat in 12 of 16 years).
  • Weakest months: May -4.6 pts (beat in 3 of 15 years); Mar -2.2 pts (beat in 4 of 15 years); Jan -0.5 pts (beat in 8 of 15 years).
  • Windows: Feb–Mar beat the Nifty in 4 of 15 years (median -6.1 pts); Apr–Jul beat the Nifty in 10 of 15 years (median +13.6 pts); Aug–Sep beat the Nifty in 10 of 14 years (median +6.3 pts); Oct–Dec beat the Nifty in 8 of 14 years (median +3.4 pts); Sep–Nov beat the Nifty in 8 of 14 years (median +3.8 pts); Jan–Mar beat the Nifty in 3 of 15 years (median -7.5 pts).

Control: small caps as a whole (Nifty Smallcap 100) beat the Nifty in April in 11 of 15 years, by an average of 3.0 points, and lag in January–February. Part of any April strength is this market-wide effect. After removing it, this industry's own April edge is about +6.2 points.

Reading it: the long history comes from older small caps (Amal, Alufluoride, Indo Borax) with BSE prices going back to 2011; Sanginita joined in 2020, Archean in 2023 and JG Chemicals in 2024. April (13 of 15), July (13 of 16) and August (12 of 16) are the reliable months; May is the weakest (3 of 15), and January–March beat the Nifty in only 3 of 15 years. With such small, illiquid names, treat the pattern as small-cap behaviour more than a chemistry signal.

A tendency, not a rule: small samples, survivorship bias (only names still listed) and equal weighting. Use it for timing context, not as a signal.

The exceptions, and why

  • April misses (2013: −7.9 pts; 2019: −4.2). The rupee crisis and the 2019 slowdown. Every other April beat the Nifty.
  • July misses (2019: −10.2; 2013: −6.9). The same two bad years; the biggest Julys were 2025 (+19.4), 2021 (+18.0) and 2026 (+13.8), each during a raw-material squeeze (sulphur, China's energy curbs, Hormuz).
  • May is the weak month (3 of 15). The worst were 2017 (−16.5, after a huge run) and 2022 (−15.6, the global sell-off as prices peaked).
  • April–July failures (2013, 2017, 2018, 2019, 2022). Market-wide small-cap weakness in each case; 2017's miss came after a +60-point January–March.
  • January–March only works in shock years: 2017 (+60.3, Chinese closures), 2021 (+18.0) and 2026 (the Hormuz crisis).
  • The worst October–December (2020: −29.2) was Sanginita's collapse after its early-2020 peak, not an industry event.
How to use this

For these small caps, the calendar is mostly the small-cap calendar. The durable signal is the raw-material squeeze: when a mineral gets scarce (sulphur in 2025–26, Chinese supply in 2017 and 2021), the owner or the dominant domestic converter re-rates. Watch sulphur, bromine and borate prices, not the month.

8 · What moves demand and margins

Supply shocks dominate

  • Gulf sulphur supply: the Gulf is the world's largest sulphur exporter, so the 2026 Hormuz disruption sent sulphur to record levels (ADNOC set a record $860/t June price; Indian CFR $1,000–1,100/t in August). India halted sulphur exports in July and is considering a 20% subsidy on sulphur and acid for fertiliser makers.
  • Chinese resource constraints: Shandong brine depletion tightens bromine; Chinese environmental curbs periodically cut output of many inorganics; China controls phosphate exports.
  • Metal prices: LME zinc for zinc oxide; copper for copper salts; aluminium smelter output for AlF₃ demand.
  • Supplier power: Eti Maden's pricing decides boron costs worldwide.
  • End demand: tyres (autos), fertilisers (monsoon, subsidy), flame retardants (electronics, construction), oilfield activity (clear brine fluids), energy storage (bromine).
  • Logistics: Archean's salt exports depend on road access and shipping; Q1 FY27 volumes fell 12% on vessel shortages.
  • Import duties and anti-dumping cases on specific chemicals; new domestic capacity (Paradeep's AlF₃, JG's Dahej zinc oxide).

The anatomy of an inorganic-chemical cycle

  1. A supply shock hits one mineral (Chinese plant closures in 2017, energy curbs in 2021, the Gulf sulphur disruption in 2025–26).
  2. Owners and dominant converters earn windfalls; small caps multiply (Alufluoride +362% in 2017; Indo Borax +204% in 2021).
  3. Supply returns or buyers substitute (China reopens, other producers add capacity, government subsidises users).
  4. Prices normalise and the stocks give back gains (2018, 2022), often before earnings fall.

Patterns that repeat

  • Listings mark tops. Archean listed at the 2022 bromine peak and has fallen 43% from its 2024 high.
  • Scarcity rewards the scarce thing. In 2025–26 sulphur scarcity helped the acid maker (Amal) but hurt the acid users (fertiliser makers).
  • Micro-cap moves can be disconnected from the business. Sanginita's 2020 collapse (−81%) and 2026 surge (+595%) came without matching fundamentals.
  • New capacity follows fat margins. Paradeep's AlF₃ plant and JG's Dahej zinc oxide plant are the next test for incumbents.

11 · Where technology is going

Five shifts

  • Energy storage: zinc-bromine flow batteries (Archean has invested in a battery start-up) and lithium chemicals; bromine demand from energy storage is growing from a small base.
  • Specialty derivatives: moving from bulk bromine, boron or phosphorus into pharma- and agro-grade derivatives with customer approvals (Acume turned EBITDA-positive; Indo Borax's pharma-grade boric acid and Kronox deal).
  • Recovery and recycling: zinc from galvanising ash and battery scrap; acid regeneration; copper from scrap.
  • Waste-heat power: sulphuric acid plants co-generate steam and power, a hidden margin lever.
  • Better brine processing: recovering potash, magnesium and lithium from bittern.

16 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. Do you own or contract your key raw material, and for how long?
  2. What is the realisation vs the Chinese or global benchmark this quarter?
  3. How fast do contract prices pass through raw-material changes?
  4. What share of revenue is derivatives vs bulk?
  5. What are customer concentration (top 5) and export share?
  6. For resource owners: what did the monsoon and logistics do to production and shipments?
  7. (Indo Borax) What is the strategic logic and valuation of the Kronox deal?

Red flags

  • Margins riding a temporary commodity spike, presented as structural.
  • Capex in bulk products where China has surplus.
  • Working capital ballooning with metal prices.
  • Trading revenue presented as manufacturing.
  • Share-price surges with no business news.

17 · Myths and live debates

What you'll hear, and the evidence

ClaimEvidence
"Bromine demand from batteries will transform the industry."Zinc-bromine storage is early-stage; flame retardants, oilfield fluids and pharma still dominate demand.
"High sulphur prices are good for acid makers."Only for smelter-acid producers and acid sellers with pricing power. Sulphur burners pay more for their key input, and fertiliser makers are squeezed.
"Zinc oxide margins rise with zinc prices."It is a conversion fee; higher zinc mostly inflates revenue and working capital.
"Archean is a pure bromine play."Industrial salt is a large volume business with its own logistics risks (Q1 FY27's 12% volume drop).
"Sanginita's rally reflects the business."I found no results or announcements explaining a seven-fold rise; it's a micro cap with thin trading.

19 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. Why did the Iran war raise sulphur prices?

The Gulf is the largest sulphur exporter, as a by-product of its oil and gas, and Hormuz disruption cut supply.

2. Who benefits from high sulphur prices?

Producers of smelter acid (zinc and copper smelters) and acid sellers with pricing power; sulphur-burning acid makers without pricing power and fertiliser producers lose.

3. What is Archean's moat?

Access to Rann of Kutch brine for bromine, industrial salt and SOP: a resource others cannot easily replicate.

4. Why doesn't a zinc rally lift zinc oxide margins?

The margin is a conversion fee; zinc cost passes through, raising revenue and working capital instead.

5. Who sets world boron prices?

Turkey's state-owned Eti Maden, with ~60% of supply.

6. Why does a long monsoon hurt salt and bromine output?

Both rely on solar evaporation of brine, which stops in heavy rain.

7. Why did Archean's salt volumes fall in Q1 FY27?

Transport disruption (road works, diesel costs), vessel shortages and customer order deferrals.

8. What new competitor faces Alufluoride?

Paradeep Phosphates, which is building a ₹250 cr aluminium fluoride plant.

9. What policy did India adopt for sulphur in July 2026?

It halted sulphur exports to keep supply for fertiliser makers; a sulphur and acid subsidy is also under consideration.

10. Which year was the basket's biggest outlier, and why?

2017 (+225%): China's environmental inspections shut many inorganic plants, and Indian producers like Alufluoride, Amal and Indo Borax gained pricing.
