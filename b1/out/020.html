Tractors

India buys more tractors than any other country: about a third of the world's volume. A tractor is a farmer's biggest purchase, made with harvest cash and a loan, so the industry runs on rainfall, crop prices and credit. The listed makers are a small part of the market; the biggest player sits inside Mahindra.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

The farmer's biggest purchase

Explain it to a 10-year-old

A tractor is a farm's engine. It ploughs the field, pulls a trailer, runs a pump and sometimes earns money hauling bricks or sand for neighbours. It costs as much as a small car, so a farmer buys one only after a good harvest, usually with a loan repaid over five years. If the rains are good, crops sell well and everyone buys tractors. If the rains fail, sales drop hard the following year. That is why tractor companies watch the sky as closely as their factories.

Say it to a client in one minute

Tractors are a rural credit cycle with a weather trigger. FY26 was a record: 11.3–11.5 lakh units, up 23.5%, helped by a 108% monsoon, the GST cut from 12% to 5% in September 2025, and pre-buying before new emission norms. FY27 is the payback year, with ICRA forecasting 1–4% growth and the monsoon forecast at 90% of the long-period average.

  • Mahindra (in 017) leads with over 41% share including its Swaraj division; TAFE, Sonalika and John Deere are unlisted here.
  • Escorts Kubota is the listed pure play, now controlled by Japan's Kubota, gaining share (Q1 FY27 tractor sales +20.5%).
  • Swaraj Engines makes engines for Mahindra's Swaraj tractors, a quiet compounder.
  • VST Tillers owns the power-tiller and small-machinery niche.
  • Indo Farm is a small maker of tractors and pick-and-carry cranes, listed in January 2025.

The stocks have already de-rated 25–34% in 2026 for the coming slowdown, while volumes are still growing and margins are falling.

Analogy

A tractor is to a farmer what a delivery van is to a shopkeeper: a tool bought to earn, not to enjoy. It is also a status symbol at village weddings, and a rental business on the side. That mix of income, credit and pride is why sales swing so hard with the harvest.

Everyday examples

The red Mahindra ploughing a field outside a highway town. The Swaraj hauling sugarcane to a mill. The John Deere with an air-conditioned cab on a large Punjab farm. The VST power tiller in a Kerala paddy field. The yellow Escorts backhoe digging a village drain under a government works scheme.

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Materials: a tractor is heavy steel, because it has to pull a plough through hard soil without lifting off the ground.
  • Engines: the diesel engine and gearbox are the expensive parts. Some companies buy them; Mahindra's Swaraj tractors use engines from a separate listed company, Swaraj Engines.
  • Assembly: the factory bolts the engine, gearbox, axles and big rear wheels onto a frame.
  • Dealers and finance: a village dealer arranges the loan; the farmer pays back after harvests.
  • Use: many farmers rent their tractor out to neighbours, so it earns money even when their own field is idle.
Fund-manager lens

Tractors look like an auto business but behave like rural credit. Three things decide the year: rainfall and crop cash flows (can the farmer pay?), lender appetite (will someone finance 80% of it?), and any tax or emission-rule change that shifts timing. Market share moves slowly because the dealer network and resale value matter more than the product. Watch the gap between wholesale dispatches and FADA retail registrations to spot channel stuffing in a weak year.

What makes a good year

  1. Two good seasons in a row: a strong kharif harvest (October–November cash) followed by a good rabi (March–April cash) is what really lifts sales.
  2. Financing: loan approval rates and NBFC appetite; a rural credit squeeze can cut sales even after a good harvest.
  3. Non-farm demand: roughly a fifth of tractor use is haulage and construction, so infrastructure spending cushions bad crop years.
  4. Mix and price: higher horsepower and cabs raise realisations; the GST cut effectively lowered on-road prices by about 6–7%.
Analogy

Selling tractors is like selling sewing machines to tailors. The customer is buying a way to earn, so he waits for a good season and a cheap loan. Once bought, he uses it for a decade and buys spare parts every year, which is where the steady profit lives.

7 · The business calendar

Two harvests and a monsoon

  • April–June: pre-monsoon buying for kharif sowing; dealers stock up. The strongest window for the stocks.
  • June–September (monsoon): sowing; sales depend on how the rains spread. The first IMD forecast (April) sets sentiment.
  • October–November: the kharif harvest brings cash, and festive buying (Dhanteras and Diwali are auspicious for a new tractor) peaks.
  • December–February: rabi sowing and a quieter stretch for sales.
  • March: a year-end push and rabi harvest cash in some states.
  • Monthly data: company dispatches on the 1st; FADA retail registrations mid-month.

8 · What the stocks do

Seasonality test on TradingView prices

Basket: 4 names (Escorts Kubota Limited, Swaraj Engines Ltd, VST Tillers Tractors Limited, Indo Farm Equipment Ltd.), equal-weighted, monthly from Jul 2011, compared with the Nifty 50.

  • Strongest months vs the Nifty: Apr +6.8 pts (beat in 13 of 15 years); Jun +5.7 pts (beat in 10 of 15 years); Aug +2.8 pts (beat in 9 of 16 years).
  • Weakest months: Feb -1.9 pts (beat in 3 of 15 years); Mar -1.9 pts (beat in 3 of 15 years); Jul -1.0 pts (beat in 7 of 16 years).
  • Windows: Feb–Mar beat the Nifty in 3 of 15 years (median -5.0 pts); Apr–Jul beat the Nifty in 12 of 15 years (median +16.8 pts); Aug–Sep beat the Nifty in 11 of 14 years (median +4.5 pts); Oct–Dec beat the Nifty in 7 of 14 years (median 0.0 pts); Sep–Nov beat the Nifty in 6 of 14 years (median -1.1 pts); Jan–Mar beat the Nifty in 5 of 15 years (median -8.5 pts).

Control: small caps as a whole (Nifty Smallcap 100) beat the Nifty in April in 11 of 15 years, by an average of 3.0 points, and lag in January–February. Part of any April strength is this market-wide effect. After removing it, this industry's own April edge is about +3.8 points.

Reading it: this is the cleanest seasonal pattern in the library so far. April beat the Nifty in 13 of 15 years and April–July in 12 of 15, with a median of +16.8 points: the market buys tractor stocks ahead of the monsoon and the kharif sowing season. February–March is the opposite (3 of 15, median −5.0), and January–March is worse (5 of 15, median −8.5), when the rabi harvest is still uncertain and the sales year is ending. October–December is a coin toss (7 of 14).

A tendency, not a rule: small samples, survivorship bias (only names still listed) and equal weighting. Use it for timing context, not as a signal.

The exceptions, and why

  • April–July failed only three times: 2012 (−4.8), 2018 (−15.5) and 2019 (−25.4). 2012 was a failing monsoon; 2018 and 2019 were the rural distress and NBFC credit freeze, when no forecast could help. The biggest runs were 2020 (+46.6, the COVID rural boom) and 2016 (+38.9, the monsoon recovery after two droughts).
  • April misses: 2019 (−4.9) and 2025 (−4.9). The best Aprils were 2016 (+21.7), 2024 (+12.8) and 2022 (+11.7).
  • February–March beat the Nifty in only 3 of 15 years; the exceptions were 2018 (+11.1) and 2017 (+8.3).
  • October–December misses: 2020 (−22.2) after the huge summer run, and 2014 (−15.7) when the drought became undeniable. The best was 2013 (+39.9), a bumper harvest year.
How to use this

This basket has the library's most consistent seasonal edge: own it from April to July, avoid January to March. The logic is real, because the market prices the monsoon forecast and sowing expectations before volumes arrive. The rule fails when credit breaks (2018–19), so check rural lending and dealer inventory before trusting it. In 2026 the window worked in a small way (+5.0 points) even in a de-rating year.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Nine drivers

  1. Monsoon and its spread: the first forecast in April moves the stocks; the distribution across districts moves the volumes.
  2. Crop prices and MSP: what the harvest earns decides the down payment.
  3. Rural credit: around 80% of tractors are financed; NBFC and bank appetite is decisive (2018–19 proved it).
  4. Taxes: GST cut from 12% to 5% in September 2025 lowered on-road prices and pulled demand forward.
  5. Emission norms: TREM stages. The draft moves above-75 HP and below-25 HP to TREM V from October 2026, while 25–75 HP is deferred to 2032 (with TREM IIIAA for 25–50 HP in 2028). Deadlines cause pre-buying, then a hole.
  6. Non-farm use: haulage and construction demand, and government works programmes.
  7. Replacement cycle: tractors last 8–12 years, so the FY21 record cohort will come up for replacement.
  8. Commodity costs: steel and rubber, with a one- to two-quarter lag.
  9. Exports: compact tractors to the US and Africa; Kubota's network for Escorts, and Mahindra's and TAFE's own.

The anatomy of a tractor cycle

  1. A good monsoon lifts crop output and cash; sales rise for two to three years as confidence and credit build (2016–17, 2020–21, 2025–26).
  2. The stocks front-run it, rising on the forecast before the volumes appear.
  3. A shock arrives: a drought (2014–15), a credit freeze (2018–19) or simply a high base after a stimulus (2026).
  4. De-rating: the stocks fall 20–35% while volumes are still growing, then bottom before the volume trough.

Patterns that repeat

  • Stocks lead volumes by two to three quarters, in both directions. 2026 is a textbook example: volumes up 19% in April–July, stocks down 25%.
  • A tax or norm change pulls demand forward and creates a hole the next year (GST 2025, TREM pre-buying).
  • Two droughts in a row do the real damage (2014 and 2015), because the second one exhausts savings.
  • Share shifts are slow but persistent: Sonalika and Escorts have been gaining, TAFE slipping.

11 · The global picture

India sells the most; the West sells the biggest

India is the world's largest tractor market by units, roughly a third of global volume, but its average tractor is small (35–50 HP) and cheap. North America and Europe buy far fewer, far larger machines with cabs, GPS guidance and precision equipment, at many times the price. That split explains the industry's structure: Indian makers dominate at home and in other developing markets, while Deere, CNH and AGCO dominate high-horsepower machines and technology.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • Imports to local champions (1970s–1990s): licences and then liberalisation built Mahindra, TAFE, Escorts and Punjab Tractors (whose Swaraj brand Mahindra later bought).
  • Japanese entry (2010s–2020s): Kubota invested in Escorts in 2020 and took control in 2022, bringing technology and export access.
  • Value players gaining: Sonalika has grown share steadily with aggressive pricing and exports.
  • Technology moving down: GPS guidance, cabins and precision implements are entering the Indian 50 HP-plus segment.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Emission norms (TREM): the deferral for 25–75 HP to 2032 avoids a big cost step for the volume segment, while larger and very small tractors move to TREM V from October 2026.
  2. Electric tractors: BIS has issued India's first testing standards, and 40–60 HP pilots can run six to eight hours on a charge. Adoption is still negligible, with fewer than 100 registered as of 2024 against an installed base of over 9 million.
  3. Precision farming: GPS auto-steer, laser levellers and variable-rate spraying, still mostly on large farms.
  4. Rental and custom hiring: app-based tractor rental and government-funded custom-hiring centres let small farmers use machines they cannot buy.
  5. Drones and implements: spraying drones and better implements (rotavators, seeders, balers) raise the value of each tractor.
  6. Compact tractors for export: the US hobby-farm segment has become a big market for Indian-built compacts.

The honest read

The market has already paid for the slowdown, which is what tractor stocks always do first. The question for the next two quarters is how bad the monsoon shortfall actually was by district and whether the GST cut's demand pull-forward leaves a hole in H2 FY27. Escorts has the cleanest exposure and is gaining share; Swaraj Engines is the low-drama way to own Mahindra's volumes; VST is a niche with better structural growth than the industry.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What are dealer inventory days, and how do wholesale dispatches compare with retail registrations?
  2. How is demand splitting between states, and how has the monsoon shortfall affected bookings?
  3. What is the horsepower mix, and how much of growth is price versus volume?
  4. How much margin was lost to commodities, and what pricing has been taken?
  5. What share of sales is financed, and have approval rates or delinquencies changed?
  6. (Escorts) What is Kubota adding in technology and exports, and what is the ₹900 cr capex for?
  7. (VST) How much of power-tiller demand depends on subsidies?
  8. How are you preparing for TREM V in the segments that move in October 2026?

Red flags

  • Dispatches running well ahead of FADA retail.
  • Rising discounts and free-insurance offers in a "good" quarter.
  • Receivable days stretching at the dealer level.
  • Capacity expansion announced at the top of a rural cycle.

18 · Myths and live debates

What you'll hear, and the evidence

ClaimEvidence
"A bad monsoon means a bad year for tractor stocks."Usually, but not always: 2014 and 2015 were drought years and the stocks still rose on recovery hopes. The stocks trade the forecast, not the rainfall.
"The GST cut changed the industry's growth rate."It lowered prices about 6–7% and pulled demand forward into FY26; FY27 growth is forecast at just 1–4%.
"Electric tractors are about to disrupt this market."Fewer than 100 were registered as of 2024 against 9 million diesel tractors. Standards now exist, but diesel torque, price and resale value still win.
"Escorts is losing to Mahindra."Its Q1 FY27 domestic volumes grew 22.9% against industry growth of 18.6%, and its August retail share rose to 11.09%.
"Tractor demand is purely agricultural."Roughly a fifth of use is haulage and construction, which is why infrastructure spending cushions weak crop years.

19 · Glossary

Every term you'll meet

Machines and farming

HP (horsepower)
The standard way tractors are segmented; India's volume segment is 25–50 HP.
PTO
Power take-off: the shaft that drives implements such as rotavators and pumps.
Power tiller
A walk-behind two-wheeled machine for small and wet-land farms; VST's speciality.
Power weeder
A smaller machine for weeding between rows, popular in horticulture.
Implement
A tool towed or driven by a tractor: plough, rotavator, seed drill, trailer.
Rotavator
A rotating-blade implement that prepares soil in one pass.
Custom hiring centre
A government-supported facility that rents machinery to small farmers.
Backhoe loader
A digger with a loader bucket, sold by Escorts and others.
Pick-and-carry crane
A mobile crane used at construction sites; Indo Farm's second business.
2WD / 4WD
Two- or four-wheel drive; 4WD costs more but pulls better in wet or heavy soil and is growing in India.
Lift capacity
How much weight the tractor's hydraulic arms can raise; the headline spec farmers compare after horsepower.
Tillage
Breaking and preparing soil before sowing; primary tillage (ploughing) and secondary tillage (levelling).
Zero-till seeder / happy seeder
An implement that sows into unploughed soil and crop residue, saving diesel and reducing stubble burning.
Combine harvester
A self-propelled machine that cuts and threshes grain in one pass; usually owned by contractors, not farmers.
Compact tractor
An under-30 HP machine; India's big export product for American hobby farms.
Sowing acreage
The area actually planted in a season; the weekly official number during kharif.
Horticulture mechanisation
Machinery for orchards and vegetables, the fastest-growing use for small tractors and weeders.
Loan-to-value (LTV) / down payment
How much of the tractor the lender funds, and what the farmer pays upfront from harvest cash.
Delinquency
Missed loan instalments; rising delinquencies make lenders tighten and sales fall.
Kharif / rabi
The monsoon crop season / the winter crop season (see 014).
MSP
Minimum support price: the government's guaranteed purchase price for key crops.
Mandi
The regulated wholesale market where farmers sell produce.
Farm mechanisation
The share of farm work done by machines rather than manual labour.
Installed base
Tractors already in use; over 9 million in India, the source of spare-parts demand.

Regulation and the cycle

TREM
Tractor Emission Norms: India's emission stages for agricultural tractors.
TREM V
The stage requiring after-treatment systems; drafted for above 75 HP and below 25 HP from October 2026.
TREM IIIA / IIIAA
The current stage, and an intermediate step planned for 25–50 HP in 2028.
Pre-buying
Buying ahead of a norm or price change (see 019).
SMAM
The central farm-mechanisation subsidy scheme.
PM-Kisan
Direct income support to farmers.
LPA
Long-period average rainfall; "normal" is 96–104% (see 014).
El Niño
A Pacific warming pattern that often weakens the Indian monsoon.
FADA
The dealers' association, which publishes retail registration data (see 017).
Wholesale vs retail
Factory dispatches vs actual farmer purchases (see 017).
Custom hiring / rental income
Money a farmer earns renting his tractor to neighbours, which helps repay the loan.
Residual value
The resale price of a used tractor; high residuals make financing easier.

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. Why did FY26 tractor sales hit a record?

A 108% monsoon, the GST cut from 12% to 5% in September 2025, and pre-buying ahead of emission norms lifted volumes 23.5% to 11.3–11.5 lakh units.

2. Why are the stocks down 25–34% in 2026 while volumes grow?

Tractor stocks trade the forecast: ICRA sees FY27 growth of only 1–4%, the monsoon forecast is 90% of LPA, and margins are falling on commodity costs.

3. What is the best seasonal window, and why does it make sense?

April–July (12 of 15 years, median +16.8 points), because the market prices the monsoon forecast and kharif sowing before volumes appear.

4. Who leads the Indian tractor market?

Mahindra, with over 41% of August 2026 retail sales including its Swaraj division; it is inside the passenger-vehicle watchlist, not this one.

5. What does Swaraj Engines actually do?

It makes the diesel engines for Mahindra's Swaraj tractors, so it is a low-capex proxy for one brand's volumes.

6. What is the TREM V situation?

Draft rules move above-75 HP and below-25 HP tractors to TREM V from October 2026, while the 25–75 HP volume segment is deferred to 2032, avoiding a big cost step.

7. Why did 2018–19 hurt so much despite a decent monsoon in 2019?

Rural distress and the NBFC credit freeze stopped financing, and about 80% of tractors are bought on loans.

8. What is VST Tillers' niche?

Power tillers and power weeders for small and wet-land farms, plus small tractors; Q1 FY27 power weeders grew 56%.

9. How big is the electric-tractor market?

Negligible: fewer than 100 registered as of 2024 against an installed base of more than 9 million diesel tractors, though BIS standards now exist.

10. What cushions tractor demand in a bad crop year?

Non-farm use: roughly a fifth of tractor work is haulage and construction, supported by infrastructure and rural works spending.
