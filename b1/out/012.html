1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Rubber from a refinery

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Rubber trees in Kerala and Thailand drip a white milk called latex, which dries into natural rubber. During the Second World War, countries couldn't get enough tree rubber, so chemists learned to make rubber from oil products (butadiene and styrene). Different recipes give different powers: one resists oil (for car hoses and gloves), one resists sunlight (for car door seals), one holds air (for tyre inner linings). Some rubber is sold as a liquid milk, like latex paint, to coat paper or make gloves. When oil gets expensive, synthetic rubber costs more; when trees give less milk, natural rubber costs more. The two compete, so each one's price pulls the other.

Say it to a client in one minute

Synthetic rubbers (SBR, PBR, NBR, EPDM, butyl) are made from butadiene, styrene and acrylonitrile and priced as spreads over those petrochemicals. India's big capacity sits with Reliance (PBR, SBR, and halobutyl via its Sibur joint venture) and Indian Synthetic Rubber (an IOC–Trinseo venture); neither is a listed pure play. The listed plays are niche:

  • Apcotex: synthetic latex for paper and construction, NBR for hoses and seals, and nitrile latex for gloves. It has just posted a record quarter, as West Asian disruption cut imports and lifted prices.
  • Rubfila: natural latex rubber thread for garments. Volumes are falling and latex is at a record price.

The cycle's big lesson: nitrile latex boomed with COVID gloves in 2020–21, then collapsed as glove makers overbuilt. Margins here are borrowed from disruptions, and management itself says the 2026 margin will normalise.

Dry rubber vs latex

The same polymer can be sold two ways. Dry rubber comes in bales, and tyre and hose makers mix and cure it (see 011 for the vulcanisation chemistry). Latex is the polymer suspended in water as tiny droplets, like milk. Customers dip, coat or spray it and let the water evaporate. That is how gloves, balloons and coated paper are made. Latex is harder to transport (it's mostly water and can spoil), so it is usually made near the customer, which gives local makers like Apcotex a natural moat against imports.

Analogy

Synthetic rubbers are like a family of fabrics: nitrile is the raincoat that keeps oil out, EPDM is the sun-proof fabric, butyl is the balloon that holds air. Latex is the same material sold as "paint" instead of as a sheet.

Everyday examples

The glossy coating on a cereal box is SB latex. The blue gloves at a hospital or a food counter are nitrile latex. A car's fuel hose and engine oil seals are NBR, its door seals EPDM, and a scooter tube butyl. The elastic in your underwear waistband is natural-latex rubber thread, possibly Rubfila's. The rubber in a tyre is a blend of natural rubber, SBR and PBR.

Diverse demand

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Feedstocks: when a refinery "cracks" naphtha to make plastics, it also makes a gas called butadiene. That is the Lego brick for most synthetic rubber. From trees, tappers cut a thin groove in the bark every morning and collect the white milk in cups.
  • Polymerisation: the Lego bricks are snapped into very long chains in big tanks of soapy water (emulsion) or solvent (solution). Long, tangled chains are what make rubber stretchy.
  • Latex products: some of the chains are kept as milk and sold in tankers to paper mills and glove factories.
  • Converters: glove factories dip hand-shaped moulds into nitrile latex, like dipping a biscuit in chocolate, then dry and peel them. Thread makers squeeze latex through tiny nozzles into an acid bath, where it sets into elastic threads.
  • End products: the tyre, the hose, the glove and the waistband.
Fund-manager lens

Commodity rubbers (SBR, PBR) are a game for integrated cracker owners like Reliance, who own the butadiene. Small listed players survive in niches where grades, service and approvals matter: specialty latex and NBR grades tuned to particular customers' machines. Imports from Korea, China, Taiwan and Europe cap domestic prices, so any disruption to imports (as in 2026) lifts Indian producers' margins, temporarily. Pay for the niche, not for the disruption.

Why the margins swing

  1. Monomer costs move first: butadiene in Northeast Asia rose ~42% between December 2025 and March 2026. Latex prices follow with a lag, so margins dip, then recover.
  2. Import parity sets the ceiling: Indian prices sit at the landed cost of Korean, Chinese and Taiwanese imports. When freight or supply is disrupted, that ceiling jumps (2021, 2026).
  3. Glove cycles are extreme: nitrile latex went from shortage (2020–21) to glut (2022–24) as Malaysian and Chinese glove makers doubled capacity.
  4. Natural vs synthetic substitution: when natural rubber hits records (2026), tyre makers use more synthetic, which supports SBR and PBR prices.
Analogy

A latex maker is like a paint shop that mixes custom colours. Bulk white paint (commodity SBR) is cheap everywhere, but the exact shade a customer's machine is tuned to (a specialty latex grade) earns a premium, because switching means re-testing. And because latex is mostly water, shipping it far is like shipping bottled water: expensive, so local makers win.

7 · The business calendar

Tree seasons and customer seasons

  • Natural rubber: in Kerala, trees "winter" (shed leaves) in February–April and yield little; the monsoon (June–August) disrupts tapping; peak output is October–January. Latex prices tend to firm in the lean months. Thailand's wintering is similar (February–April).
  • Paper and board: steady, with festive packaging demand in August–October.
  • Construction latex: weaker in the monsoon.
  • Gloves: follow healthcare stocking cycles and US tariff deadlines, not the weather.
  • Auto (NBR, EPDM): the Indian festive-season production build (August–October).

The exceptions, and why

  • April misses: 2025 (−5.3), 2015 (−1.2), 2019 (−0.1). 2025 was the US tariff shock; 2019 the auto slowdown. The best Aprils were 2022 (+26.6), 2016 (+23.7, after the Omnova deal) and 2021 (+21.6).
  • April–July failures: 2018 (−7.5), 2019 (−8.2), 2023 (−3.6). These were the small-cap sell-off, the auto slowdown and the glove glut. The biggest runs were 2021 (+80.8, the glove boom) and 2017 (+48.4).

How to use this

With two stocks, read this as "Apcotex tends to run from April to July and fade in the final quarter and the first quarter." That fits its results calendar (strong March-quarter and June-quarter prints) better than any industry season. Company events (the Omnova deal, the glove boom) explain the outliers.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Seven drivers

  1. Monomer prices: butadiene, styrene and acrylonitrile, which follow naphtha and crude and cracker operating rates.
  2. Import parity and logistics: Asian export prices plus freight. Disruptions (COVID, the 2026 West Asian conflict) lift Indian prices.
  3. Natural rubber prices: they set the substitution balance with SBR and PBR and decide thread makers' margins. Tree yields, weather and Thai policy matter.
  4. Glove-industry capacity: Malaysian (Top Glove, Hartalega, Kossan, Supermax) and Chinese expansions decide nitrile latex demand and price. The 100% US tariff on Chinese medical gloves (2026) shifts orders to Malaysia, Thailand and Vietnam, where glove prices are up 15–25%.
  5. Auto and tyre output: NBR and EPDM for vehicles, SBR and PBR for tyres.
  6. Paper, board and construction: latex for coated board (packaging, e-commerce) and construction chemicals.
  7. Trade defence: India's NBR anti-dumping cases (Apcotex was the complainant in 2020; a newer case covers China, the EU, Korea and Russia).

The anatomy of a latex cycle

  1. A shock creates scarcity: COVID glove demand (2020), China's energy curbs (2021), shipping disruption (2026). Import parity jumps and domestic makers' margins double.
  2. Capacity follows: glove makers and latex producers expand, as does Apcotex (₹220 cr now).
  3. The glut: prices fall to import cost or below, and margins halve (2022–24).
  4. Niche grades hold up; commodity grades don't.

Patterns that repeat

  • Record quarters come with warnings: in Q1 FY27 and in FY22, Apcotex's own management flagged normalisation.
  • Natural rubber spikes hurt converters, not polymerisers: Rubfila suffers when latex hits records; synthetic makers gain from substitution.
  • Deals re-rate: the Omnova purchase (2016) made Apcotex India's NBR monopoly and began its re-rating.

11 · The global picture

War invented it; Asia makes it

Germany developed "Buna" synthetic rubber in the 1930s to escape dependence on imported natural rubber. When Japan seized Malaya's and the Dutch East Indies' plantations in 1942, the US built a synthetic rubber industry almost from nothing in about three years, one of history's great industrial crash programmes. After the war, synthetic rubber moved to wherever crackers were cheap: the US Gulf, Europe, Japan, then Korea, Taiwan and China.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • Plantations → factories (1940s): war and supply security.
  • US/Europe → Asia (1980s–2010s): cracker economics and customer location (tyres and gloves moved to Asia).
  • Natural rubber: Malaysia → Thailand, Indonesia, Vietnam → Côte d'Ivoire: Malaysia's planters switched to palm oil; West Africa is planting fast.
  • Gloves: US → Malaysia → China → back to Malaysia and Thailand: tariffs are redrawing the map again in 2025–26.

12 · Where technology is going

Five shifts

  1. Solution SBR and functionalised rubbers for low-rolling-resistance and EV tyres, which need precise polymer design and are replacing emulsion SBR in premium tyres.
  2. Bio-based and recycled monomers: bio-butadiene and bio-isoprene research; recycled tyre rubber and devulcanisation.
  3. Thinner, stronger gloves: lower-weight nitrile gloves need higher-performance latex (carboxylated grades), which favours specialty suppliers.
  4. Hydrogenated nitrile (HNBR): for EV and high-temperature seals.
  5. Traceable natural rubber: satellite and blockchain tracing to meet EU deforestation rules.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What is a normal EBITDA margin once imports normalise, and how much of Q1 FY27 was one-off?
  2. What are volumes by segment (latex, NBR, nitrile latex), domestic vs export?
  3. What is the status of the ₹220 cr expansion: commissioning date, approvals, and utilisation targets?
  4. What is the status of NBR anti-dumping duty, and what share of imports it covers?
  5. (Rubfila) What caused the audit qualification, and when will it be resolved? What happens to the ₹61 cr from Premier Tissues?
  6. How much of the natural-latex price rise can be passed on to garment customers?

Red flags

  • Repeated audit qualifications.
  • Capacity commissioned into a normalising market.
  • Glove-driven profit spikes treated as recurring.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. Why do synthetic rubbers come in so many types?

Each recipe gives a different property: NBR resists oil, EPDM resists weather, butyl holds air, SBR and PBR are cheap and wear-resistant for tyres.

2. Why does latex give local producers a natural moat?

It is mostly water and can spoil, so shipping it far is costly; customers prefer nearby suppliers.

3. What drove Apcotex's record Q1 FY27?

West Asian shipping disruption cut imports and lifted Indian prices; its margin rose to 22.3% from 10.3% even as total volumes fell.

4. What happened to nitrile latex after COVID?

Glove makers overbuilt capacity, so the 2020–21 shortage became a 2022–24 glut and margins collapsed.

5. How are US tariffs changing the glove trade?

Section 301 tariffs on Chinese medical gloves reached 100% in January 2026, shifting orders to Malaysia, Thailand and Vietnam.

6. Why is Rubfila hurt when natural rubber prices spike?

Natural latex is its main raw material, and it can't always pass the rise on to garment customers.

7. What made Apcotex India's only NBR maker?

Its 2016 purchase of Omnova's NBR plant at Valia.

8. Why is Southern Latex excluded from the basket?

It had no operating revenue in FY26; it earns rent and interest and plans to change business.

9. When did the US build a synthetic rubber industry, and why?

During the Second World War, after Japan seized the Southeast Asian rubber plantations in 1942.

10. What is the strongest seasonal window, and what's the caveat?

April–July (12 of 15 years, median +23 points), but the basket is only two stocks, so company events drive it.
