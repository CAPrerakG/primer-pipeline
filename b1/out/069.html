Steel Wire & Wire Ropes

Wire is steel pulled through a series of shrinking holes until it is thin, strong and precise. Twisted together it becomes rope — the cable that lifts a mine cage, holds a suspension bridge, raises a crane or runs an elevator. Inside a tyre, a coated wire ring holds the tyre onto the wheel; inside a tall building, high-tensile strand holds the concrete in tension. These are small industries by market value but excellent illustrations of a general rule: the further a steel product moves from the mill and the more it must be certified, the less it behaves like a commodity. This primer explains the drawing process, the four wire markets, why rope earns more than wire, and what separates the one high-quality Indian producer from the rest.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Two businesses: commodity wire and certified rope

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Imagine pulling a thick strand of clay through a smaller and smaller hole until it becomes a long thin thread. Steel wire is made the same way, by pulling a rod through hard rings until it is the thickness you want. Then, like plaiting hair, many wires are twisted together to make a rope strong enough to lift a lift full of people or hold up a bridge. Plain wire is sold by the kilo and everyone can make it. The special ropes are different: if one breaks, people die, so buyers pay more and only trust a few factories.

Say it to a client in one minute

A small sector with one good business model and several poor ones.

  • The quality name: Usha Martin — Q1 FY27 revenue ₹1,033 cr, profit ₹142 cr (+41%), wire-rope volumes +18%; rope is half its tonnage but 73% of revenue.
  • Guidance: 10–12% volume growth, about 15% value growth and EBITDA margins above 20% in FY27, with a ₹250–300 cr elevator-rope expansion starting October 2026.
  • The specialty push: Bansal Wire is spending ₹2,000–2,500 cr on specialty wire, targeting ₹600–800 cr of EBITDA, and has won a trial order for steel tyre cord — a product India imports.
  • The niche compounder: Rajratan Global's tyre bead wire profit rose 70% on better mix, with volumes up 16%.
  • The calendar: February–March has beaten the Nifty just twice in fifteen years.

The economic ladder runs from plain wire, where anyone with a drawing bench competes, to certified ropes and specialty wires, where approvals, testing and failure consequences create real barriers. Usha Martin's disclosure captures it exactly: wire rope is about half of its physical sales but nearly three-quarters of its revenue.

The chain in one breath

  • Steel mills supply rod.
  • Wire mills clean it and draw it thinner and stronger.
  • Heat treatment and coating give it the properties customers need.
  • Stranding machines twist wires into rope.
  • Mines, cranes and lifts wear the rope out and buy another.
Fund-manager lens

The critical distinction is between tonnes and value. A wire drawer measures success in tonnes at a thin spread; a rope maker measures it in revenue per tonne, because a certified elevator or offshore rope sells for a multiple of plain wire. Usha Martin's rope business — half the volume, 73% of revenue, group margins above 20% — is what the good version of this industry looks like. The poor version is a drawer buying rod from a mill that also makes wire, selling into a fragmented market with no approvals to protect it.

The rest of the industry

Much of the wire industry is unlisted: Tata Steel's wire division, the JSW and ArcelorMittal Nippon Steel wire businesses, and hundreds of small drawers. Tyre cord — the finest and most valuable wire product — is dominated globally by Bekaert and by Chinese producers, and India still imports most of what it uses, which is why Bansal Wire's trial order matters. Welded wire mesh, nails and fasteners sit in other sections.

Two case studies in the same quarter make the point. Usha Martin grew revenue 16% and profit 41% because its mix moved further toward rope and value-added products. Bansal Wire grew revenue 24% but profit fell 48% and its operating margin dropped to 4.8%, because it is selling more tonnes of ordinary wire while building capacity and absorbing higher input and energy costs. Same industry, same quarter, opposite outcomes — the difference is product mix.

Why replacement demand matters

A wire rope is a consumable. A dragline rope in a coal mine, a crane rope at a port or the ropes in a lift wear out on a schedule and are replaced whether or not anyone is investing in new capacity. That gives the rope business a base of recurring revenue that the plain wire business lacks, and it is why exports — to mines and ports across the world — can be a steadier business than selling into a single domestic construction cycle.

5b · The import-substitution push

Moving up the wire ladder

India's wire industry grows at about 7–8% a year in tonnes, roughly with the economy. The interesting growth is in the products India still imports: steel tyre cord, high-carbon specialty wires, suspension-spring wire, and the finer grades used in autos and engineering. These need cleaner steel, tighter process control and customer qualification that takes years — which is why they earn better margins and why domestic capacity has been slow to arrive.

The investment case in this sector rests almost entirely on this ladder. A company that stays in plain wire is a price taker with a thin spread and no protection; a company that climbs into certified rope or qualified specialty wire earns durable margins. The capital cost of climbing is high — Bansal Wire's ₹2,000–2,500 cr programme is roughly half its market value — and the payback takes years, which is why the market treats the transition sceptically until the margin shows up.

5c · The economics of safety

Why a worn rope must be thrown away

Almost every good business in this sector rests on one fact: a wire rope is a life-safety component with a written expiry rule. It is not replaced when a purchase manager feels like it; it is replaced when an inspector measuring broken wires says it must be. The Indian code of practice for wire ropes, IS 3973, recommends retirement once localised or distributed flaws have cost the rope roughly a tenth of its original breaking strength. Indian mining regulations are more specific still: a rope comes off when broken wires exceed 5% of the total in one lay length, or when the cross-sectional area is down 10% over the active length. The international equivalent, ISO 4309, and the American crane rule under OSHA 1926.1413, work the same way — a documented inspection at a set frequency, with numerical discard criteria that leave the operator no discretion.

Three commercial consequences follow, and they explain most of what you see in the financial statements.

  • Demand is non-discretionary. A mine winder or a port crane cannot postpone a rope change in a bad year the way it can postpone buying a new machine. That is why rope revenue is steadier than capital-goods revenue and why the replacement base grows with the installed fleet rather than with this year's capex budget.
  • Life is a selling point, not a giveaway. If a compacted or plastic-impregnated rope lasts 50% longer, the customer's cost per tonne hauled falls even at a much higher purchase price — which is exactly how a rope maker escapes price competition. The economics are the same as premium refractories or premium tyres: the buyer is purchasing hours of service, not kilograms of steel.
  • The supplier list is short and slow to change. A rope that fails can kill people and stop a mine, so buyers qualify a handful of makers and stay with them. Magnetic non-destructive testing and routine mine inspections generate a performance record for every rope on site, and a supplier whose ropes retire early loses the next order.

This is the whole argument for owning the rope makers rather than the wire drawers, and it is the reason elevator rope — where a failure is unthinkable and the certification burden is heaviest — is the most attractive tonne in the industry, and why Usha Martin is spending ₹250–300 cr to make 6,000 tonnes more of it.

7 · The business calendar

Projects, monsoon and year-end destocking

  • January–March: distributors run down stock before the fiscal year ends; the weakest window, with only 2 wins in 15 years in February–March.
  • April–July: new budgets, pre-monsoon construction and mining activity; April has worked in 10 of 15 years.
  • July–September: the monsoon slows mining and construction; August–September has worked in only 4 of 14 years.
  • October–December: post-monsoon project work and export shipments; December has worked in 9 of 15 years.
  • Replacement cycles: rope replacement at mines and ports continues through the year, smoothing the trough.

The shape follows the customers: mining, construction and infrastructure projects order before the monsoon and again in the second half, while the January–March quarter brings year-end destocking at distributors.

A tendency, not a rule: small samples, survivorship bias (only names still listed) and equal weighting. Use it for timing context, not as a signal.

The exceptions, and why

  • February–March has worked twice in fifteen years, with a median loss of 11.4 points — the clearest negative season in the Metals family.
  • April has failed only in 2019 (−12.8), 2025 (−10.3) and 2012 (−1.5), and delivered +35.6 in 2021 and +23.7 in 2018.
  • 2019 was the worst year (−47.1), when small-cap wire makers with debt were sold indiscriminately.
How to use this

The first-quarter weakness is consistent across the whole Metals family — mining (064), ferro alloys (065), steel (066) and wire all lose to the Nifty in February–March — which makes it one of the more reliable patterns in this project. It reflects the same physical reality: the Indian fiscal year-end and Chinese New Year fall together, and metal buyers stop buying.

9 · What moves margins

Eight drivers

  1. Product mix. Rope and specialty wire against plain wire.
  2. Wire rod prices. The input, and the source of inventory effects.
  3. Mining and port activity. Replacement demand for rope.
  4. Construction and infrastructure. LRPC strand and galvanised wire.
  5. Auto and tyre production. Bead wire, cord and spring wire.
  6. Exports. Usha Martin's global network; Middle East disruption hurt this quarter.
  7. Zinc and power costs. Galvanising and drawing.
  8. Capacity expansion. Whether new tonnes come with better mix or just more tonnes.

10 · A decade of cycles

2012 to 2026, year by year

YearWhat happenedBasketNiftyDiff (pts)

Basket: Usha Martin, Bharat Wire Ropes (from 2016), Bedmutha and Shalimar Wires, equal-weighted monthly. Bansal Wire, Rajratan and other recent or thinly traded listings are excluded. Monthly returns capped at ±50%, price only; 2026 to 18 September.

The anatomy of the cycles

  1. Debt and dilution (2012–2015): expansion funded with borrowing met a weak market; Usha Martin fell 62% in 2015.
  2. Restructuring (2016–2019): Usha Martin sold its steel business in 2019 to become a focused rope maker; the small caps struggled.
  3. The recovery (2020–2023): exports, mining demand and a small-cap boom; the basket rose 119% in 2021 and 133% in 2023.
  4. Divergence (2024–2026): Usha Martin's mix-led growth against flat or falling small caps.

Patterns that repeat

  • Mix beats volume. Every good year for Usha Martin has come from rope, not tonnes.
  • Leverage kills in this sector. The 2015 and 2019 collapses were balance-sheet events, not demand events.
  • The first quarter of the calendar year is reliably weak.

Wire rope is a genuinely global business: a mine in Chile, a port in Rotterdam and a fishing fleet in Norway all buy from a handful of certified makers. That is why an Indian company with the right approvals can export more than half its output, and why geopolitical disruption — Middle East shipping this year — shows up directly in quarterly volumes.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Compacted and plastic-impregnated ropes. Longer life and higher breaking loads for cranes and mines.
  2. Rope condition monitoring. Sensors that predict replacement, sold as a service.
  3. Stay cables and structural strand. Usha Martin's first international plasticated LRPC order this quarter.
  4. Ultra-fine tyre cord. The hardest wire product, still mostly imported.
  5. Higher-strength spring wires. Lighter vehicles need stronger, cleaner steel.
  6. Coating chemistry. Zinc-aluminium and organic coatings that outlast plain galvanising.

What is priced in

Usha Martin trades close to its high on a business that is compounding volumes at 10–12% with margins above 20% — the market is paying for consistency, and the elevator-rope expansion gives it a further mix lever. Everything else in the sector is priced for disappointment: Rajratan is 66% below its 2022 high despite 70% profit growth this quarter, Bharat Wire Ropes 56% below and Bedmutha 65% below. That gap is the sector's central question. If Rajratan's niche and Bansal's specialty programme deliver, today's prices will look cheap; if they remain volume businesses in a 7–8% growth industry, they will not.

The honest read

This is a small sector with one high-quality business and a long tail of converters. The lesson generalises across the Metals family: in commodity processing, the only durable profits come from certification, approvals and mix — the things that stop a customer switching supplier on price. Buy the ladder-climbers, and treat the plain drawers as leveraged bets on steel spreads.

19 · Glossary

Every term you'll meet

Process

Wire rod
Hot-rolled steel in coil form, the input to wire drawing.
Drawing
Pulling rod through progressively smaller dies to make wire.
Die
The hard ring, usually tungsten carbide or diamond, through which wire is pulled.
Descaling
Removing the oxide layer from rod before drawing.
Patenting
A heat treatment that gives high-carbon wire strength and ductility.
Galvanising
Coating wire with zinc for corrosion resistance.
Stranding
Twisting wires together into a strand.
Closing
Laying strands around a core to form a rope.
Core
The centre of a rope, of steel or fibre.
Lay
The direction and length of the twist in a rope.
Compacted rope
Rope whose strands are pressed to increase steel area and strength.
Plasticated rope
Rope impregnated with polymer for longer life.

Products

Wire rope
The finished cable used for lifting and hauling.
LRPC strand
Low-relaxation prestressed concrete strand used in bridges and sleepers.
Stay cable
The cables that hold up a cable-stayed bridge.
Bead wire
High-carbon coated wire that anchors a tyre to the rim.
Steel tyre cord
Very fine wire reinforcing the body of a tyre.
IHT / OHT wire
Induction- and oil-hardened-tempered wire for suspension springs.
Elevator rope
Certified rope used in lifts.
Dragline rope
Heavy rope used on mining excavators.
Breaking load
The force at which a rope fails — the key specification.

Business terms

Conversion spread
The gap between rod cost and wire selling price.
Revenue per tonne
The best single indicator of product mix.
Replacement demand
Sales driven by wear rather than new investment.
Qualification
The process of being approved as a supplier for a critical product.
Import substitution
Replacing imported products with domestic production.
Utilisation
Output as a share of installed capacity.
Operating margin
Operating profit as a share of revenue.
Value-added share
The proportion of sales from higher-margin products.
Tonne-realisation
Revenue divided by tonnes sold; the shorthand for mix.
Pass-through
Passing input-cost changes on to customers, with a lag.
Installed base
The fleet of cranes, mines and lifts that generates replacement demand.

Safety and standards

IS 3973
The Indian code of practice for selecting, installing and maintaining wire ropes.
ISO 4309
The international standard for rope inspection and discard on cranes.
Discard criteria
The numerical rules — broken wires, area loss, corrosion — that force a rope out of service.
Lay length
The distance along a rope in which a strand makes one full turn; broken wires are counted per lay length.
Active length
The part of a rope that actually runs over sheaves and drums, and so wears.
Non-destructive testing (NDT)
Magnetic or ultrasonic inspection that finds internal broken wires without cutting the rope.
Rope life
Service hours or cycles before discard — the number a premium rope is sold on.
Mine winder
The hoist that raises and lowers the cage in a shaft; its ropes are the most safety-critical of all.
Sheave
The grooved pulley a rope runs over; its diameter governs rope fatigue.
Fatigue
Damage accumulated by repeated bending, the usual cause of broken wires.
Corrosion loss
Section lost to rust, counted against the discard threshold.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. How is wire made?

Steel rod is cleaned and pulled through progressively smaller dies, growing stronger as it thins.

2. Why does rope earn more than wire?

It is designed, tested and certified, and failure has severe consequences, so buyers pay for trust.
