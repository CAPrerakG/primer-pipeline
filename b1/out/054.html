Electronic Equipment

This section gathers companies that make or sell electronic instruments: systems that test radar and missile electronics, laboratory instruments that measure light and materials, hospital monitors that watch a patient's heart, and distributors that bring electronics brands to Indian shops. The listed companies are all small. Understanding them means understanding a large market — test and measurement, scientific and medical instruments — in which India still imports most of what it uses.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Measuring things precisely is a valuable, specialised business

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Before a missile, a phone or a hospital machine can be trusted, someone has to check that its electronics work exactly right. That needs special measuring machines — like a doctor's stethoscope, but for circuits. Scientists need other machines to measure light, colour and tiny movements in their experiments. Hospitals need screens that show a patient's heartbeat and oxygen all the time. These machines are hard to make, so a few companies around the world make most of them. India has some small companies that make them, and some that sell other companies' electronics in shops.

Say it to a client in one minute

A small, mixed section whose listed companies are micro-caps, but whose end-markets — defence, science, healthcare — are growing and import-dependent.

  • Creative Newtech (the largest): IT and electronics distribution plus own brands and surveillance projects; revenue +21%, profit +32% in Q1 FY27; stock +55%.
  • Digilogic Systems: test, measurement and simulation systems for defence and aerospace; listed January 2026; FY25 revenue +40%.
  • Holmarc: scientific and teaching instruments from Kochi; stock +14% after −47% in 2025.
  • Maestros: patient monitors, ECG machines and telemedicine; FY26 profit +66%.
  • Big picture: global leaders (Keysight, Rohde & Schwarz, Thermo Fisher, Philips, GE HealthCare) dominate Indian demand; defence indigenisation is the main opening for Indian makers.

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Parts: special chips, sensors and lenses, most bought from abroad.
  • Design: clever engineering and software decide how well it measures.
  • Building: each instrument is built and checked against a known standard.
  • Approval: the army, hospitals or universities must approve it before buying.
  • Selling: shops and distributors bring it to customers.
  • Upkeep: it is re-checked every year so it stays accurate.
Fund-manager lens

The critical question in instruments is whether a company owns a measurement franchise or is simply assembling or distributing. Franchises — defence test systems qualified with DRDO labs, medical devices registered and installed in hospitals, laboratory instruments specified in university curricula — earn steady margins and recurring service income. Distribution businesses earn thin margins on large revenue and need working capital. This section contains both, so its average statistics mean little; each company has to be judged on its own model.

What is missing, and why it matters

India's largest instrument businesses are either foreign subsidiaries (Keysight, Rohde & Schwarz, Thermo Fisher, Philips, GE HealthCare, Siemens Healthineers — mostly unlisted in India) or divisions of larger listed groups: BEL and Data Patterns build defence test and electronic systems (058), Tata Advanced Systems is unlisted, and medical-device makers such as Poly Medicure sit in healthcare sections. The service tail

Instruments must be calibrated regularly to stay accurate, and medical and defence systems need maintenance contracts for years. For established makers this installed-base income can become a large and stable share of revenue — the model that makes global instrument companies so valuable. For India's small makers it is still modest, because their installed bases are small; building it is one of the clearest routes to better quality of earnings.

The comparison makes a useful point. A distributor with a 5% operating margin trading near 30 times profit is being valued as though its shift to own brands and projects will lift margins; a defence test-system company trading on a much higher multiple of its last reported profit is being valued on expected programme wins. In both cases the price embeds a change in the business, so the company's next few results matter more than its history.

Analogy

An instrument maker is like a lift company: selling the lift is competitive, but the maintenance contract that follows runs for decades and is where much of the profit sits.

Support and upgradesMaintenance, software updates, recalibrationThe platform's service lifeRecurring, high-margin revenue

The model rewards patience: a test-system supplier that is designed into a missile or radar programme early can earn from it for a decade or more, because re-qualifying a different supplier's test equipment is costly and slow. This is why defence test companies such as Digilogic describe their growth in terms of programmes won rather than quarterly orders.

The patient-monitor market in India

Critical-care monitoring has grown with the number of intensive-care and high-dependency beds in private and government hospitals. The top end is dominated by Philips, GE HealthCare and Mindray; Indian makers — Maestros among the listed ones, and unlisted firms such as BPL Medical Technologies and Skanray — compete for cost-conscious private hospitals, smaller towns and government programmes. Registration under the Medical Devices Rules, service networks and clinical trust decide who wins. Telemedicine has added a new layer: monitors that transmit data so specialists can supervise patients remotely.

Distribution economics, in one illustration

For an electronics distributor, a typical rupee of sales might carry a gross margin in the high single digits, most of which is spent on warehousing, logistics, sales staff and credit to retailers, leaving an operating margin of a few per cent — Creative Newtech's EBITDA margin is about 5%. Profit therefore depends on volume, inventory turns and how quickly customers pay. Moving into own brands and technology projects raises gross margins, which is why the market has rewarded Creative Newtech's shift: its standalone profit grew 70% on 27% revenue growth in Q1 FY27. 7 · The business calendar

Budgets, academic years and festivals

  • January–March: government and defence year-end purchasing; laboratories spend remaining budgets.
  • April–June: new budgets released; academic procurement for the coming year.
  • July–September: university and research tenders; monsoon season for projects.
  • October–December: festive demand for consumer electronics at distributors.
  • Defence programmes: test-system orders follow platform development milestones rather than seasons.

  • Windows: Feb–Mar beat the Nifty in 2 of 7 years (median -16.8 pts); Apr–Jul beat the Nifty in 4 of 7 years (median +10.8 pts); Aug–Sep beat the Nifty in 2 of 6 years (median -5.0 pts); Oct–Dec beat the Nifty in 5 of 7 years (median +24.4 pts); Sep–Nov beat the Nifty in 4 of 7 years (median +1.1 pts); Jan–Mar beat the Nifty in 2 of 7 years (median -14.2 pts).

Control: small caps as a whole (Nifty Smallcap 100) beat the Nifty in April in 11 of 15 years, by an average of 3.0 points, and lag in January–February. Part of any April strength is this market-wide effect. After removing it, this industry's own April edge is about -3.3 points.

Reading it: only seven years of data and a two-stock basket, so treat this as anecdote rather than evidence. October has been the best month (6 of 7 years, +17.7 points on average) and October–December the best window (5 of 7, median +24.4). January–March has been weak (2 of 7, median −14.2), as has February–March (2 of 7, median −16.8). The October strength is consistent with festive-season electronics demand at Creative Newtech, which dominates the record.

A tendency, not a rule: small samples, survivorship bias (only names still listed) and equal weighting. Use it for timing context, not as a signal.

How to read it

  • October–December failed only in 2020 (−33.3) and 2025 (−3.6); its best was 2021 (+85.0).
  • January–March failed in five of seven years, including every year from 2023 to 2026.
  • 2021 dominates: Creative Newtech rose 315% that year.
How to use this

Seven years of one or two illiquid stocks is not enough to trade on. The record mainly shows how volatile micro-cap electronics names are — a 177% basket gain in 2021, a 35% loss in 2025. Use company fundamentals and liquidity, not the calendar.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Eight drivers

  1. Defence indigenisation: test and simulation systems for Indian-made radars, missiles and electronic warfare (see 058).
  2. Electronics manufacturing growth: factories need test equipment (052).
  3. Research and education budgets: university and lab spending.
  4. Healthcare expansion: hospitals, critical care beds and government health schemes.
  5. Consumer electronics demand: for distributors like Creative Newtech.
  6. Government technology projects: smart warehousing, surveillance, e-governance.
  7. Import substitution policies: preference for Indian-made products in public procurement.
  8. Component supply: access to high-grade imported chips and optics.

9b · The largest opportunity

Why defence test and simulation matters most

Every weapon system India now designs and builds at home — radars, missiles, electronic-warfare suites, communication systems, avionics — needs test equipment and simulators during development, on the production line and in maintenance depots. Historically much of that equipment was imported alongside the platform. As more platforms are designed in India, their test systems increasingly are too, and procurement rules that favour domestic suppliers reinforce the shift. This creates a steady, engineering-heavy market for specialist companies. Digilogic is the listed pure-play in this section; larger defence-electronics companies such as Data Patterns and BEL (see 058) also build test and simulation systems as part of their work. The attraction is the long life of each programme and the recurring support revenue; the risk is lumpy ordering tied to government budgets and the pace of platform development.

11 · The global picture

A few global leaders, very high margins

Test and measurement is dominated by Keysight (spun out of Agilent, originally Hewlett-Packard's instrument business), Rohde & Schwarz, Tektronix (Fortive), Anritsu and National Instruments (now part of Emerson). Scientific instruments are led by Thermo Fisher, Agilent, Shimadzu, Horiba and Bruker; patient monitoring by Philips, GE HealthCare, Mindray and Nihon Kohden. These companies earn high margins because accuracy, reliability and software ecosystems create switching costs, and because customers value a trusted name more than a lower price. China has built strong domestic instrument makers, notably in medical monitoring. India's opportunity is narrower: defence, where indigenisation policies favour domestic suppliers; cost-sensitive education and research markets; and mid-range medical devices.

SegmentGlobal leadersWhere Indian firms can compete
Test and measurementKeysight, Rohde & Schwarz, Tektronix, AnritsuCustomised defence test systems, simulators
Scientific instrumentsThermo Fisher, Agilent, Shimadzu, HoribaTeaching and mid-range laboratory instruments
Patient monitoringPhilips, GE HealthCare, Mindray, Nihon KohdenCost-effective monitors for smaller hospitals and government programmes

For Indian buyers this concentration has practical consequences: long lead times for imported instruments, service that depends on a foreign company's local office, and exposure to export controls on advanced equipment. Those frictions are exactly what give domestic suppliers an opening in defence and government markets, even where their products are less sophisticated than the global leaders'.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • From hardware to software: instruments increasingly defined by software and data analysis.
  • From Western to Chinese mid-range suppliers in medical devices and general instruments.
  • From imports to local defence suppliers in India, driven by procurement policy.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Software-defined instruments: modular hardware with analysis in software.
  2. Hardware-in-the-loop simulation: testing weapons and vehicle electronics against simulated environments.
  3. Remote and connected monitoring: telemedicine and cloud-linked patient monitors.
  4. AI-assisted analysis: automated interpretation of ECGs and test results.
  5. Higher frequencies: 5G, satellite and radar testing at millimetre-wave frequencies.
  6. IoT-based monitoring for logistics: smart warehouses, cold chains and surveillance.

A note on liquidity

Liquidity deserves as much attention as fundamentals here. Three of the five companies are SME or thinly traded listings, where a few large orders can move the price by several per cent in a day and where exiting a position can take weeks. The price record reflects this: the basket rose 177% in 2021 and fell 35% in 2025 while the underlying businesses changed far less. For a portfolio, that argues for small positions sized to daily trading volumes, patience on entry, and a preference for the companies whose results are audited and disclosed quarterly rather than half-yearly. It also means that index-style statistics for this section should be read as descriptions of what happened to a few illiquid shares, not as evidence about the instrument industry.

The honest read

This section is too small and too mixed to be an investable sector in its own right. Each company should be judged individually, with liquidity as a first-order concern. The genuine long-term opportunity is in defence test and simulation, where Indian procurement policy is creating demand for domestic suppliers, and in affordable medical devices. The listed exposure to both is thin here and deeper in the defence-electronics section that follows in this series.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What share of revenue is from own products versus distribution or trading?
  2. What is the order book, and from which customers?
  3. How much revenue is recurring service and calibration?
  4. What approvals and qualifications do your products hold?
  5. How dependent are you on imported components, and are any export-controlled?
  6. What are receivable days on government orders?
  7. What is the R&D spend and product pipeline?
  8. How will you fund growth without diluting shareholders?

Red flags

  • Revenue dominated by low-margin trading presented as manufacturing.
  • Receivables growing faster than revenue.
  • Frequent preferential issues at rising prices.
  • Very low trading volumes relative to market value.
