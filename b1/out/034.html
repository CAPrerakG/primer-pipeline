City Gas Distribution

The last mile of the gas chain, and the only part of it that meets a household. These companies hold exclusive licences to pipe gas into kitchens and to sell compressed natural gas at filling stations in a defined geographical area. For a decade the business was close to perfect: a regulated monopoly, guaranteed cheap gas and a customer base that kept converting. Then the cheap gas was taken away.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

A monopoly whose input price was changed

Explain it to a 10-year-old

In some cities, the gas for your kitchen does not come in a cylinder — it arrives through a thin pipe under the road, and you pay for what you use, like water or electricity. The same company also sells gas to cars and auto-rickshaws at special pumps, because gas is cheaper than petrol. Only one company is allowed to do this in each city, so it has no competitor. For years the government gave these companies a large share of India's own cheap gas, which made everything easy. Then the government said: that cheap gas is needed elsewhere, you must buy more of yours from abroad at the world price. The pipes are still full and more people keep joining — but each unit now earns much less.

Say it to a client in one minute

This is a regulated monopoly whose economics were rewritten by an input decision, not by competition.

  • Volumes are at records. IGL 9.66 mmscmd, its highest ever; Mahanagar's volumes +7%; Adani Total Gas +13%.
  • Margins are not. IGL's EBITDA margin fell to 6.4% from 10.2% in the prior quarter and profit fell 48%; Mahanagar's EBITDA fell 31.5% year on year; Adani Total Gas blamed spot purchases for its squeeze.
  • The cause is the APM allocation cut from October 2024: administered gas now covers under 40% of CNG requirement against about 70%, and spot gas costs 80–100% more.
  • Pass-through is possible but politically bounded — Mahanagar raised CNG prices four times in fourteen days at one point — because CNG must stay meaningfully cheaper than petrol to keep converting customers.
  • The stocks have fallen for four consecutive years and now trade far below their peaks: IGL −50% from 2021, Adani Total Gas −83% from January 2023.

The question is whether ₹7.2–7.5 per scm of operating profit is the new normal or a trough. That depends on LNG prices (see 033) and on whether allocation policy shifts again.

Analogy

A city gas company is a cable operator that has been told what it may charge and, separately, what it must pay for programming. For years it received a quota of cheap content. When that quota was cut, it had to buy the rest at market rates — but it still cannot raise subscription prices much, because its customers will go back to the old service (petrol, or an LPG cylinder) if the saving disappears. The subscriber count keeps rising; the profit per subscriber does not.

Everyday example

An auto-rickshaw driver in Delhi fills CNG at about ₹83 a kilogram because it is meaningfully cheaper per kilometre than petrol. If the CNG price rises far enough, the arithmetic reverses and he stops converting — which is why IGL and Mahanagar raise prices in small, frequent steps rather than in one move. Mahanagar once revised its price four times in fourteen days.

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Sourcing: the company gets some cheap Indian gas by government order and must buy the rest at world prices.
  • Transmission: the gas travels to the edge of the city in a big pipe, for a fee.
  • City gate: the pressure is lowered and a smell is added so leaks can be detected.
  • Network: the company digs up roads for years to lay the small pipes that reach each building.
  • Retail: it sells gas at pumps to vehicles and by meter to kitchens.
  • Conversion: every new household and every converted vehicle is a customer for decades.
Fund-manager lens

Three numbers define a city gas company, and only one of them is volume. Gas cost per unit — the weighted average of administered, long-term LNG and spot gas — is the variable that broke the sector. Operating profit per standard cubic metre (₹7.2–7.5 expected for FY26) is the industry's own scoreboard, and it is quoted in rupees per scm precisely because volume growth can hide margin collapse. And the CNG-to-petrol discount sets the ceiling on pass-through: when it narrows, conversions slow and the volume growth that justifies the capital spending stops. A company reporting record volumes and falling profit is telling you the second and third numbers are under pressure.

Why record volumes produced falling profits

Because in this business profit is volume multiplied by a spread, and the spread was cut by an administrative decision. Before October 2024, roughly 70% of the gas needed for CNG came from the administered pool at a formula-linked price well below the market. After successive cuts, that share fell below 40%, and the balance has to be bought as imported LNG or spot cargoes that cost 80–100% more. A company can respond in three ways: raise pump prices, accept a lower margin, or both. All three operators have done both — which is why IGL's revenue rose 16% on 6% more volume (price), while its EBITDA margin fell from 10.2% to 6.4% (margin). The limit on the first response is competitive rather than regulatory: if CNG stops being clearly cheaper than petrol, the conversions that drive volume growth stop too.

Analogy

Imagine a bakery that had been buying 70% of its flour at a subsidised price. The subsidy is cut to 40%. Bread sales keep rising, the shop is busier than ever, and the owner raises prices a little every fortnight — but the profit on each loaf has halved, and if he raises prices much further people will buy rice instead. That is the entire city gas story since October 2024.

7 · The business calendar

Steadier than most, but not immune

  • Allocation reviews: the government periodically revises the priority and quantum of administered gas — the October 2024 revision reset the industry's economics.
  • Half-yearly gas price resets (1 April and 1 October): the administered formula changes in steps, moving the cost base twice a year (see 028).
  • Monsoon: road-digging slows, so network build and new connections decelerate for a quarter.
  • Winter: piped gas demand rises modestly with cooking and heating in northern cities.
  • Price revisions: not seasonal at all — they follow the gas cost, sometimes several times a month.

The exceptions, and why

  • April–July worked in 6 of 10 years (median +2.4), with the failures in 2023 (−14.2), 2018 (−10.3), 2021 (−5.2) and 2025 (−3.9) — each a year when gas costs or allocation news moved against the sector.

  • October–December has a positive median (+3.4) but only a 5-of-10 record, with 2025 (−18.9) and 2024 (−11.2) the worst — both allocation-squeeze years.
  • The 2021 and 2019 readings are distorted by Adani Total Gas's group-driven moves rather than by city gas fundamentals.
How to use this

Sparingly, and only as context. Ten years, four names and one stock that moves on group news make this the least reliable seasonal table in the family. What actually matters here is a policy calendar, not a seasonal one: the half-yearly administered price revisions on 1 April and 1 October, and any change in the allocation priority for CNG. Those two dates have moved these stocks far more than any month of the year.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Eight drivers

  1. APM gas allocation: the single most important variable. Cutting it from about 70% to under 40% of CNG requirement rewrote the industry's economics.
  2. Imported LNG prices: what the balance costs, and therefore the blended gas cost (see 033).
  3. The CNG-to-petrol and PNG-to-LPG discount: the commercial case for conversion, and the ceiling on price increases.
  4. Volume growth from conversions: new households and vehicles, which compound for decades once connected.
  5. Operating profit per scm: the metric the industry manages to — ₹7.2–7.5 expected for FY26.
  6. Network capital expenditure: the cash cost of growth, heaviest for newer licensees.
  7. Electric vehicles: a long-term threat to the CNG fleet, which is why Adani Total Gas is building charging alongside gas.
  8. Exclusivity periods and third-party access: the eventual end of monopoly protection in each area (see 030).

The anatomy of this cycle

  1. A protected input plus a regulated monopoly produces high, stable returns on capital and a growth runway of conversions. The market pays a premium multiple — 2017 to 2022.
  2. The protected input is reallocated because the same cheap gas is wanted elsewhere. Margins compress immediately; volumes do not fall.
  3. The companies pass through what they can in frequent small price rises, and absorb the rest.
  4. The multiple de-rates for years while the business itself keeps growing — which is the position in 2026, four negative years into the adjustment.

Patterns that repeat

  • Volume growth has never stopped. Every operator is at or near record volumes through the entire de-rating.
  • Policy, not competition, sets the margin. These are monopolies in their areas; what changed was an allocation decision.
  • The basket average has been unreliable since 2019 because of Adani Total Gas's group-driven moves.
  • The de-rating is now longer than the squeeze that caused it — worth noting for anyone assuming the adjustment must be over.

11 · The global picture

An unusual model, for an unusual reason

Piped gas to households is ordinary in Europe, North America and much of East Asia, where networks were built over a century on domestic or piped-in supply. What makes India's model distinctive is the size of the vehicle-fuel segment: CNG for taxis, auto-rickshaws and buses is the larger part of volume for most Indian licensees, driven originally by court-ordered conversion of Delhi's public transport fleet on air-quality grounds. That makes Indian city gas a transport-fuel business as much as a utility, and it ties the industry's fortunes to the petrol price and, increasingly, to electric vehicles. The other distinctive feature is the administered gas allocation — a domestic subsidy in kind that no comparable market provides, and whose partial withdrawal is the industry's current crisis.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • From air-quality mandate to commercial fuel: Delhi's court-ordered CNG conversion created the first large market, which then spread on economics rather than orders.
  • From two cities to a national roll-out: successive PNGRB bidding rounds took authorised areas to 307, covering effectively the whole population (see 030).
  • From guaranteed cheap gas to market-priced supply: the October 2024 allocation cut is the structural break this industry is still absorbing.

12 · Where the business is going

Six shifts

  1. Electric vehicle charging at CNG stations: Adani Total Gas is building both, hedging the fuel it sells against the fuel that may replace it (see 022).
  2. Compressed biogas blending: domestically produced biogas injected into the network, which is both a supply hedge and a policy priority.
  3. Small-scale and trucked LNG: serving industrial customers beyond the pipeline grid.
  4. Smart metering and digital billing: reducing the cost of serving millions of small household accounts.
  5. Network densification: more connections on existing mains, which is far more profitable than new-area build.
  6. Third-party access: the eventual opening of networks to other suppliers once exclusivity expires, changing the monopoly's shape (see 030).

The honest read

The business is intact and the multiple is not, which is the most common way a good industry becomes a difficult investment. Nothing has gone wrong operationally: monopoly licences, record volumes, millions of connected households and a conversion runway across 307 authorised areas. What changed is that a subsidy in kind was partly withdrawn, and the market has spent four years re-pricing the earnings power that subsidy had supported. Two things would resolve it. If LNG prices fall towards the $6–7 range buyers call workable (see 033), the blended gas cost falls without any policy change and margins recover on their own. If the allocation policy is revised again — in either direction — the whole sector re-rates overnight. Neither is forecastable, so the discipline is to watch operating profit per scm rather than volumes, and to read Indraprastha and Mahanagar rather than the basket, because Adani Total Gas has moved on group news in four of the last six years.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What is the gas sourcing mix this quarter — administered, long-term LNG and spot — and at what weighted average cost?
  2. What is operating profit per scm, and what is the target?
  3. What is the current CNG discount to petrol per kilometre, and at what level do conversions stall?
  4. How much of the recent cost increase has been passed through, and how much is still to come?
  5. What is volume growth split between CNG, domestic PNG and industrial PNG?
  6. What is the capital expenditure plan per area, and the expected payback on newer geographical areas?
  7. When does infrastructure and marketing exclusivity expire in your largest areas?
  8. What is the plan if administered allocation is cut further?
  9. (Adani Total Gas) What is the capital allocation between gas network and electric-vehicle charging?

Red flags

  • Volume growth celebrated while operating profit per scm falls.
  • Capital expenditure in new areas funded by debt while margins compress.
  • Price increases deferred for political reasons with no compensating mechanism.
  • Industrial volumes falling as customers switch to alternative fuels.

18 · Myths and live debates

What you'll hear, and the evidence

ClaimEvidence
"Monopoly licences make these safe compounders."The monopoly protects volume, not margin. Volumes are at records and the basket has fallen for four consecutive years.
"Record volumes mean the worst is over."Volumes never fell. The variable that broke was the gas cost, and it is set by allocation policy and LNG prices, not by demand.
"They can simply raise CNG prices."Only up to the point where CNG stops being clearly cheaper than petrol. That ceiling is why margins were absorbed rather than passed through in full.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. Why did IGL's profit fall 48% in a record-volume quarter?

Gas cost. With administered gas covering under 40% of CNG requirement, the balance was bought at spot prices 80–100% higher, and EBITDA margin fell from 10.2% to 6.4%.

2. What changed in October 2024?

The administered gas allocation to CNG was cut about 20%, taking APM coverage from roughly 70% of requirement to under 40% by the second half of FY25.

3. Why can't these companies simply raise prices to fix it?

CNG must remain clearly cheaper than petrol per kilometre, or conversions — the source of volume growth — stop. The discount is the ceiling on pass-through.

4. What is the right margin metric here?

Operating profit per standard cubic metre, expected at ₹7.2–7.5 for FY26, because revenue and EBITDA margin both move with the gas price.

5. Why is the basket misleading in 2021 and 2023?

Adani Total Gas rose 358% in 2021 while the median stock fell 6%, and fell 73% in 2023 after the January short-seller report on the group, while Mahanagar Gas rose 43%.

6. What did the three operators report for volumes?

IGL 9.66 mmscmd (+6%, a record), Mahanagar 433.7 million scm (+7%), Adani Total Gas 303 million scm (+13%).

7. What makes Indian city gas different from European utilities?

The vehicle-fuel segment. CNG is the larger part of volume for most Indian licensees, so the business is priced against petrol rather than against heating alternatives.

8. What would resolve the de-rating without a policy change?

Cheaper LNG. A fall towards the $6–7 per mmbtu range would cut the blended gas cost and restore margins on its own (see 033).

9. Why is Adani Total Gas building EV charging?

It hedges the fuel it sells against the technology that could replace CNG in vehicles, using the same forecourt real estate.

10. What ends the monopoly, and when?

The expiry of marketing and infrastructure exclusivity in each geographical area, after which third parties may use the network.
