Auto Components

About 70% of every vehicle is made by somebody else. This is that somebody: 82 listed suppliers making wiring harnesses, gears, castings, seats, mirrors, brakes, radiators and hundreds of other parts, for Indian vehicle makers and for the world. It is India's most successful engineering export industry, and in 2026 its biggest customer is taxing it.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

The 70% of a vehicle nobody sees

Explain it to a 10-year-old

When you buy a car, the badge on the front belongs to one company, but most of the pieces inside were made by dozens of others. One company makes the wires, another the seats, another the gears, another the mirrors. They are called suppliers. They have to make each part exactly the same, millions of times, deliver it to the car factory the morning it is needed, and cut their price a little every year. If they are good at it, they also sell the same parts to car factories in Germany and America. India has become very good at this.

Say it to a client in one minute

Auto components are India's best engineering export story: ₹7.6 lakh cr of turnover in FY26 (+12.7%), doubled in five years, with $24 bn of exports. ACMA expects 8–10% growth in FY27.

Three things decide how a supplier does. Content per vehicle: whether its parts grow in value as vehicles get bigger, safer and electric. Customer and segment mix: passenger vehicles, two-wheelers, trucks or exports. Cost pass-through: whether steel, aluminium and wage inflation can be passed to the carmaker, usually with a lag.

2026 has been good for the group (+22% against a Nifty down 11%) but very uneven: the median stock is up 6%, with the winners being those with electric-vehicle content (Sona BLW +69%, order book 5.4 times revenue) or scale (Motherson +36%, record revenue). The two live risks are US tariffs of 25% under Section 232 and China's grip on rare-earth magnets, which India imports entirely.

Analogy

A car company is a restaurant chain and the component makers are its kitchen suppliers. Some supply the salt (cheap, replaceable). Some supply the signature sauce made to a secret recipe (hard to replace, better margins). The trick for an investor is knowing which supplier sells salt and which sells the sauce, because they trade at very different multiples for good reason.

Everyday examples

The wiring harness threading through your car's dashboard is probably Motherson's. The differential gears sending power to the wheels of a Tesla or a Tata EV may be Sona BLW's. The leaf springs under a truck are likely Jamna Auto's. The brake shoes on your scooter could be ASK Automotive's. The decorative badge on the bonnet may be SJS's.

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Raw materials: most parts start as steel bars, aluminium ingots, copper wire or plastic granules.
  • Tier-3 and tier-2: small workshops shape, cut and coat those materials into simple pieces.
  • Tier-1: bigger firms assemble those pieces into whole systems and deliver them to the car factory, often within hours of when they are needed.
  • The carmaker: decides which supplier gets the part for the life of a model, usually five to seven years, and asks for a small price cut every year.
  • Aftermarket: when a part wears out, the owner buys a replacement, often at a much higher price than the carmaker paid.
Fund-manager lens

Two questions separate good suppliers from bad. First, is the part designed in (specified by the carmaker for the model's life) or bought on price each year? Designed-in parts earn stable margins; commodity parts do not. Second, does content per vehicle rise with each new model? Safety rules, premiumisation and electrification all add electronic and precision content, which is why Sona BLW and UNO Minda re-rate while pure machining shops do not.

The three rules of supplier economics

  1. Annual price-down. Carmakers typically ask for 1–3% price cuts each year on the same part, so a supplier must cut costs faster than that just to stand still.
  2. Commodity pass-through with a lag. Most contracts adjust for steel and aluminium, but a quarter or two later. That lag is exactly why margins fell across the group in Q1 FY27 (UNO Minda 10.3% from 10.7%, Endurance and Motherson citing input costs).
  3. Content per vehicle beats volume. Indian vehicle volumes grow mid-single digits, but suppliers whose content rises with safety, comfort and electrification grow much faster. UNO Minda's EV systems grew 130% and Sona's BEV revenue 107% in a market growing far slower.
Analogy

Being a supplier is like being a cricket net bowler for a star batsman. The job is secure while you are useful, but the pay rises slowly and you are expected to get better every season. The bowlers who develop a new delivery (electrification content) get promoted; the rest keep bowling the same ball for less money.

Fund-manager lens

The rare-earth magnet issue is the one to watch in 2026–27. India imports every sintered NdFeB magnet it uses, so China's export controls can stop EV motor lines. Localisation rules for electric bus and truck traction motors took effect from 1 September 2026, and the industry has asked to push the deadline to April 2027 because of those shortages. Suppliers with magnet-free motor designs, or with secured supply, have a genuine competitive advantage here.

7 · The business calendar

Following the customer's production plan

  • April–June: new model-year programmes start; annual price negotiations with carmakers conclude.
  • July–September: production builds ahead of the festive season; suppliers run at peak utilisation.
  • October–December: festive production and exports; results reflect the strongest quarter for most suppliers.
  • January–March: carmakers manage year-end inventory, and suppliers absorb the annual price cut; historically the group's weakest stretch.
  • Monthly: OEM dispatch numbers on the 1st are the best real-time read on supplier volumes.

Reading it: April–July is the window: it beat the Nifty in 12 of 15 years with a median of +13.4 points, and April itself in 12 of 15. October–December also works (10 of 14), which fits the festive production build. The weak stretch is January–March (5 of 15, median −5.8) and February–March (4 of 15). With 57 stocks in the basket, about 3 points of April is the general small-cap effect, but the rest lines up with the production and results calendar.

A tendency, not a rule: small samples, survivorship bias (only names still listed) and equal weighting. Use it for timing context, not as a signal.

The exceptions, and why

  • April–July failed only three times: 2013 (−8.3), 2018 (−12.4) and 2019 (−20.9), the rupee crisis and the two years of the auto slowdown and credit freeze. The biggest runs were 2014 (+46.3) and 2021 (+40.0), both recovery years.
  • April misses: 2019 (−4.8), 2013 (−0.7), 2025 (−0.5). The best Aprils were 2012 and 2026 (+11.7 each) and 2015 (+9.1).
  • October–December works (10 of 14, median +2.6), matching the festive production quarter; the misses were 2025 (−6.6) and small negatives in 2020 and 2023.
  • January–March is the weak stretch (5 of 15, median −5.8): year-end inventory management and the annual price-down.
How to use this

The April–July window is one of the most reliable in the library for a large, liquid basket: 12 of 15 years and a median of +13.4 points. It works because the new financial year brings programme starts, price settlements and the first read on the year's volumes. It fails in credit or demand shocks, so check OEM volumes and dealer inventory before leaning on it.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Nine drivers

  1. Vehicle production across cars, two-wheelers, trucks and tractors (017–020): the volume base.
  2. Content per vehicle: safety rules, premiumisation, electronics and electrification raise the value of parts per vehicle faster than volumes grow.
  3. Commodity costs and pass-through lags: steel, aluminium and copper.
  4. Exports and tariffs: $24 bn of exports, with the US applying 25% Section 232 tariffs on roughly 55% of India's shipments there; Europe is now the fastest-growing market.
  5. Currency: a weaker rupee helps exporters and hurts importers of components and magnets.
  6. Customer concentration: many suppliers depend on one or two carmakers; a model's success or failure moves their earnings.
  7. Aftermarket demand: an ageing vehicle fleet supports replacement sales, which are higher margin and counter-cyclical.
  8. Electrification mix: engine-part makers face long-term decline while driveline, motor, electronics and thermal suppliers gain.
  9. Rare-earth and semiconductor supply: the physical constraints on EV programmes.

The anatomy of a supplier cycle

  1. Vehicle volumes turn up. Suppliers' utilisation rises and margins expand faster than revenue (operating leverage).
  2. The market re-rates the whole group, including commodity suppliers with no moat (2014, 2017, 2021).
  3. Costs or credit break the run: commodity spikes (2022, 2026), a credit freeze (2018) or a demand slump (2019). The weakest suppliers fall hardest.
  4. Structural winners separate: those with rising content per vehicle (EV driveline, electronics, safety) keep compounding while the rest track volumes.

Patterns that repeat

  • Suppliers amplify the OEM cycle in both directions: 2014's +192% and 2019's −18.5% against far smaller moves in vehicle sales.
  • The median lags the average in most good years, because a few winners carry the basket (2026: basket +22%, median +6%).
  • Export exposure cuts both ways: a tailwind in 2021, a tariff headwind since 2025.
  • Micro caps produce the extremes: Automotive Stampings +2,430% in 2021, Frontier Springs +678% in 2017, and falls of 50%+ in bad years.

11 · The global picture

India as the world's low-cost engineering workshop

Global component making is dominated by a few giants: Bosch, Denso, ZF, Magna, Continental and Aisin. India's advantage is cost, engineering talent and a deep supplier base built around its own large vehicle industry. Exports reached $24 bn in FY26, with engine components and drive transmission and steering parts making up more than half. Europe is now the fastest-growing market, partly because the US has become harder.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • From job work to systems (2000s–2010s): Indian suppliers moved from making parts to designing and delivering whole systems.
  • From domestic to global (2010s–2020s): Motherson bought dozens of overseas businesses; Sona won global electric-vehicle programmes.
  • From engines to electrification: value is shifting from pistons, camshafts and exhausts to drivelines, motors, electronics and thermal management.
  • Trade politics as a new variable (2025–26): tariffs and export controls now shape sourcing decisions as much as cost does.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Electrification content: drivelines, traction motors, battery housings, thermal systems and high-voltage connectors replace engine parts. Sona's BEV revenue is already 44% of its automotive sales.
  2. Lightweighting: aluminium and high-strength steel replacing iron, which favours casting and forming specialists (Craftsman, Endurance, ASK).
  3. Electronics and software content: sensors, controllers and displays; mostly covered in 024 but increasingly bundled into mechanical systems.
  4. Safety regulation: airbags, ABS on all two-wheelers (see 018) and Bharat NCAP raise content per vehicle.
  5. Magnet-free and rare-earth-light motors: a direct response to China's export controls, and a potential Indian advantage.
  6. Adjacent markets: suppliers using the same precision skills in robotics, aerospace and defence; Sona's ₹800 cr robotics pipeline is the clearest example.

The honest read

This is the healthiest part of the auto family right now, because suppliers capture both volume growth and rising content per vehicle. But the 2026 return (+22% basket, +6% median) shows the market is paying only for the structural winners. The discipline is to separate suppliers whose content grows (EV driveline, electronics, safety, lightweighting) from those simply riding volumes, and to check tariff and magnet exposure before paying for export growth.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What is content per vehicle today, and how does it change on the next platform?
  2. How much of revenue is designed-in versus re-quoted annually, and what price-down was agreed this year?
  3. What share of revenue is exports, and how much goes to the US under Section 232?
  4. What is the customer concentration, and which models drive it?
  5. How much margin was lost to commodities, and when does pass-through catch up?
  6. What is the EV share of the order book, and does it need rare-earth magnets?
  7. What is capacity utilisation, and what capex is planned?
  8. (Exporters) What is the currency hedge policy?

Red flags

  • Revenue growth with falling gross margins over several quarters.
  • Heavy dependence on one customer or one model.
  • Capex into engine-only product lines.
  • Receivables stretching as OEMs manage their own working capital.

18 · Myths and live debates

What you'll hear, and the evidence

ClaimEvidence
"EVs will destroy component makers."They destroy engine-part demand and create driveline, motor, electronics and thermal demand. Sona's BEV revenue is 44% of its auto sales and UNO Minda's EV systems grew 130%.
"US tariffs will crush Indian suppliers."They have flattened US exports (about $6.2 bn, unchanged) but total exports still grew 9.3% in H1 FY26 as Europe and others took up the slack. The bigger risk is losing new programme awards.
"Suppliers are just a leveraged bet on car sales."For commodity parts, yes. For content winners, no: the industry grew 12.7% in FY26 while vehicle volumes grew far less.
"The magnet problem is temporary."India imports 100% of sintered NdFeB magnets. The ₹7,280 cr scheme takes years to build 6,000 tpa, and the localisation deadline has already been challenged.
"Buy the whole sector in an upcycle."In 2026 the basket rose 22% while the median stock rose 6%. Selection has mattered more than the sector call for three years.

19 · Glossary

Every term you'll meet

The supply chain

OEM
The vehicle maker that assembles and badges the vehicle (see 017).
Tier-1
A supplier that sells complete systems directly to the OEM.
Tier-2 / tier-3
Suppliers to the tier-1s: components, sub-assemblies and raw processing.
Content per vehicle
The rupee value of a supplier's parts in one vehicle; the key growth metric.
Designed-in part
A part specified by the OEM for a model's life, hard to switch.
Price-down
The annual price reduction OEMs expect, usually 1–3%.
Pass-through
Recovering raw-material cost changes from the customer, usually with a lag (see 002).
Programme / platform win
Being chosen to supply a part for a vehicle model or platform.
Order book
Lifetime value of awarded programmes; Sona's is 5.4 times annual revenue.
Aftermarket
Replacement parts sold after the vehicle is in use; higher margin.
OES
Original equipment spares: replacement parts sold through the OEM's own network.
Customer concentration
The share of revenue from the largest one or two customers.
Localisation
Making a part in India rather than importing it (see 017).
PPAP (production part approval)
The documented process by which an OEM approves a supplier's part for mass production; it takes months and makes switching suppliers costly.
Validation cycle
The testing period before a part is approved; long cycles are why tariffs do not instantly displace embedded suppliers.
Value added per employee
A measure of how much engineering, rather than assembly, a supplier does.
Kitting / JIT supply
Delivering parts in sequence, just in time for the assembly line.

Products and processes

Wiring harness
The bundled electrical wiring of a vehicle; Motherson's core product.
Forging
Shaping hot metal under pressure for strong parts such as gears and axles.
Casting / die-casting
Pouring molten metal into moulds; aluminium die-casting is central to two-wheeler parts.
Machining
Cutting metal to precise dimensions; Craftsman's business.
Stamping
Pressing sheet metal into panels and structures.
Sintering
Forming parts by compacting and heating metal powder.
Differential gear
The gear set that lets wheels turn at different speeds; Sona's speciality.
Traction motor
The electric motor that drives an EV; needs rare-earth magnets in most designs.
NdFeB magnet
Neodymium-iron-boron permanent magnet, the standard EV motor magnet.
Leaf spring / parabolic spring
Truck suspension components; Jamna Auto's products.
After-treatment system
Exhaust equipment that cleans emissions (see 011 for the chemistry, 017 for norms).
Body-in-white
The welded vehicle structure before paint and trim.
Lightweighting
Replacing heavy metal with aluminium or high-strength steel to cut weight.

Trade and policy

Section 232
The US law used to tariff imports on national-security grounds; 25% on auto parts.
Reciprocal tariff
A country-specific duty layered on top of product tariffs (see 008).
ACMA
The Automotive Component Manufacturers Association, the industry body and data source.
PLI
Production-linked incentive scheme (see 017).
Rules of origin
The tests that decide a part's country of origin for tariffs and trade deals.
Anti-dumping exposure
Duties on imported inputs such as steel that raise a supplier's cost base (see 002).
Export incentive
Duty drawback and similar schemes supporting exporters.
Trade deficit (components)
When component imports exceed exports; India slipped into one in H1 FY26.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. What share of a vehicle do suppliers make, and why does that matter?

About 70% of its value, so the component industry is larger and more diverse than the vehicle makers themselves.

2. What is "content per vehicle" and why is it the key metric?

The rupee value of a supplier's parts in one vehicle; it lets suppliers grow far faster than vehicle volumes as safety, comfort and electrification add content.

3. How big is the Indian industry now?

₹7.6 lakh cr (about $86 bn) in FY26, up 12.7%, double its size five years ago, with $24 bn of exports.

4. What are US Section 232 tariffs doing to exports?

A 25% duty covers roughly 55% of India's US-bound parts; US exports are flat at about $6.2 bn while other markets grow.

5. Why is Sona BLW re-rating?

Battery-electric revenue rose 107% to 44% of its automotive sales, with an order book of ₹24,000 cr, about 5.4 times annual revenue.

6. What is the rare-earth magnet problem?

India imports 100% of its sintered NdFeB magnets from China, which controls exports; a ₹7,280 cr scheme targets 6,000 tpa of domestic capacity.

7. Why did margins fall across the group in Q1 FY27?

Steel, aluminium, gas and wage inflation hit before contractual pass-through caught up; UNO Minda's margin fell to 10.3% from 10.7%.

8. Which seasonal window is strongest, and why?

April–July (12 of 15 years, median +13.4 points): programme starts, price settlements and the first read on the year's volumes.

9. What does the gap between the basket (+22%) and the median stock (+6%) in 2026 tell you?

That returns are concentrated in structural winners; buying the whole sector would have captured much less.

10. Which suppliers face long-term decline?

Those tied to internal combustion engines alone: pistons, camshafts, exhaust and fuel systems, unless they diversify.
