Industry primer · 60 of 278 · Defence family, part 4

Explosives

Every tonne of coal, iron ore and limestone dug from an open-cast mine in India is loosened first with explosives, and every tunnel, dam and hill road needs them too. The same chemistry — ammonium nitrate, fuel, and carefully timed detonators — also makes rocket propellant, artillery shells and warheads. Over fifteen years India's largest explosives maker has used that overlap to become a defence company. This primer explains the products, the mining calendar, the raw materials, how the listed companies make money, and what the latest wave of deals — Solar's African takeover and Apollo's bid for Premier — means for the industry.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

A mining-chemicals business that became a munitions business

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Imagine trying to dig a mountain of rock with a spoon — it would take forever. Miners instead drill small holes, fill them with a special mix that burns incredibly fast, and set it off with a tiny spark at exactly the right moment, so the rock cracks into pieces a digger can scoop up. The companies in this industry make that mix and the tiny sparks. The same science that cracks rock can also push a rocket into the sky or make the shell a tank fires, so the biggest explosives company in India now makes rockets and ammunition for the Army as well as explosives for coal mines.

Say it to a client in one minute

Indian explosives is now a defence growth story built on a mining cash cow.

  • Mining base: bulk and cartridge explosives for coal, limestone and iron-ore mines and infrastructure; Coal India is the largest buyer.
  • Defence growth: Solar Industries' defence revenue rose 123% in Q1 FY27; its defence order book is over ₹18,000 cr (Pinaka rockets, 155 mm shells, loitering munitions, counter-drone systems). It guides to ₹14,000 cr of revenue in FY27, up about 42%.
  • Deals: Solar is buying South Africa's Omnia Holdings for $1.355 bn in cash; Apollo Micro Systems is buying 41% of Premier Explosives; GOCL has sold IDL Explosives to Apollo.
  • Stocks: Solar +51% in 2026 but 17% below its 8 September high since the Omnia announcement; Premier +27%.
  • Calendar: June has beaten the Nifty in 12 of 15 years; July–September is weak because the monsoon slows mining.

The key point for an investor is that mining explosives and military energetics share raw materials, safety systems, licences and know-how. A company that can safely make and handle thousands of tonnes of explosive a year has most of what it needs to make propellants and warheads; what it lacks — military qualification and customer relationships — takes years to build. Solar began that journey more than a decade ago and now earns a large share of its growth from defence.

The chain in one breath

  • Gas becomes ammonia, then nitric acid, then ammonium nitrate.
  • Explosives makers mix it with fuel and emulsifiers in licensed plants.
  • At the mine, trucks pump it into holes and detonators set it off.
  • For the Army, the same companies fill shells, grenades and rocket motors.
  • Coal India's annual tenders set the price for much of the domestic mining business.
Fund-manager lens

The critical economic feature is that domestic mining explosives are a low-growth, price-competitive business — Coal India awards large volumes by tender — while defence and exports earn more and grow faster. Solar's valuation rests on the shift in mix: defence is guided to about ₹4,500 cr of its ₹14,000 cr FY27 revenue, and international business grew 65% in Q1. The Omnia deal would add another large, mostly mining-and-fertiliser business, which is why the market questioned it.

The rest of the industry

Orica, the world's largest commercial explosives company, operates in India but is listed in Australia. Munitions India and other former ordnance factories make much of the Army's ammunition. IDL Explosives is now part of Apollo Micro Systems (058). Ammonium-nitrate makers such as Deepak Fertilisers sit in chemicals. The basket therefore uses only Solar and Premier.

5 · How money is made

Tendered volume, services and high-margin defence

SegmentHow it is soldMargin shapeWhat drives it
Coal India and PSU miningAnnual tenders, often split among several suppliersThin; price-competitiveCoal output, tender prices, ammonium nitrate cost
Private mining, cement and infrastructureContracts and servicesBetter; service contentCement and infrastructure activity
International miningLocal plants in Africa, Asia and elsewhereBetter than IndiaGold, copper and coal mining abroad
DefenceMinistry contracts, often after years of trials; exportsHighestOrders for rockets, shells, grenades, drones

Ammonium nitrate is the largest cost in mining explosives, and its price moves with ammonia and natural gas. Coal India contracts include price-variation clauses, but with a lag, so sharp rises in ammonium nitrate squeeze margins for a quarter or two. Solar has insulated itself partly by making some of its own inputs and partly by growing defence, where contracts are priced on cost plus margin and margins are higher.

Defence changes the shape of the business. Orders are large and multi-year — Pinaka rocket contracts run into thousands of crores — but revenue arrives in lumps as batches pass acceptance tests. Solar's defence order book of over ₹18,000 cr is about four years of its guided FY27 defence revenue. Premier's results show the other side: a quarter with few defence dispatches cut its revenue 28% and its margin to 5.7%.

Why exports matter

Two export businesses are growing. In mining, Solar runs plants abroad and grew international revenue 65% in Q1 FY27. In defence, the war in Ukraine exposed a worldwide shortage of artillery shells and rockets, and Indian makers — with licensed capacity and lower costs — have won export orders. Defence exports carry some of the industry's best margins, and they are a key reason Solar's valuation has risen above that of global mining-explosives peers.

The logic is clear: Omnia's BME is one of Africa's leading mining-explosives businesses, and Solar already operates in several African countries. The concerns are also clear. The deal roughly doubles Solar's revenue with a lower-margin business that includes fertilisers, it will be funded with cash and debt, and it arrives just as investors had begun to value Solar mainly as a defence company. The fall in the stock is the market re-weighting Solar back toward mining — the question for the next two years is whether integration and cross-selling justify that.

Ammonium nitrate is also one of the most tightly regulated chemicals in India after a series of accidents worldwide, including the 2020 Beirut port explosion. Storage and transport rules add cost and limit who can compete. On the defence side, nitrocellulose and high-explosive capacity became scarce globally as Europe rushed to rebuild its ammunition stocks; companies that invested early, as Solar did, gained a lasting advantage.

7 · The business calendar

Mining seasons and defence deliveries

  • January–March: Coal India's highest-production quarter as it chases annual targets; blasting volumes peak; defence deliveries rush before the fiscal year ends.
  • April–June: pre-monsoon mining remains strong; new Coal India tenders and defence orders.
  • July–September: the monsoon floods open-cast mines and slows blasting; the weakest quarter for mining explosives.
  • October–December: mining recovers after the rains; demand builds again.

That mining calendar is why this industry's stock pattern differs from the rest of the Defence family: January–March is only mildly weak, and the pre-monsoon months, especially June, have been the strongest. Coal India's production — 768 mt in FY26, below its 875 mt target and lower than the year before, while captive and commercial mines grew 10% to 210 mt (see 036) — is the main volume driver for the domestic business.

  • Windows: Feb–Mar beat the Nifty in 8 of 15 years (median +0.2 pts); Apr–Jul beat the Nifty in 11 of 15 years (median +14.2 pts); Aug–Sep beat the Nifty in 7 of 14 years (median +0.3 pts); Oct–Dec beat the Nifty in 8 of 14 years (median +0.6 pts); Sep–Nov beat the Nifty in 6 of 14 years (median -3.4 pts); Jan–Mar beat the Nifty in 6 of 15 years (median -1.5 pts).

Control: small caps as a whole (Nifty Smallcap 100) beat the Nifty in April in 11 of 15 years, by an average of 3.0 points, and lag in January–February. Part of any April strength is this market-wide effect. After removing it, this industry's own April edge is about +3.4 points.

Reading it: the basket is Solar Industries alone until 2016 and Solar plus Premier Explosives after that, so it is largely Solar's record. June has beaten the Nifty in 12 of 15 years (average +6.9 points) and April–July in 11 of 15 (median +14.2). Unlike the rest of the Defence family, January–March is only mildly weak (6 of 15, median −1.5) and February–March is a coin toss (8 of 15). July is the weakest month (5 of 16). The difference is mining: blasting peaks before the monsoon, when Coal India pushes production, and slows in July–September when open-cast mines flood.

A tendency, not a rule: small samples, survivorship bias (only names still listed) and equal weighting. Use it for timing context, not as a signal.

The exceptions, and why

  • April–July failed in 2013 (−20.9), 2016 (−16.9), 2018 (−16.7) and 2019 (−11.9) — years of mining slowdowns (2013) or small-cap crashes. The best runs were 2014 (+118.1) and 2026 (+55.1).
  • June failed only in 2018 (−10.5), 2023 (−1.9) and 2016 (−1.6).
  • July has been weak lately: 2025 (−17.8) and 2026 (−11.8) were among its worst years.
How to use this

This is mostly one company's record, so it mixes Solar's own news (results, defence orders, the Omnia deal) with any industry season. The June strength fits the mining calendar and fourth-quarter results; the July weakness fits the monsoon. Treat it as context, not a signal.

9 · What moves margins

Eight drivers

  1. Defence share of revenue. The single biggest margin lever.
  2. Ammonium nitrate cost. Moves with ammonia and gas; passed on with a lag.
  3. Coal India tender prices and volumes. Set much of the domestic mining business.
  4. International mining. Higher margins; exposure to gold, copper and coal abroad.
  5. Defence exports. The global shell and rocket shortage.
  6. Acceptance timing. Defence batches create lumpy quarters.
  7. Capacity and backward integration. Own propellant and high-explosive capacity.
  8. Acquisitions. Omnia would change Solar's mix toward mining and fertilisers.

The anatomy of the cycles

  1. Mining explosives (2012–2016): growth tied to coal; Solar's 2014 surge came with the election and hopes of a mining revival.
  2. The defence build (2017–2020): Solar invested in propellants and ammunition before orders came; the stocks fell in the 2018–19 small-cap bust.
  3. Defence and exports pay off (2021–2025): grenade, rocket and shell orders, then the Ukraine-driven global shortage; the basket rose 84%, 83%, 100% and 58% in 2021–2024.
  4. Consolidation (2025–2026): Apollo buys IDL and Premier; Solar bids for Omnia.

Patterns that repeat

  • Mining explosives alone are a low-return business, as Orica's flat fifteen-year price shows.
  • Defence re-rates the sector, as Rheinmetall and Solar both show — and can de-rate it quickly, as Rheinmetall's 49% fall shows.
  • Large acquisitions are met with scepticism until integration proves out.

Two forces shape the global industry. Mining demand follows commodity prices; gold and copper strength has supported it since 2024. Defence demand changed structurally after 2022, when the war in Ukraine showed that armies can fire more shells in a month than Europe was making in a year. European governments have since signed multi-year ammunition contracts, and NATO members agreed in 2025 to raise defence and related spending toward 5% of GDP. Indian makers benefit through exports and through the Indian Army's own drive to hold larger stocks after Operation Sindoor.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Electronic detonators. Programmable timing improves fragmentation and safety; they need chips and software.
  2. Bulk emulsions and site mixing. Safer to transport and cheaper per tonne of rock broken.
  3. Blast design software. Drones and 3D modelling plan each blast; explosives makers sell services, not just product.
  4. Loitering munitions and counter-drone systems. Solar's Nagastra and Bhargavastra show explosives makers moving up to complete systems (see 061).
  5. Guided rockets and extended-range shells. Higher value per round.
  6. Insensitive munitions. Explosives designed not to detonate accidentally, required by modern militaries.

What is priced in

Solar trades at about 66 times its Q1 FY27 profit annualised — a defence-company multiple on a business that is still mostly mining explosives. The Omnia deal would add a large, lower-margin mining and fertiliser business, and the stock's 17% fall says the market is unwilling to pay the same multiple for it. Premier's price is anchored by Apollo's offer. The key questions for the next year are whether Solar's defence growth holds near its 70% guidance, how Omnia is financed, and whether competition from new private ammunition makers erodes margins.

The honest read

Solar has built one of the best businesses in Indian defence by reinvesting mining cash flows into propellants and ammunition before the market cared. Its valuation now assumes that success continues; the Omnia deal adds risk as well as scale. The smaller listed players are either being absorbed (Premier, IDL), have left the business (GOCL), or trade too thinly to invest in (Keltech).

19 · Glossary

Every term you'll meet

Commercial explosives

Ammonium nitrate (AN)
The main oxidiser in commercial explosives, also a fertiliser.
ANFO
Ammonium nitrate mixed with fuel oil — the simplest bulk explosive.
Emulsion explosive
A water-resistant mix of oxidiser solution and fuel, safer to handle.
Bulk explosive
Explosive delivered and pumped into holes at the mine by truck.
Cartridge explosive
Packaged explosive sticks for smaller operations.
Detonator
A small device that starts the explosion.
Electronic detonator
A programmable detonator with precise timing.
Detonating cord
A cord filled with explosive that carries a detonation between charges.
Initiating system
Detonators, cords and connectors that set off a blast.
Blast hole
A drilled hole that is filled with explosive.
Fragmentation
How finely rock is broken — better fragmentation lowers mining costs.
Site mixing unit
A truck that mixes bulk explosive at the mine.
Open-cast mine
A surface mine, where most Indian coal is produced.
Overburden
Rock and soil removed to reach coal or ore.
Magazine
A licensed, secured store for explosives.
Stemming
Inert material packed on top of the charge in a blast hole to contain the energy.
Burden and spacing
The distances between blast holes and the rock face, which set how rock breaks.
Misfire
A charge that fails to detonate — a serious safety hazard.
Flyrock
Rock thrown dangerously far by a poorly designed blast.

Defence energetics

Energetics
Explosives, propellants and pyrotechnics as a group.
Propellant
A substance that burns to push a rocket or shell.
Nitrocellulose
The base of most gun propellants.
Ammonium perchlorate
The oxidiser in most solid rocket propellants.
HMX / RDX
Powerful military high explosives.
TNT
A classic military explosive used to fill shells.
155 mm shell
The standard NATO and Indian artillery calibre.
Pinaka
India's multi-barrel rocket launcher; Solar makes its rockets.
Loitering munition
A drone that circles a target area and then strikes.
Counter-drone system
A system to detect and defeat hostile drones.
Chaff and flares
Countermeasures that decoy radar- and heat-guided missiles.
Insensitive munition
Ammunition designed not to detonate accidentally.
Warhead
The explosive payload of a missile or rocket.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. What is ANFO?

Ammonium nitrate mixed with fuel oil, the simplest bulk explosive.

2. Why can an explosives maker become a defence company?

Mining explosives and military energetics share raw materials, safety systems, licences and know-how.

3. How fast did Solar's defence revenue grow in Q1 FY27?

123%; its defence order book is over ₹18,000 cr.

4. What is Solar buying, and for how much?

South Africa's Omnia Holdings, for $1.355 bn (₹12,951 cr) in cash.

5. How did the market react?

Solar fell 13.6% the next day and ended 17% below its 8 September high.

6. What is happening to Premier Explosives?

Apollo Micro Systems agreed to buy 41.33% for ₹1,550 cr, with an open offer of ₹975.66 cr.

7. Why is GOCL excluded?

It sold IDL Explosives to Apollo in November 2025 and is now mainly monetising Hyderabad land.

8. Why is July weak for the stocks?

The monsoon floods open-cast mines and slows blasting.

9. What is the main raw material in mining explosives?

Technical ammonium nitrate, made from ammonia and nitric acid.

10. What do Orica and Rheinmetall show?

Mining explosives alone have been a low-return business, while ammunition re-rated sharply after 2022 — and can de-rate quickly.
