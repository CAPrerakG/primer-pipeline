Apparel & Footwear Retail

The companies that design, source and sell clothes and shoes in India: value chains like Zudio, premium brand houses like Louis Philippe and Van Heusen, ethnic-wear specialists like Manyavar, innerwear makers like Jockey's licensee, and footwear retailers from Bata to Metro. It is a business of stores, brands and inventory — and in 2026, of slow like-for-like growth after a long valuation boom.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Growth from new stores, not from existing ones

Explain it to a 10-year-old

A clothes company is part designer, part shopkeeper. It decides what shirts, kurtas or shoes people will want months from now, gets factories to make them, and puts them in shops. If it guesses right, everything sells at full price. If it guesses wrong, the clothes sit on shelves until the end-of-season sale, when they are sold cheaply. Some companies sell very cheap, trendy clothes to lots of people; others sell expensive brands to fewer people. Right now in India, the cheap-and-trendy shops are opening new stores everywhere, while people are not buying much more in each existing shop than they did last year.

Say it to a client in one minute

Indian apparel and footwear retail is growing through store expansion while demand per store is flat to slightly positive.

  • Value fashion leads: Trent's revenue +19% to ₹5,666 cr with EBITDA +36%, on 982 Zudio stores — but like-for-like growth only in low single digits.
  • Premium and ethnic are slow: Manyavar revenue +7% (same-store +3.8%), ABLBL +11%, Page +7.9% with volume +5.7%, Bata +4%.
  • Margins are being defended: Manyavar at 44.6% EBITDA, Metro at 29.8%, Bata's profit +23% on premiumisation; ABFRL still loses money.
  • GST reset: since September 2025, apparel and footwear up to ₹2,500 attract 5% GST and above that 18%, helping value retail and hurting premium price points.
  • The stocks have de-rated: the basket is down in 2025 and 2026; most leaders are 30–78% below their peaks.

Analogy

Think of a restaurant chain versus a single famous restaurant. The chain (Zudio) grows by opening another outlet every few days and keeping each one busy; the famous restaurant (a premium brand) grows by raising prices and filling tables at special occasions. Both can be excellent businesses, but in a year when fewer people eat out, the chain still grows because it opens new outlets — while the famous restaurant sees the slowdown immediately.

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • The cloth: cotton and polyester are spun and woven into fabric.
  • Choosing: designers decide what styles to make, long before the season.
  • Sewing: factories cut and stitch the clothes.
  • The name: a brand makes people trust the clothes and pay more.
  • The shop: the clothes go to shops, dealers and websites.
  • You: people buy most at festivals, weddings and sales.
Fund-manager lens

The critical number in fashion retail is like-for-like (same-store) growth, because it separates demand from expansion. A retailer opening stores can report 19% revenue growth while each store sells little more than last year, as Trent did this quarter. That growth is real, but it depends on finding new catchments and consumes capital in fit-outs, rent deposits and inventory. When like-for-like growth turns negative the whole model is at risk, because new stores cannibalise old ones. The second number is full-price sell-through: brands that sell most of their stock before the sale season earn far higher margins than those that rely on discounting.

The levers

  1. Like-for-like growth: sales per existing store; the demand signal.
  2. Store additions: the growth engine for value retail, measured in stores and square feet.
  3. Full-price sell-through: the share of stock sold before discounting.
  4. Inventory days: slower turns mean more markdowns and working capital.
  5. Channel mix: own stores, franchises, department stores, dealers and online have very different margins.
  6. Rent and staff: the fixed costs that punish weak stores.
Analogy

A fashion retailer is like a bakery that must decide in the morning how many cakes to bake. Bake too few and customers leave empty-handed; bake too many and the rest are sold half-price at closing time. The best retailers bake smaller batches more often — which is essentially what Zudio's fast-fashion model does.

7 · The business calendar

Festivals, weddings and end-of-season sales

  • April–June: summer season launches and the April–May wedding window; end-of-season sales from late June.
  • July–September: the monsoon lull, then festive stocking begins.
  • October–December: Dussehra, Diwali and the peak wedding season — the most important quarter for ethnic wear and footwear.
  • January–March: winter end-of-season sales, fewer wedding dates in some years, and the weakest margins.
  • Wedding dates: the number of auspicious dates in a quarter moves ethnic-wear sales sharply from year to year.

  • Weakest months: Jan -2.5 pts (beat in 6 of 15 years); Mar -1.7 pts (beat in 4 of 15 years); Oct -0.9 pts (beat in 6 of 15 years).
  • Windows: Feb–Mar beat the Nifty in 6 of 15 years (median -1.0 pts); Apr–Jul beat the Nifty in 11 of 15 years (median +8.3 pts); Aug–Sep beat the Nifty in 8 of 14 years (median +0.7 pts); Oct–Dec beat the Nifty in 10 of 14 years (median +3.9 pts); Sep–Nov beat the Nifty in 8 of 14 years (median +1.7 pts); Jan–Mar beat the Nifty in 4 of 15 years (median -5.1 pts).

Control: small caps as a whole (Nifty Smallcap 100) beat the Nifty in April in 11 of 15 years, by an average of 3.0 points, and lag in January–February. Part of any April strength is this market-wide effect. After removing it, this industry's own April edge is about 0.0 points.

Reading it: a moderate pattern that follows the Indian shopping calendar. April–July has beaten the Nifty in 11 of 15 years (median +8.3 points) and October–December in 10 of 14 (median +3.9), with December the most reliable single month (12 of 15, +2.5 points) as festive and wedding sales are reported. January–March is the weak stretch — 4 of 15 years, median −5.1 — when end-of-season sales cut margins and the winter quarter's results are digested.

A tendency, not a rule: small samples, survivorship bias (only names still listed) and equal weighting. Use it for timing context, not as a signal.

The exceptions, and why

  • April–July failed in 2019 (−19.4), 2020 (−13.8), 2018 (−9.2) and 2012 (−1.0): two small-cap sell-offs and the Covid lockdown that shut every store. The best runs were 2021 (+35.9) and 2015 (+15.7).
  • October–December failed in 2025 (−12.2), 2022 (−10.2) and 2019 (−5.0) — each a year when festive demand disappointed.
  • January–March has failed in 11 of 15 years, worst in 2025 (−19.9), 2026 (−11.0) and 2023 (−10.9); it worked only in 2019, 2022, 2020 and 2021.
How to use this

The calendar matches the business: margins are weakest in the January–March end-of-season-sale quarter, and the festive and wedding quarter is when brands make their year. Treat January–March weakness as the more reliable half of the pattern — it has held in each of the last four years — and the April–July strength as a tendency.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Eight drivers

  1. Discretionary income: urban wage growth, inflation and household budgets.
  2. Store expansion: especially value retail in smaller towns.
  3. Wedding and festival calendars: ethnic wear and footwear peaks.
  4. GST slabs: the ₹2,500 threshold now separates 5% and 18%.
  5. Fabric costs: cotton and polyester prices.
  6. Discounting intensity: online marketplaces and end-of-season sales.
  7. Quick commerce and online: changing where basics and footwear are bought.
  8. Quality rules: BIS quality-control orders for footwear favour organised makers.

The anatomy of the cycles

  1. The brand re-rating (2012–2017): organised brands took share from unorganised tailors and local shops, helped by GST in 2017; +70% that year.
  2. The small-cap bust (2018–2019): falls of 50%+ in small apparel names.
  3. Covid and reopening (2020–2021): stores shut, then a surge in demand and a small-cap boom (+94% in 2021).
  4. The value-retail boom (2022–2024): Zudio's expansion made Trent the sector's defining stock (+126% and +133%).
  5. The de-rating (2025–2026): weak like-for-like growth, a GST reset and stretched valuations; most leaders 30–78% below their peaks.

Patterns that repeat

  • Store growth can mask weak demand for a long time — until like-for-like growth turns.
  • High-margin franchise models re-rate furthest and fall furthest (Manyavar −63% from peak).
  • Innerwear follows cotton: Lux −55% in 2022 when cotton spiked.
  • IPOs arrive at peaks: Go Fashion (2021), Sai Silks (2023), Credo (2023) are all well below listing-era highs.

11 · The global picture

Fast fashion won the world; India is building its own

Globally, apparel retail has been reshaped by fast fashion — Inditex (Zara) and H&M with short design-to-shelf cycles — and then by ultra-cheap online sellers such as Shein. India's version of that story is Zudio: a low-price, fast-turning chain that grows by opening stores in towns other brands overlooked. Foreign chains (Zara via a Tata partnership, H&M, Uniqlo) operate in India but at higher price points. Shein returned in 2025 through a licensing arrangement with Reliance Retail. The Indian market remains more fragmented and more ethnic-wear-heavy than most, which is why specialists like Manyavar and Kalamandir exist at scale.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • From tailors to brands (1990s–2010s): ready-made garments displaced custom tailoring.
  • From department stores to exclusive brand outlets and value chains (2010s–2020s).
  • From premium to value (2022–2026): Zudio's growth versus flat premium like-for-like.
  • From offline to omnichannel: marketplaces, brand websites and now quick commerce for basics.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Automated replenishment: Page's Advanced Retail Scheme links dealer stock to actual sales.
  2. Demand forecasting and data-led design: smaller batches, faster reorders.
  3. Omnichannel inventory: one stock pool for stores and online.
  4. RFID and store analytics: faster counts and fewer lost sales.
  5. Quick commerce for basics: innerwear, socks and slippers in minutes.
  6. Social commerce: Instagram-led discovery, especially for designer ethnic wear.

The honest read

The sector's long re-rating assumed that rising incomes would lift spending per store for years. That has not happened in 2025–26: growth is coming from new stores and new towns, not from existing ones. The market has responded by cutting valuations across the board, including for the best operators. The businesses that deserve attention now are those that keep margins and cash flow intact while demand is flat — Metro, Manyavar and, on its turnaround, Bata — and the one that is still compounding stores at high returns, Trent, if its like-for-like growth recovers. The ones to be careful with are loss-making or highly leveraged multi-brand retailers and the illiquid small caps, several of which have fallen 70% or more.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What was like-for-like growth, and how did it split between transactions and ticket size?
  2. How many stores opened and closed, and what is the payback on new stores?
  3. What share of sales was at full price, and how did end-of-season discounting compare with last year?
  4. What are inventory days, and how much stock is more than one season old?
  5. How has the GST change affected sales above and below ₹2,500?
  6. What is the pre-Ind AS EBITDA margin?
  7. How much of growth is from tier-2 and tier-3 towns, and how do those stores perform?
  8. (Licensees) When do key brand licences come up for renewal?

Red flags

  • Negative like-for-like growth masked by store additions.
  • Inventory growing faster than sales.
  • Rising franchise receivables.
  • Repeated one-off markdown charges.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. Why can revenue grow 19% while demand is weak?

New stores add revenue; like-for-like growth, which measures existing stores, was in low single digits at Trent.

2. How did GST change in September 2025?

Apparel and footwear up to ₹2,500 per piece moved to 5%; above ₹2,500 they attract 18%.

3. Why does Manyavar have a 44.6% EBITDA margin?

Very high gross margins on wedding wear and a franchise model in which franchisees bear most store costs.

4. What is full-price sell-through?

The share of inventory sold before discounting — the key to fashion margins.

5. Why did Page's margin fall?

Logistics and manpower disruption during its replenishment-system rollout; EBITDA margin 20.3% against 22.4%.

6. What is the most reliable seasonal signal?

January–March weakness: the basket beat the Nifty in only 4 of 15 years.

7. What does Ind AS 116 do to retailer margins?

It moves rent below EBITDA, making reported margins higher than pre-Ind AS margins.

8. What happened to ABFRL in 2025?

Its lifestyle brands were demerged into ABLBL; the remaining company still reported a ₹249 cr loss this quarter.

9. Where is Zudio expanding?

More than 80% of new stores in Q1 FY27 were in tier-2 and tier-3 towns and peripheral markets.

10. Why did innerwear stocks fall in 2022?

Cotton prices spiked and demand faded; Lux fell 55%.
