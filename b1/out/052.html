Industry primer · 52 of 278 · Electronics & EMS family, part 3

Electronics Manufacturing Services

Electronics manufacturing services (EMS) companies build electronic products for brands that design and sell them: phones, televisions, air-conditioners, smart meters, car electronics, industrial controls, medical devices and aircraft systems. India has turned this into one of its fastest-growing manufacturing industries, with iPhone exports alone worth about ₹2 trillion last year. But the listed companies fall into two camps with very different economics — and in 2026 the market is rewarding only one of them.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Two businesses under one name

Explain it to a 10-year-old

When you buy a phone with a famous brand name, the company that designed it often did not build it. Another company — a contract manufacturer — bought the parts, put them together, tested the phone and packed it. Some contract manufacturers build millions of the same thing, like phones or air-conditioners; they are like a giant bakery making one kind of bread very cheaply. Others build a few hundred of many complicated things, like boards for trains, satellites or hospital machines; they are like a cake shop making special orders. The giant bakery earns a tiny profit on each loaf but sells millions. The cake shop earns more on each order but must be very skilled.

Say it to a client in one minute

EMS in India is growing fast, but high-volume consumer assembly and high-mix industrial electronics are now diverging sharply.

  • Scale: electronics exports $47.96 bn in FY26 (+24%); smartphone exports about ₹2.6 tn, iPhones about ₹2 tn.
  • High-mix winners: Syrma revenue +67% and profit +112%; Avalon revenue +50%, profit +145%, order book ₹2,208 cr; Cyient DLM order book a record ₹2,598 cr.
  • Consumer assemblers squeezed: Dixon revenue +21% but margins compressed after the mobile PLI ended in March 2026; PG Electroplast revenue +35%, profit +13%.
  • Cash matters: Kaynes revenue +41% but profit −24% and operating cash flow −₹250 cr; stock −54% from its peak.
  • The next leg is components: Dixon's display and camera modules, Vivo joint venture, ECMS projects (051).

Analogy

High-volume EMS is a printing press for bestsellers: enormous runs, razor-thin margins per copy, and the publisher can take the next book to another printer. High-mix EMS is a bespoke tailor for demanding clients: every order is different and harder, but once a client trusts the fit, they rarely change tailors.

Where most value is captured

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Ingredients: thousands of tiny parts, most bought from abroad.
  • Recipe: someone designs how everything fits together.
  • Boards: robots place parts on green boards and solder them.
  • Finishing: workers assemble the case, load software and test it.
  • Shipping: products go to shops in India and to other countries.
  • Selling: the brand sells it with its own name on it.
Fund-manager lens

The critical measure in EMS is value added — revenue minus bought-in components — because headline revenue is inflated by pass-through parts. A phone assembler with ₹15,000 cr of revenue may add only a small fraction of that itself; a high-mix maker with ₹500 cr of revenue may add far more per rupee. That is why EBITDA margins range from about 3% to 15% across companies doing, on the surface, the same thing. The second measure is cash conversion: EMS growth consumes working capital, and a company growing revenue 40% with negative operating cash flow — as Kaynes did this quarter — is financing its customers. The third is dependence on incentives: the mobile PLI's end in March 2026 showed how much of assemblers' margin was policy.

What is missing

The largest electronics manufacturers in India are unlisted or foreign: Foxconn, Pegatron's former plant (now Tata Electronics), Samsung, and the Tata Electronics iPhone plants. Bharat FIH (Foxconn) and several others have filed or planned listings. The listed EMS universe is therefore dominated by Indian mid-caps rather than the giants.

Why the PLI's end matters

The mobile PLI scheme, launched in 2020, paid manufacturers a percentage of incremental sales above a base year. It helped build India's phone-assembly industry and made up a meaningful share of assemblers' profits on low-margin volumes. Its expiry in March 2026 removed that support just as component costs rose — the reason Dixon's margins contracted in Q1 FY27 even as revenue grew 21%. Dixon's response — backward integration into displays and camera modules, and joint ventures with brands such as Vivo — is the industry's answer: earn more of the value, rather than depend on assembly fees and incentives.

Analogy

The PLI was like a sponsorship deal paying an athlete a bonus for every race won. It helped a lot of new athletes turn professional. When the sponsorship ended, only those who had become good enough to win on prize money alone stayed comfortable.

5b · A closer look

The room-air-conditioner chain: where three listed companies compete

Room air-conditioners are the second-largest consumer EMS product in India after phones, and three listed companies — Amber, PG Electroplast and Epack — depend on them. Brands such as Voltas, Blue Star, LG, Daikin and Panasonic design and sell the ACs, but increasingly outsource manufacturing of the complete unit or its main sub-assemblies. A split AC contains an indoor unit (heat-exchanger coil, fan, motor, control board, plastic casing) and an outdoor unit (compressor, coil, fan, valves). The compressor and many electronic parts are still imported; coils, plastics, sheet metal and control boards are increasingly made in India.

The economics are seasonal and weather-driven: production peaks from January to May, a hot summer lifts sell-through and next year's orders, and a mild or rainy summer leaves brands with inventory and cuts orders to contract makers. Margins depend on copper and aluminium prices (see 044) and on how much of the unit the contract maker produces itself — which is why PG Electroplast's compressor plan and Amber's component businesses matter more than their headline volumes.

7 · The business calendar

Summer for ACs, festivals for phones

  • January–May: room-AC production peaks ahead of summer; AC-heavy makers report their biggest quarters.
  • June–September: phones and TVs built for the festive season; monsoon softens AC demand.
  • October–December: festive retail sales; lower production after Diwali.
  • March: fiscal year-end; PLI and incentive claims; order-book resets.
  • Industrial and export EMS: driven by customers' programmes rather than seasons.

8 · What the stocks do

Seasonality test on TradingView prices

Basket: 14 names (Dixon Technologies (India) Ltd., Syrma SGS Technology Limited, Amber Enterprises India Ltd., Kaynes Technology India Ltd., PG Electroplast Limited, Avalon Technologies Limited, Cyient DLM Limited, Centum Electronics Limited, Optiemus Infracom Limited, Aimtron Electronics Limited, Vinyas Innovative Technologies Limited, Epack Durable Limited, Virtuoso Optoelectronics Limited, Elin Electronics Limited), equal-weighted, monthly from Oct 2011, compared with the Nifty 50.

  • Strongest months vs the Nifty: Jun +7.4 pts (beat in 11 of 15 years); Apr +6.0 pts (beat in 13 of 15 years); Sep +5.5 pts (beat in 12 of 14 years).
  • Weakest months: Mar -4.8 pts (beat in 4 of 15 years); May -0.2 pts (beat in 9 of 15 years); Feb -0.1 pts (beat in 7 of 15 years).
  • Windows: Feb–Mar beat the Nifty in 4 of 15 years (median -5.9 pts); Apr–Jul beat the Nifty in 12 of 15 years (median +23.6 pts); Aug–Sep beat the Nifty in 11 of 14 years (median +4.5 pts); Oct–Dec beat the Nifty in 9 of 14 years (median +5.6 pts); Sep–Nov beat the Nifty in 9 of 14 years (median +8.0 pts); Jan–Mar beat the Nifty in 5 of 15 years (median -3.8 pts).

Control: small caps as a whole (Nifty Smallcap 100) beat the Nifty in April in 11 of 15 years, by an average of 3.0 points, and lag in January–February. Part of any April strength is this market-wide effect. After removing it, this industry's own April edge is about +3.0 points.

Reading it: one of the strongest seasonal patterns in the library. April–July has beaten the Nifty in 12 of 15 years with a median of +23.6 points, and April alone in 13 of 15 (+6.0 points). February–March is the mirror image — 4 of 15, median −5.9 — and March alone 4 of 15 (−4.8 points). For the consumer-durables makers the logic is physical: room-air-conditioner production peaks from January to May ahead of summer, so their strongest quarter is reported in April–May, while the new financial year also resets PLI and order-book guidance.

A tendency, not a rule: small samples, survivorship bias (only names still listed) and equal weighting. Use it for timing context, not as a signal.

The exceptions, and why

  • April–July failed only in 2018 (−28.7), 2019 (−12.1) and 2022 (−5.9): the small-cap crash, its aftermath, and the 2022 consumer slowdown. The best runs were 2026 (+48.1) and 2017 (+37.5).
  • February–March succeeded only in 2021 (+22.0), 2015 (+12.8), 2012 (+3.6) and 2026 (+2.6); its worst was 2013 (−44.1).
  • October–December failed in 2025 (−18.0) and 2015 (−13.2) among five misses in 14 years.
How to use this

This is a pattern with a business reason for the AC makers and a market reason for everyone: results for the strongest consumer quarter arrive in April–May, and the new fiscal year brings fresh guidance and order books. The sample before 2018 is only two stocks, so the recent years deserve more weight — and April–July has worked in six of the eight years since 2019.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Eight drivers

  1. Brands' outsourcing to India: Apple, Samsung, Chinese handset makers, AC brands.
  2. Export programmes: "China plus one" for industrial, automotive and medical electronics.
  3. Defence and aerospace orders: Centum, Cyient DLM, Kaynes, Vinyas (see 058).
  4. Incentives: the end of the mobile PLI; ECMS for components.
  5. Component localisation: displays, cameras, PCBs — where the next margin lies.
  6. Summer weather: AC demand for Amber, PG Electroplast and Epack.
  7. Working capital: inventory and receivables in fast growth.
  8. Currency: imported components priced in dollars.

The anatomy of the cycles

  1. Pre-history (2012–2016): a handful of small companies, including PG Electroplast's collapse from optical discs.
  2. The first listings (2017–2019): Dixon and Amber list; the small-cap crash of 2018.
  3. The PLI boom (2020–2021): +133.8% and +137.3% as phone, AC and LED incentives launched.
  4. The EMS re-rating (2023–2024): Kaynes, Syrma and the others list; +84.5% and +102.0%.
  5. The split (2025–2026): consumer assemblers de-rate as PLI ends and margins thin; high-mix exporters surge.

Patterns that repeat

  • Incentive schemes create booms, and their expiry tests who can stand alone.
  • The market pays for margin and cash, not for revenue: Kaynes' 41% revenue growth came with a falling share price.
  • Listings cluster at re-rating peaks: Epack and Elin are 67–72% below their peaks.

11 · The global picture

From China to "China plus one"

Global EMS is dominated by Taiwanese and Chinese firms — Foxconn (Hon Hai), Pegatron, Luxshare, Wistron — and US and Singaporean groups such as Jabil, Flex and Celestica, which lead in high-mix and data-centre hardware. China built the world's electronics supply chain over three decades. Since 2020, tariffs, pandemic disruption and geopolitics have pushed brands to add capacity in India, Vietnam and Mexico. India's advantage is its domestic market and incentives; its gap is components, most of which are still imported. India's electronics exports reached $47.96 bn in FY26, led by smartphones — iPhone exports alone about ₹2 tn.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • From the US and Japan to Taiwan and China (1990s–2010s): the outsourcing of assembly.
  • To "China plus one" (2020–2026): tariffs, pandemic and security concerns.
  • From assembly to design and components: ODM and backward integration as the source of margin.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. ODM: designing products for brands, not just assembling them (Syrma, Dixon).
  2. Component integration: display modules, camera modules, PCBs and compressors.
  3. Automotive electronics: EV controllers, battery management and infotainment.
  4. Aerospace and defence electronics: high-reliability boards and systems (see 058).
  5. Automation and traceability: smarter surface-mount lines and full product traceability.
  6. Data-centre hardware: servers and networking equipment, a growing global EMS segment.

The honest read

Indian EMS is not one trade. The high-mix companies are delivering what investors hoped for in 2023–24 — growth with improving margins and exports — and their shares have re-rated sharply, so the question now is valuation. The consumer assemblers are larger and still growing, but the end of the mobile PLI shows how thin their economics are without support, and their future depends on component integration succeeding. Kaynes is the cautionary case: excellent revenue growth, but profits and cash lagging. Across the section, the metrics that matter are value added, cash conversion and order-book quality — not revenue.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What is value added as a share of revenue, and how is it trending?
  2. What share of revenue came from incentives last year, and what replaces it?
  3. What is operating cash flow, and why does it differ from profit?
  4. How concentrated is revenue by customer?
  5. What is the order book, and what share is export?
  6. What component integration is planned, with what capital and returns?
  7. How are component price and currency changes passed through?
  8. What is the ramp-up plan and utilisation of new plants?

Red flags

  • Negative operating cash flow for several quarters.
  • Rising inventory and receivables faster than revenue.
  • Margin dependence on incentives that are ending.
  • Top customer above half of revenue.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. What are the two EMS business models?

High-volume consumer assembly (phones, ACs) and high-mix, low-volume industrial and aerospace electronics.

2. Why do EBITDA margins range from about 3% to 15%?

Consumer assembly passes through expensive components and adds little value; high-mix work adds engineering value.

3. What happened to Dixon's margins, and why?

They compressed in Q1 FY27 because the mobile PLI expired in March 2026 and input costs rose.

4. Why did Kaynes' shares fall despite 41% revenue growth?

Profit fell 24% and operating cash flow was −₹250 cr as inventory and receivables rose.

5. Which company rose most in 2026?

Avalon, +189%, on 50% revenue growth and a ₹2,208 cr order book.

6. How large were India's electronics exports in FY26?

$47.96 bn, up 24%, led by smartphones.

7. What is ODM, and why does it matter?

Designing as well as building products; it earns more margin than pure assembly.
