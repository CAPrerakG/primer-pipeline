Chemical Distribution

The middlemen of chemistry. A handful of big plants make chemicals by the shipload; hundreds of thousands of small factories need them by the drum. Distributors buy, store, repack, finance and deliver, and a few add technical know-how. Globally that's a €10bn+-a-company business; in India's listed market it is mostly micro caps, with more red flags than any other primer so far.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Moving molecules from ships to shop floors

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Imagine a giant bakery that only sells flour by the truckload, and thousands of small cake shops that need one sack at a time. Somebody has to buy the truckload, store it in a warehouse, split it into sacks, deliver them on time, and sometimes let the shop pay next month. That somebody is a distributor. Chemical distributors do this for acids, solvents, plastics and special ingredients. Some just move stuff (a trader). Others also know the recipes and help customers use the chemical properly (a specialty distributor). Those earn much more.

Say it to a client in one minute

Distribution is a working-capital business with thin margins. It works when the distributor adds something the maker can't do cheaply: reach to thousands of small buyers, storage and repacking of hazardous goods, credit, and, at the top end, formulation and technical support. The global winners are specialty distributors (IMCD, Azelis) with 20–25% gross margins and acquisition-led growth, alongside the scale leaders (Brenntag, Univar). Azelis returned to organic growth in Q2 2026.

India's listed distributors are a different animal: micro caps, mostly commodity traders, with volatile inventory gains and losses and several governance flags.

  • Veritas's profit fell 83% in FY26 and the stock is down 90%.
  • A-1, Evexia and Patron are down about 90% from their peaks.
  • The genuine businesses are Stallion India (refrigerant gases, moving into R-32 manufacturing), Black Rose (acrylamide maker plus distributor) and Vinyl Chemicals (the Parekh group's VAM importer, selling largely to Pidilite).

Analogy

A commodity trader is like a vegetable wholesaler at a mandi: buy in the morning, sell by evening, and profit depends on whether prices moved your way. A specialty distributor is like a pharmacist who knows which medicine works for which patient: the product is bought in, but the advice is what customers pay for.

Everyday examples

The acid a small electroplating shop in Ahmedabad buys by the carboy probably came through a trader like A-1. The VAM behind the Fevicol in your cupboard was largely imported by Vinyl Chemicals. The refrigerant a technician refills your split AC with may have been blended and cylinder-filled by Stallion. The thickener in a shampoo and the emulsifier in ice cream often reach Indian factories via IMCD, Azelis or Brenntag's Indian arms.

Fragmented demand

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Producers: giant factories make chemicals by the shipload and would rather sell to a few big customers than to thousands of small ones.
  • Importers: someone has to bring the shipload into India, pay the customs duty and store it at the port.
  • Repacking: a tanker of acid is split into drums; a big tank of refrigerant is filled into small cylinders that a repairman can carry.
  • Credit and delivery: the small factory gets its drum tomorrow and pays in 60 days. The distributor is effectively its banker.
  • Technical service: for special ingredients, the distributor's chemist helps the customer get the recipe right.
Fund-manager lens

Distributors earn a spread on someone else's product, so they have to earn it three ways: reach (many small customers), service (technical and regulatory help) and balance sheet (credit and inventory). The first two are durable; the third is where the risk sits, because inventory and receivables can blow up in a price fall. Before you pay for growth, check how much of profit comes from inventory gains.

The working-capital arithmetic

A trader with ₹100 of sales might hold ₹15 of inventory and ₹20 of receivables, and owe suppliers ₹15, leaving ₹20 of working capital. At a 3% net margin it earns ₹3 on ₹20 of capital, a 15% return. A 10% price fall wipes ₹1.5 off inventory in a quarter, half a year's profit. That is why commodity distributors' stocks swing so hard with chemical prices: 2021–22 gains, 2023 losses, 2026 gains again.

Analogy

A chemical trader is like someone who buys mangoes at the start of the season and sells them over the next month. If prices rise, they look like a genius; if they fall, the warehouse full of mangoes becomes a loss. A specialty distributor is more like a restaurant that uses the mangoes. Price swings matter less because the customer pays for the dish.

7 · The business calendar

Distributors follow their customers

  • Refrigerants: demand peaks from February to May before the Indian summer (AC servicing and installations), and slows in the monsoon.
  • Textile chemicals: follow the textile seasons (see 009).
  • Paints and adhesives inputs (VAM): follow paint and adhesive demand, with a pre-festive build in August–October (see 013).
  • Year-end (March): customers destock to improve balance sheets, and traders often report weak March quarters.
  • Price resets: monthly contract prices for methanol, acids and solvents mean inventory gains and losses show up quarter by quarter.

8 · What the stocks do

Seasonality test on TradingView prices

The exceptions, and why

  • April–July's only real miss was 2012 (−10.8), when the small-cap basket lagged a strong Nifty; 2013 was flat. The biggest runs were 2026 (+68.4, as chemical prices spiked after the Hormuz closure) and 2020 (+39.1, the COVID supply squeeze).
  • April misses were tiny (2025 −0.8, 2013 −0.3, 2019 −0.2). The best Aprils were 2026 (+35.6), 2023 (+29.0) and 2024 (+19.1).
  • March beat the Nifty in only 4 of 15 years (2016 −16.4, 2026 −15.7, 2023 −15.3), consistent with year-end destocking and micro-cap selling.
  • October–December misses were large in 2025 (−24.4, Veritas's profit collapse) and 2018 (−16.2).
How to use this

The April–July pattern is striking but rests on four or five speculative micro caps. For a real trading signal, watch chemical prices: distributors outperform when prices rise (inventory gains) and underperform when they fall. The calendar is secondary.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Seven drivers

  1. Chemical price direction: rising prices mean inventory gains (2021–22, 2026); falling prices mean losses (2023–24).
  2. Volumes at small customers: MSME activity in textiles, paints, pharma, food and plastics.
  3. Principal strategy: makers going direct (Chinese exporters, big MNCs) or consolidating distributors.
  4. Working-capital cycle: receivable days, bad debts and bank lines.
  5. Import economics: the rupee, freight, anti-dumping duties (see 002, 008) and Quality Control Orders on imported chemicals.
  6. Consolidation: global specialty distributors buying Indian distributors for reach and principals.
  7. Product regulation: refrigerant phase-downs (Stallion), food and pharma compliance for ingredient distributors.

The anatomy of a distribution cycle

  1. Supply shock (COVID 2020, China's energy curbs 2021, Hormuz 2026): prices jump, stock on hand is worth more, and traders post record profits.
  2. Euphoria: micro-cap traders re-rate as if the profits were recurring.
  3. Normalisation: prices fall and customers destock; inventory losses and receivable stress follow, and the stocks fall 50–90% (2023–25).
  4. Survivors are those with principals, services or manufacturing (Stallion, Black Rose).

Patterns that repeat

  • Inventory profits are not earnings power. Veritas's 2020–24 rally and 2025 collapse is the case study.
  • Micro-cap traders are momentum vehicles: 2014 +172%, 2025 −32%, with several members down 90%.
  • The better businesses move up the chain: Stallion into R-32 manufacturing, Black Rose into acrylamide.
  • Related-party distributors track their anchor customer: Vinyl Chemicals follows Pidilite's VAM purchases and VAM prices.

11 · The global picture

A consolidating global industry

Globally, chemical distribution is a ~$300 bn industry that has been consolidating for 30 years. Scale players (Brenntag, Univar) move large volumes of industrial chemicals through dense networks of depots. Specialty players (IMCD, Azelis) focus on performance ingredients for food, pharma, personal care and coatings, where technical support earns high gross margins. They grow by buying hundreds of small regional distributors, many of them in Asia and India.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • Producer-owned sales → independent distributors (1990s–2000s): chemical majors outsourced small-customer sales to focus on big accounts.
  • Local → global consolidators (2000s–2020s): IMCD, Azelis and Brenntag bought regional players for reach and principals.
  • The next shift: Chinese producers and digital marketplaces selling direct, which squeezes commodity distributors. Specialty distributors with technical labs are more protected.

12 · Where technology is going

Five shifts

  1. Digital B2B platforms: online ordering, price discovery and financing for small buyers; Indian start-ups aim to cross-sell and export.
  2. Application labs: distributors building formulation labs in India to win specialty business.
  3. Traceability and compliance: batch tracking, safety data sheets, REACH and FSSAI dossiers.
  4. Safer handling: automated filling and blending for gases and hazardous liquids; tighter cylinder rules.
  5. Low-GWP refrigerants: R-32 and HFO blends replacing older HFCs (see 007), reshaping refrigerant distribution.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. How much of this year's profit came from inventory gains?
  2. Which principals do you represent, on what terms, and for how long? Is any exclusive?
  3. What are receivable days, and what share of receivables is over 90 days?
  4. What services (repacking, blending, labs) do you provide, and what share of gross profit do they earn?
  5. (Stallion) When does the R-32 plant start, and what is the expected margin vs blending?
  6. (Vinyl) What share of sales goes to Pidilite, and on what pricing basis?
  7. (Traders) Why did the share price fall so steeply, and is there any corporate action or pledge?

Red flags

  • Revenue growth with collapsing margins (Veritas).
  • Steady daily price falls with no disclosure.
  • Large related-party sales without arm's-length evidence.
  • Repeated equity issuance at falling prices.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. What separates a specialty distributor from a commodity trader?

Technical service and formulation support, which earn 20–25% gross margins; traders earn thin spreads and carry price risk.

2. Why do traders' profits swing with chemical prices?

Stock bought before a price rise produces inventory gains; a price fall produces losses and often customer destocking.

3. What happened to Veritas?

FY26 profit fell 83% (the September 2025 quarter −98%) and the stock fell 90% from its April 2024 peak.

4. Why is Stallion different from the other listed distributors?

It is moving into manufacturing (a 10,000 tpa R-32 plant) and specialty gases; Q1 FY27 profit rose 79%.

5. What is Vinyl Chemicals' business and main risk?

It imports VAM, sold largely to Pidilite (a related party); it depends on that relationship and on VAM prices.

6. Why might Supha Pharmachem's −97% be misleading?

A one-day fall from ₹10.3 to ₹0.87 suggests an unadjusted split or restructuring rather than a real 92% loss.

7. How do global specialty distributors grow?

By acquiring many regional distributors (roll-ups), adding principals and reach; organic growth depends on end-market demand.

8. What is the strongest seasonal window, and the caveat?

April–July (13 of 15 years), but the basket is a handful of speculative micro caps.

9. What threatens commodity distributors structurally?

Producers (especially Chinese) and digital platforms selling directly to users.

10. What should you ask first about a trader's profit jump?

How much of it came from inventory gains rather than recurring spread and service income.
