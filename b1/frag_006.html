1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Selling air, cleaned and cooled

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Air is a mix: about 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen and a little argon. If you make air extremely cold, around −190°C, it turns into a liquid, and each gas boils off at a slightly different temperature, so you can separate them like sorting coins by size. Steel plants need huge amounts of oxygen, hospitals need clean oxygen for patients, chip factories need super-pure nitrogen, and welders need argon. The machine that does this sorting is an "air separation unit", and running it is mostly an electricity bill. Gas companies make money by building these machines next to big customers and signing contracts that last 15–20 years. In 2021, when India ran short of hospital oxygen, everyone suddenly learned how important this quiet industry is.

Say it to a client in one minute

Industrial gases are a global oligopoly (Linde, Air Liquide, Air Products, Nippon Sanso, Messer) with utility-like on-site contracts and higher-margin merchant and packaged businesses. Power is the main cost and is passed through in contracts. In India the market is ~$1.4 bn, led by Linde India and INOX Air Products (an Air Products–INOX joint venture now planning a ~$1 bn IPO), with Air Liquide (which bought NovaAir in 2025), Praxair India and Ellenbarrie behind them. Growth comes from steel capacity, hospitals, electronics (semiconductor fabs need specialty gases) and eventually hydrogen. The listed plays are Linde India, a 75% subsidiary of Linde plc in a long-running fight with minority shareholders and SEBI over how business is split with its unlisted sister Praxair India, and Ellenbarrie (listed July 2025, Indian-owned, ~39% EBITDA margins). Helium is fully imported, and the 2026 Qatar disruption is a live supply shock.

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Equipment: a giant, very cold fridge the size of a building, full of tall metal columns.
  • Production: running the fridge day and night; the electricity bill is the biggest cost.
  • On-site: like a water tank built right next to a school with a 20-year promise to fill it; the school pays even if it drinks less.
  • Merchant: like a milk van delivering to many small shops; good money when milk is scarce, poor when there's too much.
  • Packaged: like gas cylinders delivered to homes: small, but you get paid for the bottle too.

Fund-manager lens

Three business models with different economics. On-site: low risk, low but steady return, backed by contracts that pass through power costs. Merchant: this is where profits swing; when a steel mill takes less gas, the surplus floods the merchant market and prices fall. Packaged: local density matters; the densest cylinder network wins. Oxygen is heavy relative to its value, so it can't travel far by road (typically a few hundred kilometres), which makes every market regional.

Fund-manager lens: three ways to own Indian gases

Linde India gives you the best-run global parent and project-engineering upside, but you take governance risk: the Praxair split decides how much Indian growth reaches minority shareholders, and the Supreme Court outcome is binary. Ellenbarrie is a pure, Indian-owned merchant play with high margins (~39%) and visible capacity growth, but merchant prices are the most cyclical part of the business, and new capacity from Air Liquide–NovaAir and INOX will compete in the same regions. INOX Air Products, once listed, will be the cleanest large-scale option (on-site plus merchant, ~50 plants), and its prospectus will reveal merchant pricing and returns that the industry rarely discloses. The small listed names (National Oxygen, Bhagawati, Southern Gas, Gagan) are not a way to own the industry: one is shut, one is an LPG bottler, and the others are tiny.

Related listed names elsewhere

  • INOX India (cryogenic tanks and equipment; Capital Goods). Steel makers with captive ASUs. Refiners and fertiliser makers that make their own hydrogen. Gujarat Alkalies and other chlor-alkali makers (by-product hydrogen, 003).

5 · How money is made

Capex, contracts and electricity

  • On-site: the gas company builds a ₹200–1,000 cr ASU for one customer and charges a fixed facility fee plus a variable fee linked to power costs. Returns ~12–15% on capital for 15–20 years.
  • Merchant: sold per tonne from liquefied surplus; EBITDA margins can reach 35–40% (Ellenbarrie's Q1 FY27: 39%).
  • Packaged: cylinder rentals plus gas charges; small volumes at high prices.
  • Project engineering: building plants for others (Linde India); revenue is lumpy and margins lower.
Analogy

On-site is like a landlord with a 20-year lease to one tenant. Merchant is like a hotel: great when full, painful when a new hotel opens across the street. Packaged is like a milk delivery round: profit depends on how many houses you pass per kilometre.

Why margins at global majors (Linde plc near 30% operating margin) are so high: once a plant is built, competitors rarely build next door, contracts pass on power costs, and price increases stick because gas is a tiny share of the customer's cost. Indian merchant markets are more competitive, so margins vary by region and by how much new capacity arrives.

6 · The business calendar

Mostly steady, with power and steel as swing factors

  • Steel maintenance shutdowns free up oxygen, which spills into the merchant market and softens prices.
  • Summer power tariffs and shortages (April–June) raise production costs for merchant producers without pass-through.
  • Winter respiratory season raises medical oxygen demand modestly; pandemics raise it massively (April–May 2021).
  • Year-end capex finalisation (January–March) brings project-engineering orders.
  • Governance calendar for Linde India: related-party approvals at AGMs and EGMs, SEBI and court dates.

8 · What moves demand and margins

Six drivers

  1. Steel capacity additions: the largest source of tonnage contracts. India's plan to reach ~300 mt capacity by 2030 means new ASUs.
  2. Merchant supply-demand: new ASUs (Air Liquide Mathura, NovaAir's plants, Ellenbarrie Uluberia 2 and Kurnool) and steel outages decide merchant prices.
  3. Power cost: each tonne of liquid oxygen needs roughly 0.6–0.9 MWh of electricity; captive or renewable power lowers costs.
  4. Healthcare: post-COVID hospital pipelines and PSA plants changed the mix (see technology).
  5. Electronics and hydrogen: India's semiconductor fabs (Tata Electronics' ~$10.9 bn Dholera fab targeting 2026 production; Micron's $2.75 bn Sanand plant) need ultra-high-purity gases; the green hydrogen mission could open a new business, but it is slow (~8,000 t a year commissioned by February 2026 vs a 5 mt target).
  6. Helium availability: a global shock in 2026 that raises distributor margins but hurts MRI and fab users.

The anatomy of an industrial-gas cycle in India

  1. Steel capex boom → gas majors sign tonnage contracts and build ASUs (2008–13).
  2. Steel slump or customer under-use → tonnage plants earn poor returns; surplus floods merchant markets (2014–19 for Linde India).
  3. A demand shock (COVID oxygen in 2021, helium in 2026) → merchant prices and investor attention jump.
  4. New merchant capacity (Air Liquide, NovaAir, Ellenbarrie, INOX) → prices normalise regionally.

Patterns that repeat

  • Crises re-rate everything, including shells. In 2021 Bhagawati Oxygen rose 195% even as its only plant was about to close.
  • Governance drives Linde India's multiple. Its best years (2021–23) came before the SEBI orders; since 2024 the Praxair dispute has capped it.
  • IPOs follow good years. Ellenbarrie listed in 2025; INOX Air Products is preparing to list in 2026.

11 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  • PSA oxygen plants in hospitals: after 2021, over a thousand pressure-swing adsorption plants were installed in public hospitals, cutting their need for delivered liquid oxygen.
  • Zero-boil-off MRI machines: most Indian MRIs now recycle helium and need refilling only every 4–10 years, which limits the 2026 shortage's bite.
  • Green hydrogen: electrolysers powered by renewables; gas majors want to own the plants and pipelines, but Indian commissioning is slow.
  • Carbon capture: CO₂ capture from steel and cement; gas companies build and run the units (in India, Praxair India holds the carbon-capture rights under the Linde split).
  • Electronic gases: ultra-high-purity nitrogen, argon, helium and specialty molecules for chips and displays.
  • Oxy-fuel and hydrogen steelmaking could change how much oxygen steel needs per tonne.

16 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What share of revenue is on-site vs merchant vs packaged, and how much of the on-site business is take-or-pay?
  2. What are merchant utilisation and price trends by region?
  3. Is power pass-through automatic in contracts? What share of power is renewable or captive?
  4. What is the order book for project engineering (Linde India)?
  5. What's the plan and timeline for resolving the Praxair allocation (Linde India)?
  6. (Ellenbarrie) What anchor customers underpin the new capacity, and what are merchant prices at Uluberia and Kurnool?

Red flags

  • Growth via related-party transfers or sales to the parent.
  • Merchant capacity added without anchor customers.
  • Rising receivables from state hospitals.
  • Profits from one-off arbitration rather than operations (Bhagawati).

What you'll hear, and the evidence

ClaimEvidence
"Linde India is a proxy for Linde plc."Only partly: territory and product allocation to Praxair India means some Indian growth accrues to the unlisted sister. That's exactly what the SEBI case is about.

"Gases are utilities."On-site contracts are; merchant and packaged businesses are cyclical and competitive.

19 · Test yourself

Nine questions; answers hidden

1. What's the largest cost in making liquid oxygen?

Electricity.

2. Why is merchant oxygen a regional market?

It's shipped as a cryogenic liquid by road; freight makes long hauls uneconomic, so supply is local.

3. What is the Linde India minority-shareholder dispute about?

Whether business split with sister company Praxair India needed minority approval, and whether it moved value away from Linde India's minorities.

4. Why did a missile strike in Qatar matter for Indian hospitals and fabs?

Qatar supplies ~40% of traded helium, which India fully imports for MRI machines and chip manufacturing.

5. Why do steel shutdowns hurt merchant oxygen prices?

On-site plants keep running; the unused oxygen is liquefied and sold in the merchant market, adding supply.

6. Why is Bhagawati Oxygen flagged?

Its only gas plant has been shut since October 2021; its recent profit came from arbitration income.

7. Who owns INOX Air Products?

It's a joint venture between Air Products and the INOX Group; it plans a ~$1 bn IPO.

8. Which quarter has been best for this basket, and why?

October–December (9 of 14 years), driven by December; the biggest gains came in the oxygen-crisis years.

9. Why hasn't the helium shortage shut Indian MRI centres?

Most use zero-boil-off magnets that recycle helium and need refilling only every 4–10 years.
