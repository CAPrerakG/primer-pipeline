1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Selling colour through a shop network

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Paint is four things mixed together. There is a colour powder, mostly a very white powder called titanium dioxide plus tints. There is a glue that makes it stick (the resin), a liquid that makes it spreadable (water or a solvent), and a few helpers. When the liquid dries off, the glue and colour stay on the wall. The paint itself isn't hard to make. The hard part is getting the right colour to thousands of small shops across India every day, and making painters trust your brand. A company that does this well can sell a bucket for much more than it costs to make. Adhesives like Fevicol work the same way: simple chemistry, a famous name, and shops everywhere.

Say it to a client in one minute

Indian paints were a textbook oligopoly: Asian Paints (55–60% of decorative), Berger, Kansai Nerolac and Akzo's Dulux, with ~20% EBITDA margins and returns on capital above 25%. The moat was distribution (dealers, tinting machines, painter loyalty), not chemistry.

Two newcomers tested that moat. Grasim launched Birla Opus in February 2024 with a ₹10,000 cr investment and heavy dealer incentives, and it reached ₹1,661 cr of quarterly revenue by Q1 FY27. JSW bought Akzo Nobel India, now JSW Dulux. Asian Paints lost share, its stock fell a third in 2024, and the CCI is probing it on Birla Opus's complaint.

In 2026 the Hormuz crude spike raised costs ~20% and the industry pushed through ~10% price increases. Q1 FY27 was strong: Asian Paints' profit +40%, Berger's +29%, Pidilite's +30%. The stocks are still down because investors doubt the old oligopoly margins will return. Pidilite, the adhesives near-monopoly, has held up best, and Elantas (wire enamels) is really an electrical-equipment play.

Analogy

A paint is like a pizza. Titanium dioxide is the cheese (the most expensive topping and what gives the white "coverage"), the resin is the dough that holds everything together, water is the sauce that spreads it, and fillers are cheaper toppings that bulk it up. Anyone can make pizza; the chain with the most delivery outlets and the most loyal delivery boys wins.

Everyday examples

Asian Paints Royale or Apex on a living-room wall; Berger WeatherCoat outside; Nerolac on a new Maruti's body (Kansai is the auto-coatings leader); Fevicol under a laminate; M-Seal on a leaking pipe; Dr. Fixit on a terrace; DIC ink on a chips packet; Elantas enamel on the copper wire inside a ceiling fan's motor.

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Raw materials: the white powder is made from a black sand (ilmenite) found on beaches in Kerala and Odisha and abroad. The glue comes from oil.
  • Resins: the glue is made in big reactors; the biggest paint companies make their own to save money.
  • Paint making: the powders and glue are stirred and ground in big tanks, then poured into tins. A new factory takes about two years to build, which is not hard for a rich company.
  • Distribution and tinting: a shop keeps white paint and a machine that squirts in exactly measured tints, so it can make 2,000 colours on the spot. Getting that machine into your shop, and keeping it stocked every day, is what makes a paint company powerful.
  • Painters: most people let their painter choose the brand. Companies give painters training, insurance and reward points, so the painter is the real customer.
Fund-manager lens

In decorative paints the scarce asset is shelf space and painter loyalty, not factories. A new entrant can build plants quickly (Birla Opus did) but has to pay dealers and painters to switch. So watch dealer incentives, not capacity: when entrants stop subsidising, market share settles. Industrial coatings are the opposite: technical approvals with car and appliance makers, lower margins, stickier customers.

Working capital is famously light. Dealers pay quickly and suppliers give credit, so the leaders earned very high returns on capital. Price increases usually lag raw-material inflation by one or two quarters, which is why margins dip when crude jumps and recover afterwards. In 2026 the lag was short: roughly 10% of increases were taken within about two months of the spike.

Analogy

A paint leader is like a popular chain of neighbourhood bakeries. Flour is cheap and anyone can bake, but the chain owns the best corner in every street and the customers' habit. A new rival can open next door, but first it has to pay people to try it. The question in 2024–26 is whether the new rival (Birla Opus) keeps paying long enough for the habit to change.

Fund-manager lens

The history table shows the rule. Paint stocks beat the market when Brent fell (2014–15, 2020, 2023) and lagged when it spiked (2013 in rupees, 2018, 2022, and 2026 for the leaders). Crude is the best single macro variable for this basket. But since 2024 competition has joined it: falling crude no longer guarantees margin expansion if entrants keep discounting.

7 · The business calendar

Festivals and dry weather

  • September–November: the peak repainting season before Diwali; the strongest quarter for decorative volumes.
  • January–May: dry weather suits exterior painting; wedding-season repainting.
  • June–August (monsoon): the weakest months for painting; waterproofing demand rises before and during the rains.
  • Price increases are typically announced in April and after crude spikes; companies time them to avoid the festive peak.
  • Industrial coatings follow auto production (strong October–March); wire enamels follow power-equipment orders.

8 · What the stocks do

Seasonality test on TradingView prices

The exceptions, and why

  • April misses: 2020 (−7.8) and 2013 (−3.7). In April 2020 the lockdown shut paint shops while the Nifty rebounded; 2013 was the rupee slide. The best Aprils were 2026 (+15.0, as prices were raised faster than feared), 2015 (+10.9, collapsing crude) and 2022 (+7.2).
  • April–July failures: 2013 (−12.2), 2017 (−1.2), 2018 (−9.1), 2020 (−16.6). 2013 and 2018 combined rupee weakness with rising crude; 2020 was the lockdown. The biggest runs were 2015 (+20.7) and 2026 (+19.0).
  • August beat the Nifty in 11 of 16 years; the big miss was 2021 (−10.8), when crude rose and the small-cap adhesive rally paused.
  • October–December misses: 2022 (−11.2) and 2025 (−10.0). These coincided with competition news (Birla Opus's plans in 2022, discounting in 2025) rather than the festive season itself.
  • January–March lagged in the last three years (2024 −11.6, 2025 −12.7, 2026 −6.1), the entry-shock period.
How to use this

The calendar is weak compared with crude and competition. April–July is modestly favourable, and the last three first quarters were poor. For the large caps, the entry pressure (share, dealer incentives) and crude will decide the year, not the month.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Eight drivers

  1. Crude and derivatives: 40–60% of costs are petroleum-linked. Brent's yearly average explains much of the basket's relative return (see the history table).
  2. TiO₂ prices: the single biggest input; Chinese capacity and Indian anti-dumping duty.
  3. Competition: Birla Opus's dealer incentives and discounts, JSW Dulux's push, Pidilite's Haisha; Asian Paints' response. Ad-spend and rebate trends matter more than list prices.
  4. Repainting cycles: 70%+ of decorative demand is repainting, driven by incomes, festivals and the weather (a long monsoon delays painting).
  5. Housing and real estate: new homes add first-time painting demand with a lag of 2–3 years after launches.
  6. Auto and industrial production: Kansai, Nippon and PPG-Asian; wire-enamel demand from power and EVs (Elantas).
  7. Regulation and competition law: the CCI probe of Asian Paints; GST (28% → 18% in 2018); lead-paint rules.
  8. Rupee: TiO₂ and monomers are dollar-priced; the rupee at ~₹96/$ adds cost.

The anatomy of the paint cycle, old and new

  1. The old cycle (2012–23): crude. Falling crude meant margin expansion and outperformance (2014–15, 2020, 2023). Rising crude meant a margin squeeze for a few quarters until price increases caught up (2013, 2018, 2022).
  2. The new cycle (2024 onwards): competition plus crude. Well-funded entrants (Grasim, JSW) buy share with dealer incentives. The leader defends with rebates and pays in margin. Valuations de-rate from 60x+ earnings, even when crude helps (2025).
  3. The settle-down: entrants reach scale and cut subsidies, and share stabilises. This hasn't happened yet in 2026; Birla Opus is still gaining 30 bp a quarter.

Patterns that repeat

  • Brent below $60 means paint outperformance: 2015, 2016, 2017 and 2020 all beat the Nifty.
  • Brent jumps mean a margin dip, then price rises: 2022 and 2026.
  • Small adhesive makers swing far more than the leaders (Jyoti Resins +457% in 2015, Nikhil +318% in 2017, HP −50% in 2025).
  • Specialty coatings follow their end markets: Elantas followed the power-equipment boom, not houses.
  • Entry shocks de-rate the whole group, including companies with little overlap (Kansai fell in 2024 even though it is mainly industrial).

11 · The global picture

Global giants, local champions

Paint is mostly a local business, because it's heavy and cheap per kilo and tastes differ. Global giants dominate where they arrived first; local champions win where distribution was built early. India is unusual: the local champion (Asian Paints, founded in 1942 in a Mumbai garage) beat the global giants on their own game by building the densest dealer and tinting network anywhere.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • Multinationals → Asian Paints (1960s–70s): Asian Paints became #1 in India by 1967 by selling small packs to small towns and building distribution the multinationals ignored.
  • Oligopoly → entry (2024): conglomerates with cash (Grasim, JSW) saw 20% margins and 25%+ returns and decided distribution could be bought.
  • Global owners exit: AkzoNobel sold its India business to JSW, a sign that capital-rich Indian groups now outbid multinationals for domestic scale.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Low-VOC and water-based coatings replacing solvent enamels and wood finishes (Silkflex, Sirca's water-based lines).
  2. Tinting and digital colour: automated tinting, colour-matching apps and painter platforms; data on painter loyalty is the new moat.
  3. Waterproofing and construction chemicals: the fastest-growing adjacent category (Dr. Fixit, Asian Paints SmartCare).
  4. Powder coatings and coil coatings for appliances and steel (no solvents, less waste).
  5. EV and electrical coatings: high-temperature wire enamels, battery coatings and thermal interface materials (Elantas).
  6. Lower TiO₂ loading: opaque polymers and extender technology to cut the most expensive ingredient.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What were volume vs value growth, and the mix of economy vs premium products?
  2. How have dealer incentives, rebates and painter schemes changed? What share of revenue do they take?
  3. How many retail touchpoints and tinting machines were added this quarter?
  4. How much of the raw-material rise is passed on, and what further increases are planned?
  5. What is the progress on backward integration (VAM, white cement, resins)?
  6. (Asian Paints) What is the status of the CCI investigation, and have dealer terms changed?
  7. (Elantas) How big is the order backlog from transformer, motor and EV makers?

Red flags

  • Value growth well above volume growth for many quarters (price-led, not demand-led).
  • Rising receivables (dealer stress or channel stuffing).
  • Ad spend cut to protect margins while share is falling.
  • Small-cap rallies without volumes (Silkflex).

18 · Myths and live debates

What you'll hear, and the evidence

ClaimEvidence
"Birla Opus will fail like other entrants."Earlier entrants lacked capital. Birla Opus reached ₹1,661 cr of quarterly revenue in about two and a half years and is still gaining share.
"Asian Paints' moat is gone."It still grew volumes 9% and profit 40% in Q1 FY27, with the densest network. The moat is narrower, not gone; margins are what's at risk.
"Falling crude always helps paint stocks."It did until 2023. In 2025 crude fell and the median stock still dropped 14%, because competition absorbed the gain.
"Pidilite will be hit by the paint war."Its core adhesives business barely overlaps; volumes grew 11% and margins expanded.
"Elantas is a paint stock."It is an electrical-insulation business; its drivers are transformers, motors and EVs.

Touchpoint

Rewards and training that tie painters to a brand.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. What is the real moat in Indian decorative paints?

Distribution and influence: dealer reach, tinting machines, daily replenishment and painter loyalty, not the chemistry.

2. What is the single best macro variable for this basket, and why?

Crude oil: 40–60% of costs are petroleum-linked, so the basket beat the Nifty when Brent fell (2014–15, 2020) and lagged when it spiked (2022).

3. What changed in 2024?

Grasim launched Birla Opus with a ₹10,000 cr investment and heavy dealer incentives, and JSW agreed to buy Akzo Nobel India; Asian Paints fell 33%.

4. Why did paint stocks fall in 2026 despite strong Q1 profits?

Investors doubt the old oligopoly margins will return, as competition keeps intensifying and costs rose ~20% with the crude spike.

5. What is the CCI investigating?

Whether Asian Paints abused its dominance by using dealer practices to block Birla Opus's entry (ordered July 2025).

6. Why is Elantas Beck a different kind of stock?

84% of its sales are electrical insulation (wire enamels, varnishes), so it follows transformers, motors and EVs, not housing.

7. What is the largest single raw material in white paint?

Titanium dioxide.

8. Why did Jyoti Resins' profit fall despite record revenue?

West Asian disruption spiked raw materials; its gross margin fell to 59% from 72.5% before price rises in May–June took effect.

9. What happened to GST on paints in 2017–18?

It started at 28% in July 2017 and was cut to 18% in July 2018.

10. Which month is the most reliable for this basket?

April (13 of 15 years), though some of it is the general small-cap April effect.
