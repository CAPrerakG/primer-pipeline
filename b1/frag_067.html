Stainless Steel

Stainless steel is ordinary steel with at least a tenth of its weight in chromium, which forms an invisible film that stops rust. That single change turns a commodity into a family of engineered products — the kitchen sink, the railway coach, the chemical reactor, the surgical instrument — and it also changes the economics completely. Chromium and nickel cost far more than iron, so a stainless maker is judged not on the price of its product but on the spread it earns over raw materials. This primer explains the grades, the cost structure, why nickel decides the cycle, how India's producers are fighting imports, and why one company accounts for nine-tenths of the listed section.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

A conversion business priced off nickel

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Ordinary steel rusts when it meets water and air. If you mix in enough chromium, a see-through skin forms on the surface that heals itself when scratched, and the steel stops rusting — that is stainless steel, the metal of spoons, sinks and railway coaches. Chromium and another metal called nickel are expensive, so a stainless factory is really paid for the work of mixing and rolling: it buys costly ingredients, adds its own labour and electricity, and sells the result for a bit more. If the ingredient prices jump while the finished metal is still in the warehouse, the factory can make a windfall — or a loss.

Say it to a client in one minute

One listed company, a good structural story, and a nickel market in surplus.

  • The company: Jindal Stainless — Q1 FY27 revenue ₹11,279 cr (+10.5%), EBITDA ₹1,329 cr, profit ₹769 cr (+7.6%), volumes 580,805 t (−7.3%) after West Asian supply disruption.
  • The metric: EBITDA per tonne, guided at ₹18,000–20,000 for the first half of FY27, with 7–9% volume growth for the year.
  • The move: an Indonesian nickel-pig-iron smelter and 1.2 mt melt shop commissioned ahead of schedule, taking group capacity to 4.2 mt and securing nickel units.
  • The fight: finished stainless imports of 1.73 mt in FY25; Chinese volumes halved but Vietnam's rose 72%, and an anti-dumping investigation into cold-rolled stainless from China, Indonesia and Vietnam began in September 2025.
  • The market: nickel is in surplus — around 261,000 tonnes in 2026 — which caps costs and prices alike.

India is unusual in how much 200-series and 400-series steel it uses: nickel-free or nickel-light grades are cheaper, and the domestic market — utensils, railway coaches, auto exhausts and construction — can often use them. That reduces the country's nickel exposure relative to Europe or the US, but it also means Indian producers compete in a price-sensitive segment where imports bite quickly.

The chain in one breath

  • Nickel and chrome are bought at world prices.
  • Electric furnaces melt them with scrap into stainless.
  • Rolling mills turn slabs into coils.
  • Finishers polish and cut them to customers' needs.
  • Households and industry buy the result; demand grows 7–8% a year.
Fund-manager lens

Stainless is priced as raw materials plus a conversion margin, so the right metric is EBITDA per tonne, not revenue. Jindal Stainless guides to ₹18,000–20,000 a tonne; at roughly 2.3 mt of annual sales that is about ₹4,500 cr of EBITDA before any volume growth. Nickel matters in two ways: as a cost passed through with a lag, and as an inventory effect — when nickel rises, the metal already in stock gains value, and when it falls, the same inventory produces a loss. That is why quarterly margins move even when the business does not.

The Indonesian project is the most important thing Jindal Stainless has done in a decade. Nickel pig iron made next to Indonesian ore is among the cheapest nickel units in the world; owning them converts a purchased input into an internal transfer, and adds 1.2 mt of melting capacity outside India. The trade-off is exposure to Indonesian policy — export quotas, royalties and the government's periodic threats to curb output — and to the nickel price itself, which is now the company's own revenue as well as its cost.

Why volumes fell in a growing market

Q1 FY27 shows how physical this business is. Conflict in West Asia disrupted industrial gas supplies and shipping, and volumes fell 7.3% even as the market grew. Revenue still rose 10.5% because prices and mix held. Management left its full-year guidance unchanged, expecting the quarter's losses to be made up later. For an investor the lesson is to judge stainless on annual volumes and EBITDA per tonne, not on a single quarter's tonnage.

5b · The import fight

China, Vietnam and the circumvention problem

India imported about 1.73 million tonnes of finished stainless steel in FY25, chiefly from China, Indonesia, Vietnam and South Korea — a large share of a domestic market that the industry body ISSDA expects to grow 7–8% a year. Anti-dumping duties already applied to some Chinese stainless products, and Chinese volumes have roughly halved; but Vietnamese shipments rose 72%, which the industry argues is Chinese material rerouted to avoid the duties. In September 2025 the trade authorities began an anti-dumping investigation into cold-rolled stainless flat products from China, Indonesia and Vietnam.

The asymmetry is worth noting: the same producers who want protection from imported stainless also want their imported inputs — scrap, ferro alloys, nickel — to be duty-free. That is a coherent position for a conversion industry, but it means policy can move margins in both directions, and the government has to balance stainless makers against ferro alloy makers (065) who sell them chrome.

5c · Why demand outgrows GDP

The long runway under Indian stainless

India uses far less stainless steel per person than the world average, and the gap closes as incomes rise: a household that buys a steel kitchen, a city that builds a metro, a dairy that installs stainless tanks and a refinery that orders corrosion-resistant piping all add demand that did not exist before. That is why the industry body expects 7–8% annual growth over FY26–FY28 while the wider economy grows more slowly, and why Indian capacity is planned to rise from about 7 million tonnes to 11 million.

Two features of this demand matter for investors. First, much of it is replacement-resistant: stainless is chosen precisely because it lasts, so growth comes from new applications rather than repeat purchases. Second, a large part of the Indian market is price-sensitive and served by small re-rollers using imported coil, which is why import duties translate quickly into domestic pricing power for an integrated producer.

7 · The business calendar

Festive demand, monsoon lulls and Chinese holidays

  • October–December: the strongest stretch — festive and wedding demand for kitchenware and consumer durables, plus post-monsoon construction. December has beaten the Nifty in 11 of 15 years.
  • January–March: destocking and Chinese New Year pricing; the weakest window, with only 5 wins in 15 years.
  • April–June: new-year contracts and pre-monsoon activity.
  • July–September: monsoon slows construction; maintenance shutdowns.
  • Throughout: nickel's moves on the London exchange reset the alloy surcharge every month.

8 · What the stocks do

Seasonality test on TradingView prices

The exceptions, and why

  • October–December has worked in 10 of 14 years, with a median of +8.6 points — the festive and post-monsoon window.
  • January–March has failed in 10 of 15 years (median −8.3), including the sharp falls of 2013 and 2018.
  • The extremes are enormous: +177% in 2017 and +162% in 2021 against −67% in 2018 and −47% in 2013 — a leveraged conversion business in a cyclical metal.
How to use this

With one company, this is a single stock's calendar and should be treated as context, not signal. The more useful observation is the amplitude: Jindal Stainless has had five years of 90%-plus gains and two of 45%-plus losses in fifteen years. Position sizing matters more than timing in a business with this much operating and financial leverage.

9 · What moves margins

Eight drivers

  1. Nickel. Sets the surcharge, the inventory effect and now part of revenue through Indonesia.
  2. Ferrochrome prices. The other alloy cost (065).
  3. Import volumes and duties. Cheap coils cap domestic prices.
  4. Volume growth. Guided at 7–9% for FY27.
  5. Product mix. Cold-rolled and special grades earn more per tonne.
  6. Energy and gas supply. Disruption cut volumes in Q1 FY27.
  7. Capacity additions. 4.2 mt group capacity; ₹900 cr of cold rolling by FY28.
  8. Balance sheet. The company's leverage nearly destroyed it a decade ago; it is now the reason it can expand.

YearWhat happenedJindal StainlessNiftyDiff (pts)

The anatomy of the cycles

  1. Debt and distress (2012–2015): an expansion funded with borrowings met weak spreads; the stock lost 47% in 2013 and the group was restructured and split.
  2. Protection and recovery (2016–2017): anti-dumping duties and a better cycle produced a 177% year.
  3. The nickel bust (2018): a 67% fall, the worst single year in the Metals family so far.
  4. Deleveraging and merger (2019–2023): volumes grew, debt fell, and the two halves of the group were reunited; the stock rose 95%, 162% and 139% in three of those years.
  5. Scale and integration (2024–2026): capacity to 4.2 mt, Indonesian nickel, and a fight against imports — with the share price pausing.

Patterns that repeat

  • Nickel leads, the stock follows. Every large move in the share price maps onto a move in the alloy market.
  • Leverage doubles the cycle. The same debt that nearly sank the company in 2013–15 magnified the gains after 2020.
  • Protection arrives after the damage. Duties came in 2016 and again are being sought now, each time after imports had already taken share.

The nickel market that underpins all of this is in surplus: forecasts point to an excess of about 261,000 tonnes in 2026 and an average price near $15,250 a tonne, after a spike above $18,000 in January and a 14% fall in June. Stainless steel accounts for more than 60% of nickel demand, so the two markets move together — weak stainless demand keeps nickel cheap, and cheap nickel keeps stainless prices low. For a conversion business, that is not necessarily bad: stable, low input prices with growing volumes is a better combination than a spike that later reverses.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Nickel pig iron integration. Owning cheap nickel units instead of buying refined metal.
  2. Lean grades. More 200- and 400-series steel to cut nickel dependence.
  3. Cold-rolling and finishing. Where the margin is; the focus of current capital spending.
  4. Railways and mobility. Stainless coaches, metro cars and auto exhausts are growth markets.
  5. Recycling. Stainless is almost infinitely recyclable; a formal scrap chain would cut costs.
  6. Low-carbon stainless. Electric melting already gives stainless a lower carbon footprint than blast-furnace steel — an advantage as carbon rules tighten.

What is priced in

Jindal Stainless is valued as a steady conversion business rather than a cyclical bet: the shares have paused this year while the company added a fifth of its melting capacity and secured captive nickel. What the market is not paying for is the option on protection — if anti-dumping duties on cold-rolled flats are imposed, the same volumes would earn more. What it may be underestimating is Indonesian policy risk, now that a meaningful part of the group's assets sits there. The small listed names have run hard on the same demand story from far smaller bases.

The honest read

This is the best-structured business in the Metals family so far: growing domestic demand, a dominant share, captive nickel, a clean balance sheet and a measurable margin per tonne. The caution is that it remains a spread business in a metal whose price is set in London and Jakarta, and that its own history — a 67% fall in 2018, a 47% fall in 2013 — shows what happens when the spread and the leverage turn together.
