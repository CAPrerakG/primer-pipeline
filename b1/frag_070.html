Refractories

Molten steel comes out of a furnace at about 1,600°C. Nothing ordinary can hold it — steel melts, concrete explodes, brick crumbles. Refractories are the special ceramics that line the inside of every furnace, ladle, kiln and converter, and they are slowly eaten away every time metal is made. That makes them a consumable, not a capital good: the demand comes from steel produced, not from steel plants built. It is one of the cleanest business models in the Metals family, and this primer explains why its Indian listed names have still gone nowhere for three years.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

A consumable disguised as a building material

Explain it to a 10-year-old

If you wanted to melt a metal spoon, you would need a bowl that does not melt first. Refractories are those bowls — bricks and powders made from rocks that can sit inside a fire hotter than a volcano without falling apart. But even they wear away a little each time, like the sole of a shoe, so the factory has to keep buying new ones. That is the whole business: not selling the furnace, but selling the lining that the furnace eats.

Say it to a client in one minute

A good model with a bad three years.

  • The demand rule: steel making consumes 8–12 kg of refractory per tonne. Every extra million tonnes of steel needs another 8,000–12,000 tonnes of lining, whether or not anyone builds a new plant.
  • The runway: India's crude steel output is projected to go from about 168 mt in FY26 toward 255 mt by FY31, with capacity targeted at 300 mt by 2030.
  • The problem: volumes are growing 7–8% but prices are not. RHI Magnesita's Q1 FY27 revenue rose 6% while its EBITDA rose 42% — the gain came from cost and mix, not price.
  • The raw material: magnesia and graphite come overwhelmingly from China; this is the industry's critical vulnerability.
  • The calendar: April has beaten the Nifty in 11 of 15 years; January in 4 of 15.

The chain in one breath

  • Rocks are mined and cooked into refractory grain.
  • Makers blend, press and fire them into bricks or bag them as powders.
  • Crews install the lining inside a furnace or ladle.
  • Heat eats the lining away over weeks or months.
  • The mill buys another, because it cannot make steel without one.
Fund-manager lens

Two things make this industry structurally attractive and one thing spoils it. Attractive: demand is consumption-linked, so it survives capex droughts; and the product is a tiny share of the customer's cost but total in its consequences, so buyers do not switch casually. Spoiling it: the critical raw materials are calcined in China, so the maker's gross margin is hostage to a supply chain it does not control — which is exactly what happened in 2021, when the basket badly lagged a rising market.

The audit

Orient Ceratech is excluded, and the reason is instructive. Its price series runs back to 2006, but on 11 November 2011 it fell 67% in a single day. That was not a loss — it was the demerger of the company's refractory division into Orient Refractories Ltd, which listed separately in March 2012 and is today RHI Magnesita India. In other words the basket's second-largest member was carved out of the company we excluded, and anyone who uses Orient Ceratech's pre-2012 history as a refractory track record is measuring a company that no longer exists. We start RHI Magnesita's history in March 2012, where it genuinely begins.

Also excluded: Monolithisch (listed June 2025), and the three micro-caps whose prices are 70–90% below highs set within months of listing. IFGL enters the basket from 2018, its first full year.

5 · How money is made

Kilograms per tonne, multiplied by tonnes of steel

The revenue equation for a refractory maker is unusually simple, and worth writing out because it explains everything that follows:

Revenue = (tonnes of steel produced) × (kg of refractory per tonne) × (price per kg)

  • Tonnes of steel is the growth engine, and it is genuinely growing: India's crude steel output is projected to rise from about 168 mt in FY26 toward 255 mt by FY31.
  • Kilograms per tonne is the trap. Specific consumption is 8–12 kg per tonne today, and it has been falling for decades as linings last longer. Better refractories cannibalise their own volume.
  • Price per kg is where the industry has failed for three years. RHI Magnesita's revenue grew 6% on 7–8% volume growth; there is no pricing in that number.

Total-cost selling, and why it works

The way out of the specific-consumption trap is to stop selling kilograms. A modern refractory contract is a total refractory management deal: the supplier takes responsibility for the lining's performance, is paid per tonne of steel produced rather than per tonne of brick delivered, and keeps the gain if the lining lasts longer. That converts the maker's technical advantage from a threat into revenue, and it makes the relationship almost impossible for the mill to unwind — the supplier's engineers are on the shop floor every day.

5b · The China problem

Why the gross margin is decided in Liaoning

A refractory maker's technology is in its formulation and its service. Its raw material is a cooked rock, and that cooking happens mostly in one country. Magnesite is dead-burned or fused in enormous kilns — a process so energy-hungry that capacity has concentrated where energy and ore sit together, principally in China. The United States Geological Survey's import figures make the concentration explicit: of American imports, China supplies about 91% of crude magnesite, 74% of caustic-calcined magnesia and 69% of dead-burned and fused magnesia. Flake graphite, the other essential ingredient in magnesia-carbon bricks, is similarly concentrated, and the IEA's critical minerals work notes that in several such markets virtually all supply growth has come from the single dominant supplier.

India is on the wrong side of this. It has bauxite and quartzite in abundance but very little magnesite of refractory grade, so the key input is imported — part of a bilateral trade deficit with China that reached a record $112 billion in FY26. Three consequences follow, and every one of them is visible in the price charts on this page.

  1. Margin volatility you cannot hedge. When Chinese environmental or power policy curtails magnesia output — as it did in 2021 — the refractory maker's input cost jumps and its contracts, priced per tonne of steel, do not reprice for months. 2021 is the year the basket rose 17% while the Nifty rose 24%.
  2. Working capital. Long sea supply chains mean months of inventory, which is why balance-sheet strength matters so much here. RHI Magnesita India carries ₹452 cr of net cash for exactly this reason.
  3. A strategic argument for backward integration. Monolithisch buying a ₹157 cr quartzite block is the small version of this logic; the global majors own magnesite mines in Austria, Turkey and Brazil for the same reason.

This is the single most important thing to understand about refractories as an investment: the demand side is beautiful and the supply side is fragile, and which one dominates in a given year decides the returns. For the raw materials themselves, see the adjacent industries; graphite in particular is covered in 071.

7 · The business calendar

Relining follows the mill's year, not the weather

  • April–June: a new steel-mill budget year; planned relines and shutdowns are scheduled. The strongest stretch for the stocks.
  • July–September: monsoon slows construction but not steel making; August has beaten the Nifty in 12 of 16 years.
  • October–December: post-monsoon steel output peaks; consumption follows, though the stocks are only 8 of 14 here.
  • January–March: mills defer discretionary maintenance to protect the year's numbers, and refractory orders slip. The weakest window: 6 wins in 15 years, median −3.4 points.
  • Contract resets: many supply agreements reprice annually, usually from April, which is part of why April is the best month.

The exceptions, and why

  • April–July has worked in 10 of 15 years with a median of +9.1 points, and its failures cluster in demand shocks: 2019 (−17.5) and 2013 (−12.3), both steel slowdowns.
  • April's only meaningful losses were 2013 (−4.8), 2017 (−4.2) and 2025 (−2.1); its best were 2024 (+20.8), 2026 (+19.5) and 2012 (+18.6).
  • February–March 2024 cost 24.5 points in one window — the sharpest single event in the series, as the 2023 re-rating unwound.
How to use this

Note the contrast with the rest of the Metals family. Iron ore (064), ferro alloys (065), steel (066) and wire (069) all have brutally weak February–March windows driven by Chinese New Year and fiscal-year-end destocking. Refractories share the weakness but far more mildly — a median of −1.4 points against −11.4 for wire — precisely because consumption, not buying sentiment, drives the order book. It is a useful independent confirmation that the model really is different.

9 · What moves margins

Eight drivers

  1. Steel production, not steel prices. Tonnes made, not the price they fetch.
  2. Magnesia and graphite costs. The China exposure, with a long lag.
  3. Specific consumption. Falling kg per tonne quietly shrinks the market.
  4. Product mix. Flow control and foundry consumables against plain bricks.
  5. Import competition. Finished Chinese refractories compete directly on price.
  6. Contract structure. Per-tonne-of-steel contracts versus per-tonne-of-brick.
  7. Route mix. Induction furnaces use ramming mass; blast furnace and basic-oxygen routes use bricks.
  8. Capacity utilisation. Kilns are fixed-cost assets; volume decides unit cost.

The anatomy of the cycles

  1. The consolidation decade (2012–2018): global groups bought the Indian independents — RHI took Orient Refractories, Vesuvius and Foseco deepened their arms — and the basket beat the index in six of seven years.
  2. The stall (2019): steel output flattened, IFGL's acquisitions weighed on it, and the basket lost 24 points.
  3. The theme trade (2022–2023): the "India capex" narrative re-rated everything connected to steel; the basket rose 36% and then 84%.
  4. The reckoning (2024–2026): volume growth arrived without pricing power, multiples compressed, and three flat-to-down years followed.

Patterns that repeat

  • The basket beats the index in bad markets and lags in euphoric ones. 2015, 2016, 2022 and 2026 were relative wins; 2021 was a relative loss in a strong year.
  • Input shocks show up a year late, because contracts reprice annually.
  • Consolidation has been the main source of returns, not organic growth.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Longer campaign life. Every generation of lining lasts longer, which raises price per kg but lowers kg sold.
  2. Monolithics replacing bricks. Faster installation, less skilled labour, better for complex shapes.
  3. Robotic gunning and installation. Removing people from hot, dangerous relining work.
  4. Sensors in the lining. Thermocouples and models that predict remaining life, sold with the service contract.
  5. Recycling spent refractory. Reclaiming magnesia and alumina from removed linings — both a cost saving and a partial answer to the China problem.
  6. Linings for green steel. Hydrogen-based and electric routes run different chemistries and temperatures, and need different refractories; see 066.

What is priced in

The market has made a clear judgement: it will pay for acquired growth and for a new story, and it will not pay for organic volume growth without pricing power. Foseco India, which bought a business, is up 34% and its newly acquired crucible subsidiary is at a record high. Monolithisch, which is tripling capacity with a compelling narrative, is up 158% at roughly twelve times its own revenue target. RHI Magnesita, which delivered a 42% rise in EBITDA and a 370-basis-point margin improvement, fell 5% on the day and sits 58% below its 2023 high.

Whether that judgement is right depends entirely on the specific-consumption question. If linings keep improving and kilograms per tonne keep falling, then 7–8% volume growth on a 6% revenue line is the permanent state of this industry, and today's de-rated multiples are correct. If Indian steel really does move toward 255 mt while imports are restricted and mix improves, then a business with net cash, a 14.5% margin and a structurally consuming customer is being given away.

The honest read

The two things to test before owning the de-rated names: first, whether revenue per tonne shipped is rising or merely flat — that single ratio settles the pricing-power debate; and second, whether the raw-material chain is diversifying away from China, because until it does the gross margin will keep taking shocks the company cannot forecast.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What was revenue per tonne shipped this quarter, and how has it moved over two years?
  2. What share of revenue comes from per-tonne-of-steel contracts rather than per-tonne-of-product?
  3. How has specific consumption at your major customers changed?
  4. What proportion of raw material is sourced from China, and what alternatives are qualified?
  5. How many months of raw-material inventory do you carry, and what is the repricing lag?
  6. What is the mix between bricks, monolithics and flow-control products?
  7. How much revenue comes from new steel capacity first-fills versus replacement?
  8. What return do you expect on the acquisitions announced this year?
  9. What is your exposure to the induction-furnace route versus the integrated route?
  10. What are the terms of transactions with the parent and its other Indian subsidiaries?

The vessel where oxygen is blown through hot metal to make steel.

A refractory valve that controls the flow of molten steel.

The tube that carries steel into the casting mould below the surface.

A porous refractory that bubbles gas through molten steel to stir it.

How long a lining lasts before relining, measured in heats.

Erosion

Business terms

Specific consumption
Kilograms of refractory used per tonne of steel — the industry's key ratio.
Total refractory management
A contract where the supplier is paid per tonne of steel and owns lining performance.
Kilotonne (kt)
A thousand tonnes; the unit shipments are reported in.
Revenue per tonne
Revenue divided by tonnes shipped; the pricing-power test.
Goodwill amortisation

Slump sale
Selling a business unit as a going concern for a lump sum, as with Mehsana.
Related-party transaction
A deal with a parent or sister company; common in this sector.
Net cash
Cash exceeding debt — what lets a company hold long inventory safely.
Induction furnace route
Small-scale steel making that uses ramming mass rather than bricks.
Backward integration
Buying your own raw-material source, as with the Bihar quartzite block.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. What is a refractory?

A ceramic material that holds its shape and strength at the temperatures where metal is molten — the lining inside furnaces, ladles and kilns.

2. Why is this a consumable business?

The lining is eaten away by heat, metal and slag, so it must be replaced regardless of whether new plants are built.

3. What is specific consumption, and why does it matter?

Kilograms of refractory per tonne of steel, currently 8–12. It has been falling for decades, so the market grows more slowly than steel output.

4. Where do the raw materials come from?

Mostly China: about 91% of crude magnesite and 69% of dead-burned and fused magnesia in US import data, with flake graphite similarly concentrated.

5. What did RHI Magnesita India report in Q1 FY27?

Revenue ₹1,014 cr (+6%), EBITDA ₹147 cr (+42%) at a 14.5% margin against 10.8%, and profit ₹65 cr (+83%) on 122 kt of shipments.

6. Why should you discount IFGL's 58% profit growth?

EBITDA rose only 2%; most of the profit increase came from the end of a recurring ₹26.7 cr goodwill amortisation charge.

7. Why is Orient Ceratech excluded from the basket?

Its 67% fall on 11 November 2011 was the demerger of its refractory business into what is now RHI Magnesita India, so its pre-2012 history is a different company.

8. What is total refractory management?

A contract where the supplier is paid per tonne of steel produced and takes responsibility for how long the lining lasts.

9. What is the best month for these stocks, and why?

April — 11 wins in 15 years — because mills start a new budget year, schedule relines and reset contract prices.

10. What single ratio settles the sector's central debate?

Revenue per tonne shipped: if it rises, pricing power exists; if it is flat, the de-rating is justified.
