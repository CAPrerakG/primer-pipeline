Aluminium

Aluminium is the second most-used metal on earth and the most electricity-intensive to make. It takes roughly 14,000 units of electricity to produce one tonne, which is why the industry's oldest joke — that aluminium is solid electricity — is also its complete business model. Everything that matters in this sector follows from that one fact: where the cheap power is, who owns the bauxite, why smelters cannot be switched off, and why, in 2026, artificial-intelligence data centres have become the industry's unlikeliest competitor. This primer maps the chain from red rock to rolled coil and explains why a record metal price has made the upstream rich and the downstream poor.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

A power business that happens to ship metal

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Aluminium hides inside a red rock called bauxite, glued so tightly to oxygen that heat alone will not free it. The only way to pull it out is to melt the powder and run a colossal electric current through it — so much that a single factory can use as much power as a city. That is why aluminium factories are built next to cheap power stations, and why they can never be switched off: if the liquid metal cools and sets, the whole factory is ruined.

Say it to a client in one minute

A record price, and two completely different experiences of it.

  • Upstream is booming. Hindalco's Q1 FY27 EBITDA rose 73% to ₹14,989 cr with every segment at a record; NALCO's profit rose 88% to ₹2,002 cr, a 38% net margin.
  • Downstream is squeezed. Extruders and fabricators buy metal at the same record price and cannot pass it on; Maan Aluminium is down 35% this year.
  • A new listed giant. Vedanta's demerger put Vedanta Aluminium Metal — India's largest aluminium and alumina producer — on the exchange on 15 June 2026 at about ₹1.6 lakh crore.
  • The new critical constraint: power. Smelters historically contracted electricity near $40/MWh; AI data centres now bid $115/MWh, and every $10/MWh adds roughly $125–150 a tonne to cost.
  • The recycling problem: India imports about 85% of its aluminium scrap, and Q1 2026 imports fell 16%.

Their fortunes are opposed: a high metal price is a windfall upstream and a working-capital problem downstream. That is exactly what 2026 has delivered.

The chain in one breath

  • Four tonnes of bauxite make two tonnes of alumina.
  • Two tonnes of alumina plus enormous electricity make one tonne of metal.
  • Carbon anodes are consumed in the process — see 071.
  • Rollers and extruders turn the metal into cans, cables, cars and windows.
  • Scrap comes back and is remelted at a twentieth of the energy.
Fund-manager lens

Aluminium is the purest integration story in metals. A producer that owns its bauxite, its alumina refinery and its power plant converts a commodity price into a near-fixed cost base, and its margin then moves one-for-one with the metal price. A producer that buys alumina and grid power has almost no margin at all in a bad year. NALCO's 38% net margin this quarter is not skill; it is integration meeting a $3,474 metal price. The corollary is that you should never value these companies on a through-cycle multiple of a peak-price quarter.

The audit

The Vedanta demerger. Vedanta Ltd split into separate listed companies for aluminium, power, oil and gas, and iron and steel, with the scheme effective 1 May 2026 and listing on 15 June 2026. The practical consequence for anyone screening this sector: Vedanta Aluminium Metal is now the second-largest listed Indian aluminium company by market value, and it has 68 trading days of price history. Any back-test that includes it is really testing three months of a T-group listing, so we exclude it and say so. This is the second live demerger in three primers — see 071 for HEG — and both are reminders that a classification file is a snapshot, not a time series.

Three names do not belong here. Euro Panel Products (aluminium composite panels), Innovators Facade Systems (a façade contractor) and Aluwind Infra-Tech (aluminium formwork for construction) are building-products companies whose demand comes from real-estate cycles, not metal cycles. They should sit with construction materials, and we exclude them from the basket.

5 · How money is made

The cost curve is the whole analysis

Aluminium sells at a world price that no Indian producer influences. Profit is therefore entirely a question of where a smelter sits on the global cost curve, and that position is decided by four things in a fixed order of importance.

Cost elementRoughlyWhat decides it
Electricity35–40% of costCaptive coal or hydro versus grid tariffs — the critical variable
AluminaThe next largest blockOwn refinery and bauxite, or buy at a traded price
Carbon anodesA material shareCalcined coke and pitch prices — see 071
Caustic soda and other chemicalsSmallerChemical cycle
Freight and logisticsSmaller but variableDistance from mine to refinery to smelter to port

Why smelters cannot be turned off

A reduction cell holds molten metal and electrolyte at about 960°C. Cut the current for a few hours and the contents freeze solid, wrecking the cell lining and requiring months of rebuilding. Producers therefore keep running through losses far longer than in most industries, which is why aluminium downturns are long and deep — supply simply does not leave. It also explains why power interruptions in China, where much smelting depends on grid or hydro power, have twice in a decade been the trigger for a global price spike.

The downstream mirror image

An extruder buys metal at the world price plus a premium and sells a converted product at metal plus a conversion charge. When metal doubles, the extruder's working capital doubles, its customers resist, and its spread does not move. This is why a record price year is a bad year for a converter: Maan Aluminium is down 35% and Sudal 36% in a year when NALCO's profit rose 88%. Never model these two groups with the same assumption.

5b · The power problem

Data centres are now bidding for the smelter's electricity

For a century, aluminium smelters were the world's buyer of last resort for cheap, constant power. A hydro dam in Canada or a coal plant in Odisha with nothing else to serve would sign a long contract with a smelter, because a smelter runs flat out, twenty-four hours a day, for decades. That bargain is now under direct attack from a competitor nobody in the industry modelled: artificial-intelligence data centres, which want exactly the same product — large, constant, firm power — and can pay far more for it.

The numbers are stark. Smelters have historically contracted power around $40 per MWh. Data centres are reported to be bidding around $115 per MWh for the same firm supply. Every $10 per MWh translates into roughly $125–150 a tonne of aluminium production cost, so the gap between those two numbers is worth something on the order of $900 a tonne — a figure large enough to redraw the global cost curve on its own.

Three consequences follow.

  1. Captive power becomes a strategic asset, not an accounting detail. Hindalco and NALCO own their generation; Chinese smelters largely do not. In an era of rising power prices, that gap widens rather than narrows.
  2. New smelting capacity gets harder to justify anywhere. If firm power can earn $115 per MWh serving computation, the hurdle for a project that pays $40 rises sharply — which is bullish for metal prices over time.
  3. Recycling gets more attractive. Secondary aluminium uses about a twentieth of the energy of primary, so every rise in the power price improves the economics of scrap relative to smelting — which is why the scrap-supply question below matters more each year.
How to use this

This is the most important structural change in the sector and it is not in anyone's trailing numbers. When you next meet an aluminium management, ask for the weighted average cost of power per MWh, the share that is captive, and the renewal dates on any purchased-power contracts. Those three numbers will tell you more about the next five years than the current metal price does.

The scrap crisis, in numbers

India's secondary sector supplies roughly 40% of total aluminium availability of about 2.2 million tonnes a year, and it imports about 85% of the scrap it melts. That import dependence became acute in 2026: the UAE and Saudi Arabia restricted exports of recyclable material, Middle East freight and insurance costs rose, and scrap imports fell 16% year on year in the first quarter, with reports of recycling plants running 20–40% below nameplate capacity. Imported scrap attracts 2.5% basic customs duty on top of 18% IGST and a 10% social welfare surcharge, and the Ministry of Mines has recommended that the basic duty be removed. Whether the finance ministry agrees is a live, checkable policy question with a direct effect on a sector the listed universe barely represents.

7 · The business calendar

The Chinese year sets the rhythm

  • January–March: Chinese New Year halts buying and Indian mills destock before the fiscal year ends; the weakest window, 4 wins in 15 years.
  • April–June: construction and packaging demand pick up; results season delivers the new financial year's guidance.
  • July–September: the monsoon slows construction but not smelting; the basket has won 10 of 14 August–September windows.
  • October–December: Chinese restocking, Western annual contract and premium negotiations, and northern-hemisphere power constraints; 11 wins in 14 years, median +10.5 points.
  • Continuous by nature: smelters run flat out all year, so seasonality is a demand and inventory phenomenon, not a production one.

8 · What the stocks do

Seasonality test on TradingView prices

The exceptions, and why

  • October–December has failed only three times in fourteen years: 2012 (−5.9), 2013 (−0.5) and 2019 (−11.4) — the two deepest years of the commodity winter and the pre-Covid slump.
  • February–March has worked twice meaningfully, in 2017 and 2021 (+6.3 each), both years when a Chinese supply shock overrode the seasonal pattern.
  • April–July looks strong on the mean (+8.1) but weak on the median (−1.5), because two outliers — 2021 (+71.9) and 2020 (+39.0) — carry the average. Always read both.
How to use this

The October–December record here, 11 of 14 with a double-digit median, is the strongest calendar signal found anywhere in this project so far, and it has a mechanism behind it rather than being a statistical accident: it is when Chinese buyers restock and when Western annual supply contracts and regional premiums are negotiated for the following year. The corresponding first-quarter weakness has the same mechanism running in reverse. Treat the pair as one phenomenon, not two.

The anatomy of the cycles

  1. The commodity winter (2012–2015): Chinese overcapacity drove metal to $1,644 and the basket lost to the index in three of four years.
  2. The supply-side reform rally (2016–2017): China capped smelting capacity and closed illegal plants; metal rose to $2,255 and the basket returned 84% and then 116%.
  3. The cost squeeze (2018–2019): tariffs, alumina spikes and rising coal costs while metal fell back to $1,837; the basket lost 36% in 2019.
  4. The energy era (2020–2026): Chinese power cuts, the Ukraine war, and now data-centre competition have taken metal from $2,014 to $3,474, and the upstream names have re-rated.

Patterns that repeat

  • Every major rally began with a supply cut, not a demand surge — 2016 Chinese reform, 2021 Chinese power cuts.
  • Upstream and downstream diverge at the extremes, most visibly in 2026.
  • The metal price column explains the basket better than any Indian variable does.

Two features make this different from steel. First, the product is genuinely global and fungible, so an Indian smelter competes directly with a Canadian one on cost rather than on freight-protected local pricing. Second, because the capacity cap in China is a policy rather than an economic limit, supply responds to political decisions in a way that no model can anticipate — which is precisely why the 2016 and 2021 rallies were unforecastable and enormous.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Inert anodes. Replacing consumable carbon anodes would remove direct carbon dioxide from smelting altogether; still pre-commercial, and a long-term threat to 071's customers.
  2. Low-carbon and green-premium metal. Buyers are starting to pay for hydro- or renewables-powered aluminium, creating a second price for the same product.
  3. Aluminium in vehicles. Lightweighting for efficiency and for electric-vehicle range keeps raising aluminium content per car.
  4. Can and packaging recycling loops. Novelis's model — collect, remelt, roll, repeat — is the industry's most profitable form of recycling.
  5. Smelter flexibility. Research into modulating cell power so a smelter can act as a grid balancing asset rather than a constant load — which would change the data-centre calculus.
  6. Higher-strength alloys. Replacing steel in structures and conductors; see 044 for aluminium conductors.

What is priced in

Notice what the price table says against the profit table. Metal is at a fifteen-year high and the producers are earning record money, yet Hindalco is 14% below its May high and NALCO 21% below its April high. The market is doing what it should with a commodity producer at peak margin: refusing to capitalise the peak. That is the correct instinct, and it is also why these stocks rarely look expensive at tops or cheap at bottoms on trailing numbers.

The question that decides the next three years is whether the power story makes this cycle different. If firm electricity is permanently repriced upward by computing demand, the global cost curve shifts up, marginal smelters close, and a producer with captive generation and captive bauxite earns a structurally wider margin rather than a cyclical one. If it is a temporary bidding war, then $3,474 metal is simply the top of another cycle and the normal rules apply.

The honest read

Own the integrated producers for the power thesis, not for the metal price — and size the position for the possibility that you are wrong about the thesis and right about the cycle, which would mean buying a peak. Avoid the extruders until the metal price stops rising: their spread cannot expand while their working capital does. And treat Vedanta Aluminium as a genuinely new security that needs its own work, not as a continuation of Vedanta Ltd.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. Why is aluminium called solid electricity?

Because separating it from oxygen requires an enormous electric current — electricity is 35–40% of the cost of making it.

2. What are the three stages of the chain?

Mining bauxite, refining it into alumina by the Bayer process, and smelting alumina into metal by the Hall–Héroult process.

3. Why can't a smelter be switched off?

The molten contents of the cells would freeze and destroy the linings, so producers run through losses instead.

4. Why is a record metal price bad for extruders?

They buy metal at that price and earn only a conversion charge, so their working capital rises while their spread does not.

5. What is the new competition for smelter power?

AI data centres, which want the same large firm supply and are reported to bid about $115/MWh against smelters' historic $40.

6. How much is $10/MWh worth?

Roughly $125–150 per tonne of aluminium production cost.

7. What happened to Vedanta's aluminium business?

It was demerged into Vedanta Aluminium Metal Limited, effective 1 May 2026 and listed on 15 June 2026 on a 1:1 basis.

8. Why did NALCO earn a 38% net margin?

Full integration — captive bauxite, its own alumina refinery and captive power — meeting a record metal price.

9. What is India's scrap problem?

The secondary sector imports about 85% of its scrap; Q1 2026 imports fell 16% after Gulf export restrictions, and plants are running well below capacity.

10. What is the strongest calendar pattern here?

October–December: 11 wins in 14 years with a median of +10.5 points, driven by Chinese restocking and annual contract negotiations.
