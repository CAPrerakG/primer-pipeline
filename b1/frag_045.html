Solar Cells & Modules

The factories that turn silicon into solar panels. India has built one of the world's largest module industries in four years, behind a wall of import rules, customs duties and incentives — and has built it in the wrong order: far too many module lines, not enough cell lines, and almost no wafers. In 2026 that imbalance decides who makes money.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

The scarce step earns the profit

Explain it to a 10-year-old

A solar panel is a sandwich. In the middle are thin blue or black squares called cells — they do the real work of turning sunlight into electricity. Around them is glass on the front, a plastic sheet on the back and an aluminium frame. Making cells is hard, like baking a perfect cake; putting the cells into the sandwich is easier, like assembling a burger. In India lots of companies built "burger shops" because they were quick and cheap to set up, but very few built "bakeries". Now the government says solar farms must use Indian-made cells, so the few bakeries are very busy and can charge more, while the many burger shops are fighting over too few buns.

Say it to a client in one minute

Indian solar manufacturing is profitable where it is scarce and unprofitable where it is crowded.

  • Cells are scarce: ALMM List-II has required Indian-made cells in government-linked projects since 1 June 2026, and cell capacity (about 35 GW) is a seventh of module capacity. Cell prices are up 30–35% since December.
  • Cell makers are earning: Premier Energies' EBITDA margin is 30.3%, Emmvee's profit doubled, Websol runs its cell lines at 92%.
  • Module assemblers are squeezed: Vikram Solar's profit fell 85%, Saatvik's 95%; module factories run at 35–40% of capacity.
  • The US door has shut: combined duties of 249% on Indian cells and modules were finalised; the trade commission votes on 14 October. Waaree is shifting to its US factory.
  • The next scarcity is upstream: India has about 2 GW of ingot and wafer capacity; ALMM for wafers applies from June 2028.

Analogy

Think of textiles. Growing cotton and spinning yarn is capital-heavy and technical; stitching shirts is labour-intensive and easy to start. A country that builds many stitching units but little spinning ends up importing yarn and competing on price for shirts. India's solar industry is in exactly that position: modules are the shirts, cells are the yarn, and wafers and polysilicon are the cotton.

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Polysilicon: sand is cleaned until it is almost perfectly pure silicon.
  • Wafers: the silicon is melted, grown into a big crystal and sliced very thin.
  • Cells: each slice is treated and printed with silver lines so it makes electricity in sunlight.
  • Modules: cells are joined and sealed under glass to make a panel.
  • Other parts: wires, inverters and stands turn panels into a working system.
  • Projects: companies build solar farms and put panels on roofs.
Fund-manager lens

The critical point for valuing any company in this section is where it sits relative to the scarcest step. Policy has moved the scarcity from modules (2022–2024, when ALMM List-I kept Chinese modules out) to cells (from June 2026), and will move it to wafers from June 2028. Each shift lets the companies that own the scarce step earn super-normal margins for a couple of years, until capacity catches up — the cell pipeline is expected to reach about 100 GW by December 2027. So a 30% EBITDA margin in a cell business should be valued as a temporary policy rent, not a permanent return; and a module-only company should be valued on the assumption that its margin stays thin until it integrates backward.

Missing and misleading

Several of the largest Indian solar manufacturers are inside other listed companies: Adani's integrated polysilicon-to-module plant at Mundra (Adani Enterprises), Tata Power's cell and module factory, and Reliance's Jamnagar giga-factory. And the basket's history before 2024 is made of two or three micro-caps, one of which (Aerpace) was a steel company. The 2014–2023 statistics are therefore not a record of Indian solar manufacturing; the real listed industry is only two years old.

7 · The business calendar

Policy dates matter more than seasons

  • February budget: changes to customs duty on cells, modules and inputs.
  • ALMM deadlines: List-I (modules) from April 2021 with extensions; List-II (cells) from 1 June 2026; ingots and wafers from 1 June 2028.
  • US trade rulings: the ITC's final injury vote on 14 October 2026; duty orders expected on 2 November.
  • Installation season: January–March is the strongest quarter for commissioning as developers race to meet financial-year targets; the monsoon slows construction from July to September.
  • Auction calendar: SECI, NTPC and state tenders drive the order books of utility-scale suppliers.

Seasonality test on TradingView prices

The exceptions, and why

  • April–July worked in 6 of 9 years (median +6.3); the failures were 2018 (−45.1), 2019 (−29.4) and 2020 (−4.5), the micro-cap bust years. The best runs, 2024 (+99.5) and 2023 (+59.8), were the peak of the cell-margin boom.
  • January–March is a coin toss (4 of 9): hugely positive in 2024 (+84.7) and 2021 (+45.6), sharply negative in 2018 (−33.1), 2025 (−32.7) and 2022 (−26.1).
  • September–November failed in 5 of 9 years, including 2025 (−13.4) as the IPO supply met a module glut.
How to use this

Do not use it for timing. The sample is short, the pre-2024 basket is not really a solar basket, and the big moves were caused by policy announcements — PLI and customs duties in 2021, cell scarcity in 2024, US duties and the IPO wave in 2025 — rather than by any recurring season. Track the policy calendar instead.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Eight drivers

  1. ALMM coverage: each extension up the chain creates a new scarcity rent — cells now, wafers from 2028.
  2. Cell capacity additions: from about 35 GW in August 2026 toward 100 GW by December 2027; the rent shrinks as it arrives.
  3. Domestic installations: about 45 GW in FY26, driven by auctions, green-energy corridors and rooftop subsidies.
  4. PM Surya Ghar: the rooftop subsidy programme for one crore households, which requires DCR modules.
  5. US trade policy: 249% duties close India's most profitable export market.
  6. Chinese prices: polysilicon, wafer and cell prices set the import alternative for non-ALMM demand.
  7. Silver and aluminium: significant cost items; silver's January spike hit cell makers' costs.
  8. Technology shifts: PERC lines are becoming obsolete as TOPCon and heterojunction take over.

The real history, in policy phases

  1. Import dependence (2010–2017): India's solar build-out ran on Chinese modules; domestic makers were marginal and loss-making.
  2. First protection (2018–2021): safeguard duty from July 2018, ALMM List-I announced in 2019, and the PLI scheme and customs duties announced in 2021.
  3. The module boom (2022–2024): customs duties of 40% on modules and 25% on cells from April 2022, ALMM enforcement and US export demand gave module makers exceptional margins; module capacity went from under 20 GW to well over 100 GW.
  4. The glut and the IPO wave (2025): Premier, Waaree, Vikram, Saatvik, Emmvee and Fujiyama all listed within about 14 months; module prices fell and the US opened a trade case.
  5. The cell era (2026–2027): ALMM List-II moves the profit to cells; US duties shut the export route; module-only makers struggle.

Patterns that repeat

  • Policy creates a scarcity, capacity chases it, margins normalise — first in modules, now in cells, next in wafers.
  • IPOs cluster at the top of a margin cycle: six listings in about 14 months, most now below their first-year peaks.
  • Export windows close: US duties followed within about two years of India's export surge, as they did for China and South-East Asia before.

11 · The global picture

China makes most of the world's solar supply chain

China produces the overwhelming majority of the world's polysilicon, wafers, cells and modules, and its overcapacity pushed global module prices to around 11 US cents per watt. Every other country's solar manufacturing therefore exists behind trade protection. The United States has imposed successive rounds of duties — first on China, then on Cambodia, Malaysia, Thailand and Vietnam, and now on India, Indonesia and Laos — each time as Chinese-linked supply moved to a new country. India has used ALMM lists, customs duties and PLI incentives to build its own chain. The result is three price zones: roughly 11 cents per watt in China, 14–25 cents in India depending on domestic content, and around 30 cents in the US.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • From Germany and the US to China (2008–2012): scale, cheap capital and power moved the industry to China.
  • From China to South-East Asia (2012–2024): to avoid US duties, Chinese firms built plants in Malaysia, Vietnam, Thailand and Cambodia.
  • India's rise as an exporter (2022–2025), and its exit (2026) as US duties arrived.
  • From PERC to TOPCon and heterojunction (2023–2026): efficiency gains made older lines uncompetitive worldwide.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. PERC to TOPCon: the mainstream cell technology now; Websol is converting a 600 MW PERC line.
  2. Heterojunction (HJT): higher efficiency and lower temperature loss; Waaree's first US HJT order.
  3. Back-contact cells: contacts on the rear for higher efficiency; led by Chinese makers.
  4. Less silver: copper plating and thinner fingers to reduce dependence on silver.
  5. Bifacial modules and larger wafers: more power per module and per site.
  6. Perovskite tandem cells: a possible leap in efficiency later in the decade, not yet commercial at scale.

The honest read

The industry is in a policy-created sweet spot for cell owners, and the market already treats it as temporary: the most profitable companies trade on 15–22 times annualised profit, while the struggling module maker trades on more than 70 times. The two risks are visible. Cell capacity is set to roughly triple by the end of 2027, which should compress today's DCR premium of ₹8–10 per watt; and MNRE's new monthly price reporting suggests the government is watching that premium. The long-term winners will be those who integrate into wafers before the 2028 deadline and keep costs close to Chinese levels — a high bar. Module-only makers without a credible cell plan are the weakest part of the chain.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What share of module output uses your own cells, and what share uses bought-in cells?
  2. What is the DCR premium you are realising today, and what do you expect when cell capacity doubles?
  3. What is your plan for ingots and wafers before the June 2028 ALMM deadline?
  4. What share of the order book is export, and how does the 249% US duty affect it?
  5. What is cell-line yield and utilisation, and what technology (PERC, TOPCon, HJT) are the lines?
  6. How much silver does each watt use, and what is your plan to reduce it?
  7. Are order-book prices fixed, and what happens if cell prices fall?
  8. How much of the PLI incentive have you received, and what are the conditions?

Red flags

  • PERC-heavy cell capacity with no conversion plan.
  • Module-only capacity additions in 2026.
  • Order books priced at today's DCR premium for delivery in 2027–28.
  • Rising inventory with falling sales volumes.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. Why do cell makers earn much more than module makers in 2026?

ALMM List-II requires Indian cells in government-linked projects from 1 June 2026, and cell capacity (~35 GW) is a seventh of module capacity.

2. How big is India's module overcapacity?

About 217–233 GW of capacity running at 35–40% utilisation, with roughly 135 GW more committed.

3. What did the US finalise on Indian solar?

123.04% anti-dumping plus 126.09% countervailing duty — 249.13% combined; the ITC votes on 14 October 2026.

4. What is the next scarcity after cells?

Ingots and wafers — India has about 2 GW, and ALMM applies to them from 1 June 2028.

5. Why does Emmvee trade on a lower multiple than Vikram Solar?

The market expects Emmvee's cell-driven profits to shrink as cell capacity arrives, and it values Vikram on a depressed profit (₹20 cr in the quarter), so a small denominator produces a high multiple.

6. What is TOPCon?

Tunnel-oxide passivated contact cell technology, now the mainstream and more efficient than PERC.

7. Why is silver important to this industry?

Silver paste forms the cell contacts and is the largest non-silicon material cost; silver spiked to $116.8/oz in January 2026.

8. Why is the pre-2024 price history unreliable here?

The basket was two or three micro-caps, one of which (Aerpace) was a steel company until its rename.

9. What is the DCR premium?

The price difference between modules with Indian cells and those without — roughly ₹8–10 per watt.

10. Which listed company sells rooftop kits rather than utility modules?

Fujiyama Power Systems (UTL Solar), whose revenue rose 125%.
