Two & Three Wheelers

India's vehicle of the masses. The country buys about 22 million two-wheelers a year, more than any other except China, and exports millions more to Africa, Latin America and Asia. Three-wheelers (auto-rickshaws and e-rickshaws) carry the last mile. The industry is now split between four profitable incumbents and a wave of electric challengers.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

The vehicle of the masses

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Most Indian families can't afford a car, but they can afford a motorcycle or scooter: it costs about as much as a good fridge and runs 50–60 km on a litre of petrol. A farmer rides one to the market, a delivery person to your door, a student to college. Three-wheelers, the auto-rickshaws, are small taxis. Companies like Hero, Bajaj, TVS and Royal Enfield make millions of these every year, and send many abroad. Now new companies like Ather and Ola make electric scooters that charge from a plug instead of using petrol.

Say it to a client in one minute

Two-wheelers are India's mass-mobility market, with ~22 mn domestic sales in FY26 plus a large export business.

  • Hero MotoCorp leads commuter motorcycles.
  • Honda (unlisted in India) leads scooters.
  • Bajaj Auto is the export champion (exports now exceed domestic sales) and owns KTM.
  • TVS is the all-rounder with the strongest EV incumbent (iQube).
  • Eicher's Royal Enfield dominates 250–750 cc motorcycles.

GST 2.0 (September 2025) cut tax on bikes up to 350 cc to 18%, and FY27 Q1 was a record quarter for everyone: Bajaj's profit +42%, TVS +51%, Royal Enfield volumes +27%. EV challengers are maturing. Ather turned EBITDA-positive and its stock is up 117% this year, while Ola Electric's share collapsed and is only now recovering. The group is 2026's best auto segment (+20% vs a −11% Nifty). The risks are a weak monsoon (El Niño) hitting rural demand in FY27, the ABS mandate's cost, and the EV price war.

Analogy

The two-wheeler market is like the shoe market. Commuter bikes are sturdy school shoes (cheap, durable, bought by the millions). Premium bikes are sports shoes (people pay for style and performance). Royal Enfield is the leather boot everyone wants for weekends. EV scooters are the new smart shoes: exciting, but still finding their fit and price.

Everyday examples

The Splendor your milkman rides is Hero's. The Pulsar a college student saves for is Bajaj's. The Jupiter scooter a family uses for school runs is TVS's. The Classic 350 at a weekend ride-out is Royal Enfield. The Swiggy rider's electric scooter may be an Ather or Ola. The auto-rickshaw at the station is most likely a Bajaj RE, increasingly electric.

Monsoon and incomes

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Materials: a bike is mostly steel and aluminium; an electric scooter's most expensive part is its battery, made from cells mostly bought from China.
  • Components: other companies make the tyres, lights, brakes and wiring; the bike maker usually makes the engine itself.
  • Assembly: a big plant can roll out a motorcycle every few seconds.
  • Dealers and finance: most buyers take a loan; in villages, a small sub-dealer sells the bike and a finance company collects monthly instalments.
  • Exports: Indian bikes are cheap and tough, so they sell well in Nigeria, Colombia, the Philippines and Egypt.
Fund-manager lens

Two-wheeler makers have better economics than car makers: lower capital per unit, strong brands in each niche, and big export optionality. The profit pools are premium motorcycles (Royal Enfield and Bajaj's KTM earn the highest margins) and exports. The commuter segment is a volume business sensitive to rural incomes. EVs are the disruption, and incumbents who adapted (TVS, Bajaj) kept their share. Most of the risk has sat with the EV start-ups, not the incumbents.

Why exports matter so much

Indian two-wheelers are among the world's cheapest to make and are tough enough for bad roads, so they sell across Africa, Latin America and Asia. Exports now exceed domestic sales at Bajaj, and TVS's international business is growing 30%+. Exports are priced in dollars, so a weaker rupee helps, while currency crises in customer countries (Nigeria, Egypt, Argentina) hurt. Bajaj's Q1 FY27 exports rose 54% as those markets recovered.

Analogy

A two-wheeler maker is like a popular shoe brand with factories in India. At home it sells school shoes by the million (commuters) and fashion shoes at a premium (Royal Enfield, KTM); abroad it sells the same sturdy shoes to countries with similar roads and budgets. The premium line and the export shops make the most money.

7 · The business calendar

Harvests, weddings and festivals

  • April–May: the rabi harvest and the wedding season lift rural buying.
  • June–August: the monsoon slows retail; dealers stock up in August for festivals.
  • September–November: Navratri, Dussehra and Diwali, the biggest retail stretch; kharif harvest cash arrives.
  • January–March: quieter; the financial year-end push in March.
  • The 1st of every month: dispatch numbers; Vahan registrations daily.
  • Exports follow customer countries' currencies and seasons, not India's.

8 · What the stocks do

Seasonality test on TradingView prices

The exceptions, and why

  • August–September misses: 2018 (−3.4), 2021 (−10.3), 2024 (−0.4). 2018 had rising insurance and fuel costs; 2021 had commodity inflation and a weak rural economy. The biggest runs were 2025 (+28.1, the GST cut) and 2014 (+22.9).
  • April–July failures: 2016 (−7.7), 2018 (−19.1), 2019 (−15.1), 2021 (−3.8). These were the slowdown years; the best were 2026 (+26.8) and 2022 (+23.0).
  • October–December beat the Nifty in 7 of 14 years. The big miss was 2020 (−15.2, after the post-lockdown rally); the best were 2013 (+28.8) and 2014 (+23.3).
  • January–March beat the Nifty in 6 of 15 years; the misses clustered in slowdown years (2013, 2015, 2019, 2025).
How to use this

The two-wheeler calendar is "buy in July, hold through September". The run-up to the festive season is the most consistent window, and it works for large caps. It fails in years of cost shocks or weak rural incomes, so check the monsoon and commodity prices before relying on it. A weak 2026 monsoon (El Niño) is the risk to FY27 rural demand.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Nine drivers

  1. Rural incomes and the monsoon: about half of commuter motorcycles are bought in rural India; El Niño weakens FY27's outlook.
  2. Taxes: GST 2.0 cut bikes up to 350 cc to 18% (from 28%) and raised those above 350 cc to 40%, which helps Royal Enfield's 350s and Hero's commuters and hurts KTM and larger bikes.
  3. Regulation costs: BS-VI (2020), ABS/CBS (2019) and ABS for all (January 2026), plus two helmets, each raising prices.
  4. Credit: NBFC lending to rural and gig-economy buyers.
  5. Fuel prices: petrol up ~8% in May 2026; an LPG shortage pushed three-wheeler drivers to EVs.
  6. Exports: currency stability in Nigeria, Egypt and Latin America; Bajaj and TVS.
  7. EV subsidies and costs: FAME-II (to 2024), then EMPS and PM E-DRIVE; battery cell prices.
  8. Premiumisation: buyers trading up to 125 cc+ and 350 cc bikes.
  9. Commodity costs: steel, aluminium and rubber, passed on with a lag.

The anatomy of a two-wheeler cycle

  1. Rural upturn: good monsoons and farm incomes lift commuter volumes (2013–14, 2022–23).
  2. Cost steps: new regulations (BS-VI, ABS, insurance) raise prices 5–15% and dent entry-level demand (2018–21).
  3. Tax or income shock: GST 2.0 (2025) or a drought (2014–15) moves volumes sharply.
  4. Premium and export legs run on their own cycles (Royal Enfield 2012–17; exports 2023–26).

Patterns that repeat

  • Rural distress years are bad years: 2015, 2016, 2019 and 2021.
  • Regulation-driven price rises hurt the entry segment most (Hero's worst years were 2019 and 2021).
  • Premium niches re-rate their owners: Royal Enfield made Eicher a multibagger in 2012–14.
  • EV start-ups swing wildly: Ola −75% from its peak; Ather +117% in 2026.

11 · The global picture

Asia rides; India builds

Two-wheelers are mainly an Asian and African product. China, India, Indonesia and Vietnam are the biggest markets. Japan's makers (Honda, Yamaha, Suzuki) led globally for decades and still do. India's makers have become the main challengers in emerging markets, and Indian companies now own or partner with European premium brands (Bajaj–KTM and Triumph, TVS–Norton, Hero–Harley-Davidson).

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • Scooters → motorcycles (1990s–2000s): Bajaj's Chetak era gave way to Hero Honda's fuel-efficient motorcycles.
  • Hero Honda split (2011): Honda went alone in India and became the scooter leader; Hero built its own technology.
  • Royal Enfield's revival (2010s): an old brand became the mid-size leader and a global export.
  • Indian makers buying European brands (2010s–2025): KTM, Triumph partnership, Norton.
  • EVs (2019 onwards): start-ups (Ather, Ola) led early; incumbents (TVS, Bajaj) caught up, and together hold large shares of electric scooters.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Electric scooters and motorcycles: ~6% of sales, rising towards double digits; cheaper LFP batteries and local cells.
  2. Battery swapping: for three-wheelers and delivery fleets (swapping networks with standard packs).
  3. Electric three-wheelers: more than 800,000 sold in FY26, a record, as operating costs beat CNG and LPG autos.
  4. CNG two-wheelers: Bajaj's Freedom 125 (2024), billed as the world's first CNG motorcycle.
  5. Safety electronics: ABS on all bikes from January 2026, plus connected features.
  6. Flex-fuel: E20 is standard; flex-fuel (E85+) motorcycles are showcased (see 005).

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What are rural vs urban growth rates, and what is the monsoon's effect on bookings?
  2. How much have prices risen with ABS, and what is the impact on entry-level volumes?
  3. What are export growth and the outlook by region; any currency stress?
  4. What are the EV share, EV margin and battery sourcing plan?
  5. (Bajaj) How is KTM's restructuring going, and what is the effect of the 40% GST above 350 cc?
  6. (Eicher) What is Royal Enfield's capacity plan and 650 cc demand after the GST change?
  7. (Ather, Ola) What is the path to positive free cash flow, and what does service quality look like?

Red flags

  • Dispatches outrunning Vahan registrations.
  • EV makers' market share bought with discounts.
  • Rising customer complaints and service backlogs (Ola in 2024–25).
  • Heavy dependence on one export market.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. What did GST 2.0 do to two-wheelers?

Cut tax on bikes up to 350 cc from 28% to 18% and raised it to 40% above 350 cc (EVs stay at 5%).

2. Why is Bajaj Auto different from Hero?

Bajaj earns heavily from exports (now above domestic sales), premium bikes (KTM, Triumph) and three-wheelers; Hero is the domestic commuter leader.

3. What is the most reliable seasonal window?

August–September, the pre-festive run-up (11 of 14 years, median +7.7 points).

4. What made Ather's stock double in 2026?

Deliveries +80% and its first positive EBITDA in Q1 FY27; it hit ₹1,500 on 4 August.

5. Why did Ola Electric fall 75% from its peak?

Market-share losses, service complaints and continuing losses after its 2024 IPO; its share has started recovering (8.4%).

6. What new safety rule applies from January 2026?

ABS on all new two-wheelers (previously only above 125 cc), and dealers must supply two BIS helmets.

7. Why were 2019 and 2021 bad years?

Regulation-driven price rises (ABS/CBS, BS-VI), rising costs and weak rural incomes hit entry-level demand.

8. Which segment earns the highest margins?

Premium motorcycles (Royal Enfield, ~24–27% EBITDA).

9. How fast are three-wheelers going electric?

More than 800,000 electric three-wheelers sold in FY26, with Mahindra, Bajaj and TVS leading; the LPG shortage in 2026 accelerated the shift.

10. What is the main risk to FY27 demand?

A below-normal monsoon (El Niño) hitting rural incomes, on top of a high base after GST 2.0.
