Auto Dealerships

The last hundred metres of the automobile industry. A dealer buys a car from the manufacturer with borrowed money, parks it, insures it, discounts it, and sells it for a margin of a few percent — then makes its actual living servicing that car for the next seven years. It is a working-capital business wearing a retail costume, and the only part of the auto chain where the customer is a person rather than a purchase order.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Thin margins on metal, real money on service

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Imagine you run a shop selling bicycles. You have to buy fifty bicycles from the factory before you sell even one, and you borrow money from a bank to pay for them. Every day they sit in your shop, you pay interest on that loan. When someone finally buys one, you make about fifty rupees on a thousand-rupee bicycle — almost nothing. So how do you live? You fix bicycles. Every puncture, every chain, every service brings the customer back to you, and there you make real money. A car dealership is the same shop with bigger numbers: tiny profit on the car, most of the profit on servicing it afterwards.

Say it to a client in one minute

Dealerships are the highest-turnover, lowest-margin link in the auto chain, and the only one where the balance sheet matters more than the income statement.

  • The economics: vehicle sales carry roughly 3–6% EBITDA margin; after-sales service and spares carry several times that on a fraction of the revenue. Landmark's Q1 FY27 shows it exactly — ₹1,465 cr of vehicle revenue against ₹268 cr of after-sales, but the second line is what pays the rent.
  • The risk is inventory, not demand. Dealers fund stock with borrowed money, so a slowdown that lasts one quarter becomes a solvency event in two. 2018 and 2019 are the proof.
  • 2026 is a genuinely good year operationally: record retails, GST 2.0 cutting small-car rates from an effective 29–31% to 18%, Landmark's profit nearly doubling and Popular Vehicles turning profitable on 44% revenue growth.
  • The warning sign is inventory at 38–40 days against a 21-day benchmark, with 56% of passenger-vehicle dealers reporting higher stock month-on-month.
  • The structural weakness is legal: India has no franchise protection law, so dealer agreements are written by the manufacturer. Harley-Davidson's exit is what put that on the Parliamentary record.

Analogy

A dealership is a printer business. The printer is sold at nearly no margin to get it into the house; the cartridges pay for everything. In this industry the cartridge is the service bay, which is why a dealer will fight for a franchise in a crowded city — every car sold today is seven years of workshop revenue tomorrow.

Everyday examples

The ₹20,000 discount you negotiated on a new hatchback came out of the dealer's margin, not the manufacturer's. The "free first service" was not free — it was a device to get you into the workshop. The finance the salesman recommended earns the dealership a commission the moment you sign. And the car you traded in is now the dealer's inventory, funded by the dealer's borrowing.

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Manufacturer: the factory decides how many cars you get, what you may charge, and how your showroom must look.
  • Inventory funding: you borrow to buy those cars. Every day one sits unsold costs you interest.
  • Showroom: you pay for the glass, the lights, the coffee machine and the staff, to the factory's specification.
  • The sale: you make a small amount on the car — and less if you gave a discount to win the customer.
  • After-sales: the customer comes back every six months for years, and this is where a dealership actually earns.
Fund-manager lens

Three numbers decide whether a listed dealer is investable. Inventory days, because the industry's failures are all funding failures — the sector benchmark is 21 days and the market is at 38–40. After-sales as a share of gross profit, because that is the annuity that survives a bad selling season. And brand mix, because a dealer's economics are inherited from its principal: luxury and electric franchises carry higher value per unit and a different service profile from a mass-market one. Revenue growth on its own tells you almost nothing here.

Why margins look the way they do

Landmark, the premium operator in the section, ran a 5.5% EBITDA margin in Q1 FY27. Popular Vehicles, coming off a loss and dominated by mass-market volume, ran 3.8% — and that was an improvement of nearly a full point. Those two numbers frame the industry: this is a business where a point of margin is a large achievement, and where the difference between the two companies is mostly brand mix and after-sales density, not management effort. The corollary is that scale alone does not fix it. Popular Vehicles grew new-vehicle volumes 91% and still made ₹1.4 cr of profit on ₹1,890 cr of revenue.

Analogy

Running a dealership is like running a hotel where the rooms are cars: the building is expensive, the rooms are borrowed, and every empty night costs money. Occupancy — how fast stock turns — is the only lever that really matters, and the restaurant downstairs, meaning the workshop, is where the profit actually sits.

7 · The business calendar

A year built around three peaks and one cliff

  • April–June: the new financial year opens with fresh targets, new model-year pricing and, usually, restocking after March. Historically the strongest stretch for these stocks.
  • July–September: the build-up to the festive season; manufacturers push stock into dealer yards, which is where inventory days start climbing.
  • October–December: the festive retail peak — Navratri, Dussehra and Diwali — followed by the year-end discount season on unsold stock. Good for volumes, historically poor for the stocks.
  • January–March: the cliff. Year-end destocking, annual target settlement, and buyers who wait for the new model year. March is the worst single month in the record.
  • Regulatory dates: January 2026 brought mandatory ABS and two certified helmets with every two-wheeler (see 018 and 026), both handled at the dealer counter.

The exceptions, and why

  • April–July worked in 9 of 13 years (median +7.7). The failures were 2018 (−19.6), the credit shock; 2019 (−6.4), the slowdown; 2020 (−5.2), the lockdown; and 2014 (−3.1), a small miss in a huge year.
  • The best readings were 2021 (+72.7) and 2015 (+46.2), both on a very thin basket — treat the magnitudes as micro-cap artefacts and the direction as the signal.
  • The festive quarter disappoints. October–December beat the Nifty in 6 of 14 years (median −1.1) and September–November in 5 of 14 (median −4.9), despite being the retail peak. Festive volume is forecast months ahead; the surprise afterwards is usually the inventory.
  • February–March is the clearest negative in this whole primer series: 2 of 14 years, median −4.9, with 2025 at −22.7 and 2021 at −16.8.
How to use this

Own dealer stocks into the new financial year, not into Diwali. The pattern reflects the working-capital cycle rather than sentiment: stock is pushed into the yard before the festive season and sold down afterwards, so the stocks discount the volume long before the volume arrives and then carry the inventory risk through the quarter. With only four years of a genuinely representative basket, weight the mechanism more heavily than the statistics.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Eight drivers

  1. Retail volumes: FADA's monthly registration data, not the manufacturers' dispatch numbers — the gap between the two is dealer inventory.
  2. Inventory days: the cost and the risk; 21 days is the benchmark, 38–40 is where passenger vehicles sit today.
  3. Brand mix: luxury, electric and premium franchises earn more per unit and pull a different service profile.
  4. After-sales penetration: the share of the vehicle parc that returns to the authorised workshop rather than a local garage.
  5. Taxation: GST 2.0 cut small cars from an effective 29–31% to 18%, large cars and SUVs to 40% with the cess abolished, and electric vehicles sit at 5%.
  6. Discounting intensity: a direct transfer from dealer margin to the customer when stock is heavy.
  7. Interest rates: they hit twice — on the dealer's stock funding and on the customer's loan.
  8. Manufacturer behaviour: allocation, target-setting, price increases and the ever-present possibility of a brand exiting India.

The anatomy of a dealership cycle

  1. Demand turns up. Manufacturers increase allocation; dealers take stock happily because it sells.
  2. Manufacturers push harder than retail absorbs. Inventory days climb; the gap between dispatches and registrations widens.
  3. Discounting begins, funded first by the dealer, and margin compresses before volume does.
  4. Funding tightens or demand pauses, and thinly capitalised dealers fail — which is why the 2018–19 period was far worse for this group than the fall in car sales alone would suggest.

Patterns that repeat

  • Credit events hurt more than demand events. The basket fell 27% in 2018 and 23% in 2019, against a slowdown that cut industry volumes by far less.
  • Shortages are good for dealers. 2021's combination of recovering demand and constrained supply meant full showrooms with no discounting — the basket's second-best relative year.
  • New listings arrive at the top. Landmark listed in December 2022 and is 42% below its 2023 peak; Popular Vehicles listed in March 2024 and is 66% below its listing-month peak.
  • Operational recovery and share prices have separated in 2026: record retails and sharply better results, but a median stock down 3%.

11 · The global picture

Where dealers have power, and where they do not

The defining difference between India and the mature markets is legal. In the United States, state franchise laws restrict how a manufacturer may terminate a dealer or sell directly to consumers, which is why direct-sales models have been fought market by market. Many countries — Japan, Australia, Brazil, Indonesia, Malaysia, Italy and others — have some form of franchise regulation. India has none, so the dealer agreement is whatever the manufacturer writes, and the Parliamentary standing committee's recommendation of a Franchise Protection Act, made after Harley-Davidson's exit from India stranded its dealers, remains a recommendation rather than law.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • From single-brand family businesses to multi-brand groups: Landmark and Popular Vehicles are the listed expression of a consolidation that is well advanced elsewhere and just beginning here.
  • From selling to servicing: as vehicle margins compressed globally, after-sales and used cars became the profit centre.
  • From showroom to omnichannel: online configuration and booking moved the top of the funnel away from the dealership, leaving delivery and service behind.

12 · Where the business model is going

Six shifts

  1. The agency model: the manufacturer owns the stock and the price, and the dealer earns a fixed commission for delivery and service. It removes inventory risk and caps upside — and it is spreading in Europe.
  2. Electric vehicles change after-sales: fewer moving parts and no oil changes mean lower routine service revenue per vehicle, offset by high-voltage work, software and a different tyre and brake pattern. Landmark's 30% EV share by value makes it the natural place to watch this.
  3. Organised pre-owned: certified used-car businesses with financing attached, where GST applies only on the dealer's margin.
  4. Digital retail: online booking, home test drives and delivery; the dealership becomes a fulfilment and service node.
  5. Parts e-commerce: Popular Vehicles' Yanik platform is an attempt to monetise the parts network beyond the workshop's own bays.
  6. Consolidation: scale in buying, back-office and financing is the only durable answer to a 3–6% margin.

The honest read

The operating news is as good as this industry gets — record retails, a tax cut that raised affordability, both listed operators improving sharply — and the market has paid almost nothing for it. That gap is the thesis and the warning at once. It is justified if inventory at nearly double the benchmark is the first stage of the familiar sequence: push, discount, margin loss. It is unjustified if after-sales and mix carry the operators through, as Landmark's 5.5% margin and 30% EV share by value suggest they might. The way to tell the two apart is not the quarterly result but the monthly gap between manufacturer dispatches and FADA registrations. Watch that gap, and remember that in this industry the balance sheet fails before the income statement does.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What are inventory days by brand today, and how do they compare with a year ago?
  2. What share of gross profit comes from after-sales and spares, and how has it moved?
  3. How much of the quarter's discounting was funded by the manufacturer and how much by you?
  4. What is the cost of channel finance, and what is the total stock funding outstanding?
  5. What is the service retention rate — what share of vehicles sold still come back to your workshop in year four?
  6. What does an electric vehicle earn in after-sales over five years compared with a petrol one?
  7. What return on capital does a new outlet generate, and in what period?
  8. What protection exists if a principal exits India or terminates the agreement?
  9. (Popular Vehicles) What are the integration costs of the acquired networks, and when do they end?

Red flags

  • Revenue growth from new outlets while same-store volumes fall.
  • Inventory days rising for two consecutive quarters.
  • Profit driven by commission income rather than vehicle or service gross margin.
  • Expansion into new states funded by short-term borrowing.
  • Heavy dependence on a single principal's allocation decisions.

18 · Myths and live debates

What you'll hear, and the evidence

ClaimEvidence
"Dealerships are the cheapest way to own auto demand."They are the most operationally geared way. The basket fell 27% and 23% in 2018 and 2019 — far more than industry volumes did — because dealers carry the funding risk.
"Record retails mean a great year for dealer stocks."2026 has record retails and a median stock down 3%. What the market is pricing is inventory at 38–40 days, not volume.
"Electric vehicles will destroy the service business."They reduce routine servicing but add high-voltage, software and battery work. Landmark, at 30% EV by value, is the live test case rather than a theory.
"Direct sales will disintermediate dealers in India."Delivery, registration, service and bodyshop capability remain physical and local. What is genuinely at risk is the pricing and margin structure through agency models, not the existence of the network.
"The GST cut was a windfall for dealers."It lifted affordability and volumes, but manufacturers have been raising prices through 2026, which is how the benefit gets shared out over time.
"Listed dealers give diversified exposure to the sector."Two of the six are not dealerships in the ordinary sense, one has no usable data, and the industry-representative record is about four years long.

19 · Glossary

Every term you'll meet

The business

Dealership
A business franchised by a manufacturer to sell and service its vehicles in a territory.
Principal / OEM
The vehicle manufacturer whose brand the dealer represents.
Franchise agreement
The contract granting the right to sell and service a brand; in India it is drafted by the manufacturer and terminable on its terms.
Touchpoint
Any customer-facing location — showroom, workshop, stockyard or booking office. Popular Vehicles reports about 500.
Same-store growth
Growth from existing outlets, excluding new ones; the honest measure of a retail business.
Allocation
The number of vehicles a manufacturer chooses to supply a dealer, often the real constraint on growth.
Dispatches versus registrations
Factory-to-dealer shipments versus actual customer sales; the gap is the change in dealer inventory.
Inventory days
How many days of sales are sitting in the yard; FADA's benchmark is 21 and the market is at 38–40.
Channel finance
Short-term borrowing secured against vehicle stock; the industry's lifeline and its principal risk.
Floor plan
The international term for the same stock-funding arrangement.
Stockyard
The holding area for unsold vehicles, and the physical form of working capital.
Bodyshop
The accident-repair operation, largely paid for by insurers.
Service retention
The share of vehicles sold that keep returning to the authorised workshop rather than a local garage.
After-sales
Service, spares and repair revenue; small in turnover, large in profit.
Genuine parts
Manufacturer-branded spares sold through the dealer network, at a higher margin than the vehicle (see 023).
Pre-owned / certified used
Trade-ins refurbished, warranted and resold by the dealer; taxed at 18% on the margin.
Exchange bonus
A discount offered against a trade-in, part-funded by the manufacturer.
Attachment rate
The share of buyers who also take finance, insurance or accessories at the point of sale.
Agency model
An arrangement in which the manufacturer owns the stock and sets the price while the dealer earns a commission.
Direct-to-consumer
Selling without a franchised dealer; restricted by law in parts of the United States, unrestricted in India.
Dealer margin
The fixed percentage of the vehicle price the manufacturer allows the dealer; any discount to the customer comes out of it.
Pre-delivery inspection (PDI)
The checks and preparation done before a vehicle is handed over; a cost the dealer carries on every unit.
Demo vehicle
A test-drive car the dealer buys and later sells at a loss; a compulsory, manufacturer-specified cost of holding a franchise.
Yard ageing
How long individual units have been in stock. An average inventory figure can hide a tail of old vehicles that will only move at a discount.
Workshop throughput
Vehicles serviced per bay per day; the utilisation measure for the profitable half of a dealership.
Extended warranty and maintenance package
Pre-paid service sold at delivery; it locks the customer into the authorised workshop and lifts service retention.
Insurance renewal income
Commission from renewing a customer's motor policy each year, one of the few recurring fee lines a dealer controls.
Territory
The geography a franchise covers. India's agreements rarely grant exclusivity, so a principal can appoint a competing dealer nearby.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. Where does a dealership actually make its profit?

After-sales service and spare parts, plus finance and insurance commissions. The vehicle itself carries a low single-digit margin.

2. What is the single most important number to track?

Inventory days — the gap between manufacturer dispatches and FADA registrations. The benchmark is 21 days; passenger vehicles are at 38–40.

3. Why did dealer stocks fall so much more than car sales in 2018–19?

Dealers fund their stock with borrowed money, so a credit squeeze is a solvency event, not just a demand event.

4. What did the two listed operators report in Q1 FY27?

Landmark: revenue ₹1,733 cr (+22.5%), EBITDA margin 5.5%, profit ₹14.5 cr (+97.5%). Popular Vehicles: revenue ₹1,890 cr (+44.1%), EBITDA margin 3.8%, and a return to profit at ₹1.4 cr.

5. Why is the festive quarter a poor window for these stocks?

Festive volume is known in advance and already priced; what follows is the inventory and discounting hangover. October–December has beaten the Nifty in only 6 of 14 years.

6. What did GST 2.0 change?

From 22 September 2025: small cars to 18% from an effective 29–31%, large cars and SUVs to 40% with the cess abolished, electric vehicles at 5%.

7. Why does the absence of a franchise law matter?

Dealer agreements are written by the manufacturer and terminable on its terms; when Harley-Davidson exited India, its dealers had little protection. A Parliamentary committee recommended a Franchise Protection Act, which has not been enacted.

8. How do electric vehicles change dealer economics?

Lower routine service revenue per vehicle, offset by high-voltage, software and battery work, and a higher value per unit. Landmark, at 30% EV by value, is the test case.

9. What is the agency model, and why should a dealer investor care?

The manufacturer owns the stock and sets the price while the dealer earns a commission. It removes inventory risk and caps upside — a structural change to the margin, not a cyclical one.

10. Why should the pre-2023 record here be treated carefully?

Only two micro caps were listed in 2013–2015; the two companies that resemble modern dealership businesses listed in December 2022 and March 2024.
