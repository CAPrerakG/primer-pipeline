Security & Surveillance Equipment

CCTV cameras watch shops, homes, offices, highways, railway stations and city streets. For fifteen years India bought most of them from two Chinese companies, Hikvision and Dahua, or from brands that used their parts. A cybersecurity rule notified in April 2024 and fully enforced from April 2026 changed that almost overnight: a camera now needs government certification, and certification is refused to products built on Chinese chips. This primer explains the products, the chain from chip to control room, the rule that reshaped the market, and what is priced into the handful of listed companies.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

A regulatory reset turned a price-war market into a scarcity market

Explain it to a 10-year-old

A CCTV camera is like a guard with eyes that never blink. The camera is the eye, the recorder is the guard's diary, and a tiny chip inside is the guard's brain. For years most of those brains were made in China. The government worried that someone far away might be able to peek through the guard's eyes, so it made a new rule: before any camera can be sold, a government lab must test it, and the maker must say where the brain came from. Cameras with Chinese brains could not pass. The shops suddenly had fewer cameras to choose from, and the Indian brands that had already switched to other chips sold far more than before.

Say it to a client in one minute

This is a policy-made market-share story, not a demand story.

  • The rule: MeitY's "Essential Requirements for Security of CCTV", notified on 9 April 2024. Since 1 April 2026 no uncertified camera can be sold; the government will not certify products with Chinese-origin chips.
  • The winners: Hikvision and Dahua held roughly a third of Indian sales. CP Plus (Aditya Infotech) reached 43.3% in FY26 and more than 40 brands now have certified products.
  • The numbers: CP Plus's FY26 revenue was ₹4,220.8 cr (+35.6%), EBITDA ₹579.0 cr (13.7%). In Q1 FY27 revenue rose 89.5% to ₹1,402 cr, but the margin fell to 14.8% from 18.1% in Q4.
  • The squeeze: memory and chip prices have surged with AI demand; CP Plus expects to raise prices by about 25% this year.
  • The stocks: CP Plus +125% and Prizor Viztech +273% this year. CP Plus trades at roughly 74 times its Q1 profit annualised.

2 · What the products are

Eyes, diaries and brains

A typical small-business installation — four to eight cameras, a recorder, a hard disk and cabling — is sold through a dealer and installed by a local technician. A city project — thousands of cameras, fibre networks, a command-and-control centre and years of maintenance — is won by a system integrator in a government tender. The same camera brand may serve both, but the economics differ: the first is a branded consumer-durable business with channel credit; the second is a project business with lowest-price bidding and slow government payments.

The chain in one breath

  • Chip makers sell the brain.
  • Designers write the firmware and prove to a government lab that it is safe.
  • Factories put the camera together.
  • Brands and distributors get it into thousands of shops and installers' vans.
  • Integrators wire thousands of cameras into a city command centre.
Fund-manager lens

The critical point in this chain moved in 2026. Before the rule, the scarce asset was distribution: Chinese factories supplied cheap, good cameras to anyone, and brands competed on price and channel reach. After the rule, the scarce asset is certified designs built on non-Chinese chips — and access to those chips at a time when AI data centres are absorbing the world's memory supply. That is why CP Plus's market share and revenue jumped at the same time as its margin came under pressure from component costs.

The rest of the industry

Most of India's surveillance market is unlisted or sits in other sections. Hikvision and Dahua (listed in Shenzhen) were the largest suppliers until 2026. Premium brands — Bosch, Honeywell, Axis and Hanwha — serve enterprises and critical sites. Indian brands include Matrix Comsec, Godrej's security business and Qubo (Hero Electronix), none of them listed as pure plays. Dixon (052) was CP Plus's manufacturing partner until 2024. Bharat Electronics (058) builds defence and coastal surveillance systems. System integrators for city projects include L&T and IT services firms. The audit conclusion is simple: this section has two real members, and the watchlist should keep CP Plus and Prizor and move the other three.

CP Plus's history shows how the mix matters. In FY25, 24.65% of Aditya Infotech's revenue came from Dahua products it distributed; the rest was mostly its own CP Plus brand, whose manufacturing subsidiary imported about 85% of its camera requirements in unassembled form from China, according to CARE Ratings. By Q1 FY27 the CP Plus brand was 87% of revenue, and gross margin was 30.8%. Moving from distributing someone else's cameras to selling its own is a major reason EBITDA margin rose from 8.3% in FY25 to 13.7% in FY26.

Working capital is the constraint

A camera brand carries hundreds of models, each needing chips bought months ahead, and gives dealers credit to keep shelves full. CP Plus reported a cash conversion cycle of 64 days in Q1 FY27, improved by lower inventory and debtor days. Management says it has chips and memory covered for three to four quarters ahead — a sensible hedge in a shortage, but one that ties up cash. In a growth year, rising receivables and inventory can absorb much of the profit, so operating cash flow is the number to check against reported earnings.

What a price increase does

Memory and SoC costs have risen sharply in 2026. CP Plus has been raising prices in steps — 15–20% by the end of the first half, about 25% for the year — rather than all at once. Gradual pass-through protects volumes but leaves a lag in which margins compress, as they did from 18.1% in Q4 FY26 to 14.8% in Q1 FY27. The lag is also why Q4 FY26 was unusually profitable: CP Plus sold inventory bought before the cost surge at post-rule prices.

5b · The rule that changed the market

How a certification requirement became a market-share transfer

DateEvent
9 Apr 2024MeitY notifies the "Essential Requirement(s) for Security of CCTV" by amending the compulsory registration order for electronics. Cameras must pass testing at STQC or accredited labs.
9 Apr 2025Compliance deadline for existing manufacturers; new certifications require the essential requirements.
Apr 2025 – Mar 2026Brands scramble to redesign on non-Chinese chips; older stock is allowed to sell through.
1 Apr 2026Non-compliant cameras can no longer be sold. Hikvision and Dahua have no certified models on the list.
Aug 2026More than 40 brands certified; CP Plus reports 43.3% share for FY26.

The requirements look technical: secure boot so only signed firmware runs, encrypted communication, no hard-coded passwords, vulnerability testing, source-code review and disclosure of the origin of key components, especially the SoC. In practice the disclosure clause did the work. Certification has been refused to products with Chinese-origin chipsets, which rules out the HiSilicon, Ingenic, SigmaStar, Fullhan and Goke chips that powered most low-cost cameras in the world. Camera makers shifted sourcing to Taiwan, South Korea, the US and Singapore; CP Plus says about 35% of its bill of materials — the semiconductor part — now comes from Taiwan.

Why it happened

India's rule follows a decade of similar moves elsewhere. The US barred federal agencies from buying Hikvision and Dahua equipment in the 2018 defence authorisation act, added both firms to its Entity List in October 2019, and in November 2022 the FCC stopped authorising their new equipment. The UK and Australia removed them from sensitive government sites. India's concern sharpened after the 2020 border clash, when scrutiny of Chinese equipment in telecom and power networks increased. Cameras are network devices mounted in police stations, railway stations and military areas; a compromised camera is a door into a network.

Why the effect was so large

Before the rule, three brands — Hikvision, CP Plus and Dahua — held about 75% of the Indian market, according to CARE Ratings, and two of them were linked to Aditya Infotech (its own brand and its Dahua distribution). The rule removed the two Chinese brands and forced every other brand to redesign. The companies that had started redesigning early took the vacated share. That explains the one-year jump in CP Plus's revenue — and why part of its growth is a one-time share transfer that will not repeat.

What could reverse it

The rule is an administrative requirement, not a law passed by Parliament, and India has eased restrictions on Chinese suppliers in other sectors when domestic supply fell short. A relaxation for specific components, or certification of Chinese-owned brands using non-Chinese chips, would bring competition back. Management was asked about this on its Q1 FY27 call; the answer was that the norm prohibits Chinese semiconductors and memory. Investors should treat the rule as durable but not permanent.

The memory shock deserves emphasis. Chip makers have shifted production toward high-bandwidth memory for AI servers, cutting output of the older DDR4 chips that cameras and recorders use. Security-camera prices are rising worldwide as a result. For an Indian brand that can no longer fall back on cheaper Chinese memory, this is a double constraint: fewer suppliers, higher prices. CP Plus's response has been forward buying and gradual price increases; smaller brands without scale may struggle to secure supply at all, which could strengthen the leaders further.

7 · The business calendar

Year-end budgets, festivals and deadlines

  • January–March: government and enterprise buyers spend remaining budgets before the fiscal year ends; project billing is usually heaviest in this quarter.
  • April–June: new budgets are approved but tenders take time; channel demand from shops and small businesses continues.
  • July–September: monsoon slows outdoor installation in many states.
  • October–November: the festive season lifts home and retail purchases, including Wi-Fi cameras.
  • Elections: the model code of conduct pauses new government tenders for weeks in the affected state.
  • Deadlines and price changes: dealers stock up before announced price increases or regulatory cut-offs, pulling demand forward.

CP Plus's recent quarters show how events outweigh seasons. Revenue was ₹740 cr in Q1 FY26, averaged about ₹1,030 cr in each of Q2 and Q3, reached ₹1,422 cr in Q4 as the deadline approached, and held at ₹1,402 cr in Q1 FY27 once the rule applied. A normal year will look smoother; this one will not repeat.

8 · What the stocks do

Two years of prices: not enough for a seasonality test

What the record does say

  • Both stocks have done little but rise since listing. CP Plus is almost five times its issue price; Prizor 12.7 times.
  • The large moves are linked to events, not months: the April 2026 enforcement date, CP Plus's results, and Prizor's revenue doubling.
  • Results days matter more than calendars. CP Plus hit its high on 12 August 2026, the day before its Q1 results; it fell about 5% on the margin decline.
How to use this

Ignore the monthly statistics for this industry. With two listed years, both dominated by a one-off regulatory change, any seasonal pattern is noise. The useful questions are about the business calendar in section 7 and about whether the one-time share gain is sustained, which the next four quarters will show.

9 · What moves margins

Eight drivers

  1. Regulation. The STQC rule decides who may sell. Any relaxation is the largest risk to the leaders.
  2. Market share. Every point of share from departing Chinese brands is revenue at little extra cost to distribution.
  3. Chip and memory costs. With Chinese suppliers excluded, component prices follow the global AI-driven memory cycle.
  4. Price pass-through. The speed at which brands raise prices decides how much of the cost increase hits margins.
  5. Own brand versus distribution. A higher share of own-brand revenue lifts gross margin.
  6. Product mix. IP cameras, AI analytics and specialised products earn more than basic analog cameras.
  7. Backward integration. Local lenses, enclosures, cables and PCBs cut costs and import dependence.
  8. Working capital. Inventory and channel credit decide whether profit becomes cash.

The anatomy of the cycles

  1. The Chinese build-out (2012–2017): Hikvision's revenue grew more than tenfold between 2012 and 2021 on China's domestic surveillance programmes and exports; the stocks compounded.
  2. The sanctions era (2018–2022): US, UK and Australian restrictions cut Western markets; the stocks fell sharply in 2018 and 2022.
  3. Maturity (2023–2025): revenue plateaued around RMB 90 bn; Hikvision's price is still 52% below its January 2021 high and 16% below the end of 2017.
  4. Localisation (2024–2026): India, the largest remaining open market of scale, required trusted chips; local brands inherited the share.

Patterns that repeat

  • Falls cluster around policy. The worst years for both Chinese stocks — 2018 and 2022 — coincided with US sanctions. In India, policy produced the best year instead.
  • Growth without re-rating. Hikvision's revenue more than doubled after 2017 while its share price went backwards. Market leadership in hardware does not guarantee shareholder returns once growth slows.
  • Indian security companies have a mixed record. Zicom Electronic Security Systems, once the best-known listed Indian security brand, was admitted to insolvency proceedings in 2022. Kavveri fell from ₹295 in 2008 to under ₹2 in 2020 before re-rating as a defence name.

Hikvision remains the world's largest video-surveillance company: 2025 revenue of RMB 92.51 bn (about $13 bn), essentially unchanged from 2024, and net profit of RMB 14.20 bn, up 18.5%. Overseas revenue was RMB 27.22 bn, 29.4% of the total, with emerging markets the fastest-growing part. It spends 12.7% of revenue on R&D and paid out about 74% of profit as dividends. Its first quarter of 2026 was stronger (revenue +11.8%), so the loss of India has not visibly dented the group.

India's video-surveillance market is estimated at about $4.4 bn in 2025, growing around 10% a year to roughly $7 bn by 2030 (Mordor Intelligence); estimates vary widely between research firms. Government buyers account for roughly a third of spending. The Indian market is therefore about a third of Hikvision's revenue in size — large enough to matter to Indian companies, too small to matter much to the Chinese leader.

Where India could lead

The combination of a large domestic market, certified designs and non-Chinese supply chains gives Indian brands a product that Western "trusted-supplier" markets want. CP Plus has said exports are 18–24 months from significant numbers. If Indian brands can compete on price with Korean and European makers outside China's sphere, exports could become the second growth leg once domestic share gains are complete.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Analog to IP. Network cameras already account for 79% of CP Plus's portfolio. IP systems are more valuable but are the ones the security rule targets.
  2. Edge AI. Cameras now run analytics inside the device — face recognition, number plates, intrusion and crowd detection — so the SoC matters more.
  3. Cloud video (VSaaS). Footage stored and analysed in the cloud turns a one-time sale into a subscription.
  4. Connectivity. 4G and solar-powered cameras reach highways, farms and construction sites without cabling.
  5. Security by design. Secure boot, signed firmware and encryption are now requirements, not options.
  6. Indian silicon. Start-ups under the design-linked incentive scheme, such as Mindgrove Technologies, are developing vision chips; CP Plus is working with Indian design companies. A domestic SoC would remove the last major import dependence.

Each shift raises the importance of engineering over distribution. A brand that only rebadged imported cameras could compete before 2024. From 2026 it needs its own certified firmware, secure supply of approved chips and, increasingly, software. That favours scale — which is why the rule strengthened the market leader rather than fragmenting the market among dozens of small brands.

What is priced in

At about ₹41,956 cr, CP Plus is valued at roughly 74 times its Q1 FY27 profit annualised, and at around 70–80 times the profit implied by its FY27 guidance (revenue ₹6,000–6,500 cr; EBITDA margin 14–15%). That price assumes the market share is kept, margins hold while costs rise, and exports and enterprise products add a second leg. None of this is unreasonable, but little of it is left to be discovered. The stock fell about 5% on Q1 results because margins dipped; the next few quarters will be judged mainly on margin and cash conversion, not on revenue growth.

The honest read

The share gain was the easy part and it is largely done. From here, growth slows to the market's rate plus whatever further share CP Plus takes from smaller brands, and returns depend on managing a component shortage. Prizor is a small, fast-growing SME name whose valuation already discounts several more years of doubling. Both are good businesses in a protected market; neither is cheap.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. How much of this year's growth is share gained from Chinese brands, and how much is market growth?
  2. What happens to the former Dahua distribution revenue — is any of it left?
  3. How many quarters of SoC and memory supply are secured, and at what prices?
  4. How much of the cost increase has been passed on, and what margin do you expect when pass-through is complete?
  5. What share of the bill of materials is imported, and what will localisation of lenses, enclosures and PCBs save?
  6. What is the timeline and cost of doubling capacity?
  7. How are channel inventory and dealer credit trending?
  8. What is the government and enterprise share of revenue, and how are payments?
  9. What is the plan for software and recurring revenue?
  10. What would you do if Chinese-owned brands were certified with non-Chinese chips?

19 · Glossary

Every term you'll meet

Products and technology

CCTV
Closed-circuit television — cameras whose video goes to a limited set of screens or recorders.
Analog camera
A camera that sends an analog video signal over coaxial cable.
HD-analog
Formats such as HDCVI, TVI and AHD that carry high-definition video over analog cabling.
IP camera
A network camera that digitises and sends video over a data network.
PoE
Power over Ethernet — power and data on one network cable.
DVR
Digital video recorder for analog cameras.
NVR
Network video recorder for IP cameras.
VMS
Video management software — records, displays and searches video from many cameras.
PTZ camera
A camera that can pan, tilt and zoom under remote control.
Bullet and dome cameras
The two common housings: cylindrical for outdoors, domed for ceilings.
Thermal camera
Sees heat rather than light; used for perimeters and fever screening.
ANPR
Automatic number-plate recognition.
Face recognition
Software that matches faces in video to a database.
Video analytics
Software that detects events such as intrusion, loitering or crowding.
Edge AI
Analytics that run inside the camera rather than on a server.
VSaaS
Video surveillance as a service — cloud recording and analysis sold by subscription.
Global shutter
A sensor that captures the whole frame at once; needed for fast-moving objects.
Explosion-proof camera
A housing certified for use in oil, gas and chemical plants.

Components

SoC
System-on-chip — the processor that encodes video and runs firmware and analytics.
Image sensor (CMOS)
The chip that converts light into an electrical signal.
DRAM
Working memory; DDR4 is the generation most cameras use.
HBM
High-bandwidth memory for AI chips, which has absorbed memory makers' capacity.
NAND flash
Storage memory for firmware and local recording.
Firmware
The software built into the camera.
BOM
Bill of materials — the list and cost of every part.
OEM / ODM
A maker that builds to another company's design (OEM) or designs and builds products that others sell under their brand (ODM).
White-labelling
Selling another maker's product under your own brand.

Regulation and security

Essential requirements (ER)
MeitY's 2024 cybersecurity requirements for CCTV cameras.
STQC
Standardisation Testing and Quality Certification, the government testing directorate under MeitY.
CRO
Compulsory registration order — BIS registration required before electronics can be sold.
IS 13252
The Indian safety standard for IT equipment under which cameras are registered.
Secure boot
A check that allows only signed, trusted firmware to run.
Firmware signing
A digital signature that proves firmware came from the maker and was not altered.
Penetration testing
A deliberate attempt to break into a device to find weaknesses.
Country-of-origin disclosure
The requirement to reveal where critical components such as the SoC were made.
NDAA Section 889
The 2018 US law barring federal agencies from buying Hikvision and Dahua equipment.
Entity List
The US export-control list to which Hikvision and Dahua were added in 2019.
Trusted source
A supplier approved on security grounds, as in India's telecom rules.
DLI scheme
Design-linked incentive — government support for Indian chip-design companies.

Market and finance

System integrator (SI)
A company that designs and installs a whole surveillance system from many products.
ICCC
Integrated command and control centre — a city's control room for cameras, traffic and services.
Safe City project
City surveillance and women's-safety projects funded partly through the Nirbhaya Fund.
Nirbhaya Fund
A central fund created in 2013 for women's safety.
GeM
Government e-Marketplace, the online portal for public procurement.
L1
The lowest bidder in a government tender.
Channel
Distributors, dealers and installers between the brand and the buyer.
Cash conversion cycle
Days of inventory plus receivables minus payables.
AMC
Annual maintenance contract.
SME platform
The stock exchanges' segment for small companies, with larger lots and thinner trading.
Pass-through
Passing higher input costs on to customers through price increases.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. What is the difference between a DVR and an NVR?

A DVR records analog cameras; an NVR records IP cameras over a network.

2. What rule reshaped the Indian market, and when did it take full effect?

MeitY's essential requirements for CCTV security, notified 9 April 2024; non-compliant cameras could not be sold from 1 April 2026.

3. Why could Hikvision and Dahua not comply?

Certification is refused to products with Chinese-origin chipsets, and their cameras use them.

4. What was CP Plus's market share in FY26?

43.3%, according to Frost & Sullivan.

5. What did CP Plus report in Q1 FY27?

Revenue ₹1,402 cr (+89.5%), EBITDA ₹208 cr at 14.8%, adjusted profit ₹142.2 cr.

6. Why did the margin fall from Q4 FY26?

Low-cost inventory ran out while memory and SoC prices surged; price increases are being passed on gradually.

7. Where do CP Plus's chips now come from?

Largely Taiwan — about 35% of the bill of materials.

8. Which three members of this section are misclassified?

Kavveri Defence (RF components, belongs in 058), Monarch Surveyors (engineering services) and MPF Systems (shell-like, bad data).

9. What does the Hikvision record teach?

Revenue more than doubled after 2017 while the share price fell; hardware leadership does not guarantee returns once growth slows.

10. What would most threaten the listed leaders?

A relaxation of the rule that lets Chinese-owned or Chinese-chip products back into the market.
