The industry in 60 seconds

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Imagine a bakery buying a new oven, conveyor and packing machine. The seller must make them work together, bake the promised number of good loaves, and help when something breaks. Selling the machine is only part of the job. The bakery may hold back some payment until the whole line works. Industrial machinery does the same thing with metal, grain, paper, tea and other materials. A machine that makes more usable output with fewer stoppages can be valuable. A large machine waiting for its customer to finish the factory can tie up the seller’s money.

Say it to a client in one minute

This primer merges Industrial Machinery, 28 source names, and Foundry & Metal Processing Equipment, one source name. The combined watchlist contains 29 companies, but only nine enter the price basket. It mixes complete machinery manufacturers with components, contractors, rental fleets, chemicals, consumer products and legacy classifications. A shared watchlist is not evidence of shared economics.

The useful investment distinction is between a supplier that repeatedly turns a customer’s production problem into collected cash and one that reports a growing book while financing unfinished work. DISA sells foundry systems; John Cockerill sells steel-processing lines; Energy-Mission makes sheet-metal machinery; Sona serves grain processing; T&I combines tea machinery with tea production. Lloyds and Walchandnagar span heavier engineering. ATV and Manugraph add smaller project and printing exposures. Their revenue recognition, reporting periods and financing burdens differ.

The basket lost 2.5% in 2026 through 18 September, against the Nifty’s 10.7% decline. Removing Lloyds and John Cockerill changes that result to a 15.6% loss. Removing the two recent SME entrants instead gives a 7.7% gain. Those are measured composition effects, not forecasts. The evidence argues for reading the companies individually before calling this a uniform capital-spending recovery.

Carry forward the Engineering family tests (see 078, see 080 and see 081) through 078 — Castings, 080 — Forgings and 081 — Fabrication. Apply them to process specification, retained engineering work and the contract between quotation and delivery. The machine-acceptance discussion in 089 — Machine Tools is the adjacent reference. Here the additional question is whether a complete production process delivers acceptable output under the customer’s actual operating conditions.

Throughout this page, operating earnings, reported profit and cash are separate observations. Other income is read before profit. An asset-sale gain does not demonstrate stronger machinery demand; a customer advance does not demonstrate completed work; an associate’s orders are not automatically consolidated orders. This distinction matters particularly for the companies whose latest profit is positive while core operations or annual cash generation remain weak.

The physical machine and the process promise

A motor rating or a nominal hourly capacity describes part of a machine. It does not establish how much saleable output the customer will obtain. Feed quality, operator skill, upstream supply and downstream handling can each constrain a line. A builder can therefore sell an apparently similar machine at a different price if its integration, reliability or support changes the customer’s usable output. The investment case should identify that measurable difference rather than rely on the general language of automation.

A foundry’s moulding equipment also sits within a larger system. Better mould consistency is valuable only if melting, pouring and finishing can support the required output. Steel processing brings the same issue at a different scale: a successful line must meet material and surface requirements, not merely transport strip from one end to the other. In grain or tea processing, the purchased material and the desired finished product influence what a successful acceptance trial looks like. These are different engineering problems, even when all the suppliers report revenue under machinery.

Components and consumables deserve separate treatment. A mechanical seal can be essential to uptime without being a complete production machine. An abrasive or masking product may benefit from factory activity without depending on new capacity orders. For that recurring-consumption framework, see 082 — Fasteners, Industrial Tools & Abrasives. Their presence in this source watchlist is a classification issue, not a reason to blend all their returns into a machinery index.

Service can reinforce customer retention, but the installed base must be economically accessible to the listed company. A global parent may own the technology while an Indian subsidiary earns manufacturing or service income under an agreement. A local contractor may install a machine but have no rights to its software. Before valuing recurring revenue, establish who sells the spare, owns the customer relationship, bears the warranty and can set the service price. A group-wide installation count alone does not settle any of those questions.

Fund-manager lens

Follow one order from the customer’s specification to the final bank receipt. If management can describe the equipment but cannot explain the acceptance milestone, funding requirement and rights to later service, the economic case is incomplete. DISA’s cash absorption and Cockerill’s advance-supported cash generation show why technical credibility and working-capital quality must be investigated separately.

The audit

Twenty exclusions remain visible above. Three source series end before the data date: Alphalogic on 15 September, Rex on 21 August and Rolcon on 7 September. They are not filled forward. Devson, Millworks, Paluck, Sumax, Systematic and Tipco have short histories; some also fail the product-scope test. Rathi, Rolcon, GTT and other excluded legacy series carry discontinuities recorded in the price audit. None of the nine included series has a scanned daily fall of at least 30%, rise of at least 50%, or gap longer than 45 days.

DISA and John Cockerill began their available NSE series only in April 2026. That does not make them new businesses. BSE history for the same ISIN was scaled at the first overlapping quote and joined to the NSE series. Their combined histories begin in February 2006 and December 2008 respectively. Lloyds begins in July 2016; no pre-listing history is invented. All nine basket series end on 18 September 2026 before any statistics are calculated.

Tipco is the clearest example of why exclusion needs a reason. Its products fit this primer, but a few months of prices cannot support the historical question. Conversely, Manugraph and Walchandnagar remain included despite weak operating or cash evidence. Removing companies for disappointing financial results would make the historical basket look better by construction. Membership is based on activity, usable history and endpoint integrity, with the mixed businesses openly identified.

What survives the common operating bridge

DISA’s June revenue contracted while ordinary other income remained material. Its operating proxy is positive, but the annual cash statement shows that converting earnings into cash needs separate attention. ATV’s higher revenue accompanies a small positive profit; tax losses and a deferred-tax credit affect how profit translates from pretax earnings. A low apparent tax rate is not an operating advantage that can simply be capitalised indefinitely.

Cockerill’s June group operating proxy is negative even before interest. The small JV contribution and ordinary other income do not change that conclusion. Its enlarged, restated group cannot be compared casually with an old standalone quarter. Lloyds reports the opposite direction of revenue growth, but its June presentation-style EBITDA of ₹79.23 crore includes ₹13.08 crore other income. The common operating bridge is ₹66.15 crore. That difference changes the margin from about 15.0% to 12.5%; neither figure should be quoted without its definition.

Manugraph’s reported profit is dominated by a property-sale gain. Its ₹9.7825 crore exceptional gain and ₹0.2703 crore employee-compensation charge sit outside the ordinary other-income line of ₹0.0247 crore. The core operating proxy is negative. Screening services that combine exceptional items with other income can obscure the distinction. Walchandnagar’s ₹6.97 crore other income includes a ₹3.34 crore asset-sale gain. The machinery activity cannot be judged from its positive ₹1.18 crore bottom line alone.

Energy-Mission’s half-year operating proxy is positive, but revenue growth and profit growth need not move together. Sona’s latest half-year revenue contracted and the operating proxy is negative. T&I’s group identity in a watchlist can also mislead: its standalone statement contains both machinery and tea production. June machinery revenue of ₹27.3972 crore and tea revenue of ₹2.8853 crore should not be interpreted as one pure equipment margin. Segment results are before some company-level items.

Why gross engineering content and shareholder profit diverge

Customisation can support pricing while extending design and commissioning time. A supplier may win a technically demanding contract and then absorb low utilisation, rework or delayed acceptance. Interest and depreciation continue while the job is incomplete. The best operating margin therefore does not automatically produce the best cash return, and a sudden profit recovery can come from a non-operating transaction rather than from better execution. The table deliberately preserves these differences instead of forcing a clean peer ranking.

The honest read

The current evidence does not support treating all positive PAT as a machinery upcycle. Manugraph needs a property-sale bridge; Walchandnagar needs an other-income and financing bridge; Lloyds needs an owner-profit and cash bridge. Build the operating case from accepted output and retained margin, then explain every step to the reported bottom line.

Operating cash flow is not a second measure of profit. It records when customer collections, supplier payments, inventory and other operating balances turn into cash. A growing machinery company can show weak cash because it is building a credible order book. It can also show weak cash because acceptance is delayed or customer balances are difficult to recover. The financial statement identifies the balances; the contract schedule and ageing data help distinguish those explanations.

Sona demonstrates a different route to positive cash: release of existing assets while revenue and earnings weaken. That can be useful liquidity, but it does not establish a repeatable growth engine. The next question is whether the released assets belonged to completed jobs, reduced activity or a deliberate tightening of credit. Its capacity investment then needs a separate funding bridge. A shrinking balance sheet can improve cash temporarily even while demand remains soft.

Lloyds’ rights issue makes the distinction especially concrete. Shareholders supplied financing cash while operating activities consumed cash. That financing can support growth, but it changes the capital base on which future returns must be earned. An investor should ask how much of the new money funds productive capacity, how much funds inventory or customer balances, and when those investments become collections. Counting financing inflows as evidence of strong operating conversion would reverse the meaning of the cash statement.

Working-capital terms also change risk between counterparties. An advance backed by a performance guarantee may support production but create a contingent obligation. Retention protects the customer until acceptance or warranty conditions are met. Supplier credit can reduce immediate cash needs while exposing the business to renegotiation if payments stretch. Read the maturity, security and conditions attached to each balance; a single net-working-capital ratio can hide very different contracts.

The test to apply

Ask management to reconcile the opening order book, new orders, recognised revenue, cancelled work, cash collected and closing contract assets/liabilities. Then connect capex and financing to the remaining execution schedule. For Lloyds, the key test is conversion of the funded build-up; for Cockerill, delivery against advance-supported contracts; for Sona, whether cash release can coexist with restored operating demand.

The critical input is often the component or skill that prevents final acceptance, not the largest line in the purchase ledger. A bought-out controller can be a small fraction of equipment value and still determine dispatch. A specialist engineer can be essential to a commissioning trial even when the factory has spare fabrication capacity. Capacity analysis should identify the bottleneck in accepted output rather than sum floor space, machines or employee numbers.

Metal cost matters through the quotation and procurement sequence described in 081. An OEM with contractual escalation and timely procurement faces a different exposure from one that quoted a fixed price before the design was final. Currency exposure likewise depends on when imported assemblies are ordered, whether customer pricing adjusts, and whether hedging matches the actual payment schedule. A rupee movement is not uniformly favourable for an exporter that also imports important components.

Quality failure is another input cost. Rework can consume engineering time, machine hours and field visits without appearing as a separate reported segment. A cheaper supplier is beneficial only if the delivered assembly meets the necessary process duty. The relevant procurement question is the cost of an accepted, supported machine, including rejected parts and commissioning effort. That is where the processing-depth framework in 080 becomes operational rather than rhetorical.

The calendar on a share-price chart is regular; the machinery business calendar is not. A factory project can be postponed when finance, land, approvals or customer demand changes. An existing plant may need installation to coincide with a shutdown. A process trial may be repeated if the customer’s feed or operating conditions differ from the agreed specification. These mechanisms can produce lumpy revenue without proving a permanent change in the supplier’s competitive position.

That does not justify dismissing every miss as timing. A credible timing explanation identifies the job, unfinished milestone, revised completion schedule and cash consequence. Repeatedly moving the same order without disclosing these details weakens the explanation. Watch whether inventory, contract assets or overdue receivables rise alongside the claimed delay. The balance sheet often shows whether a slipped revenue milestone is also a financing problem.

Seasonality can therefore be useful as a research calendar. A recurrent strong month may suggest when investor expectations often improve, but it does not establish that customer orders are signed in that month. This primer measures market-relative returns independently of the operating calendar. It does not retrofit each positive April to a particular government announcement or spending decision. Such causal claims would require separate dated evidence.

Reporting frequency adds another layer. Comparing June quarterly revenue at DISA with March half-year revenue at Energy-Mission would confuse both duration and date. Comparing Cockerill’s December year with another company’s March year would mix economic windows. The tables label those differences so that an investor can use the evidence that actually exists, and keep the next missing financial release on the watch list.

The failures are part of the result

April underperformed in 2018 by 4.9 points and in 2019 by 11.5 points. Its largest positive relative observations in this basket are 2026 at 24.8 points and 2024 at 22.5 points. Those data explain the average; they do not establish an industry-wide record. The verification script ranks the basket against the other published baskets before any superlative could be used.

The wider April–July window failed in 2012, 2013, 2016, 2017, 2018 and 2019. The 2018 and 2019 relative losses were 24.3 and 26.9 points. February–March was positive only in 2012 and 2017, yet April–July failed in both those years. The sequence matters: a weak early year followed by a generally strong April is not a guaranteed reversal strategy, and a strong early year does not protect the following window.

The published series begins in July 2011 with the available legacy members. Lloyds joins only when its 2016 history permits monthly returns; Sona and Energy-Mission enter after their 2024 listings. Each month equally weights available returns and requires at least two names. Individual monthly returns are capped at plus or minus 50% by the shared pipeline. This reduces the effect of extreme observations but also means the basket is not the raw annual average of individual stock returns.

The study uses the project’s closing-price series, not a reconstructed dividend-reinvestment portfolio. It does not model brokerage, impact costs, taxes, circuit restrictions or whether equal weights could have been executed in small stocks. Survivorship and changing membership remain. The sensitivity results are therefore particularly useful: the April pattern moves relatively little across these removals, while current-year performance changes materially.

How to use this

Treat April as a prompt to prepare company work before the market’s recurring window of strength. The current-year composition test is more important for exposure selection: removing Lloyds and Cockerill changes −2.5% to −15.6%. A basket label can conceal concentrated drivers even when its long-run monthly tendency looks stable.

The global industry and India’s place

These companies compete in several global markets rather than one homogeneous industrial-machinery pool. A steel-processing line, foundry system and grain-processing machine solve different production problems. Process references, service reach, financing and technology rights can matter as much as manufacturing cost. An Indian supplier can gain work through engineering capability or localisation, but domestic production does not by itself establish ownership of the underlying process technology.

SMS Group’s 2025 release illustrates the distinction between orders and revenue at a global metals-equipment supplier: order intake was about €4.0 billion, up 10%, while revenue fell to about €3.6 billion because large projects had not yet completed. EBIT was €145 million. That is a useful example of execution timing, not a numerical forecast for the Indian basket. The figures refer to SMS’s global perimeter. SMS Group, 2025 results released 20 May 2026

The global comparison should begin with responsibility. Does a supplier design the full process, build under licence, manufacture selected assemblies, commission the line, or provide replacement parts? Each position can be commercially attractive, but the margin, capital intensity and addressable customer relationship differ. A global parent’s engineering reputation may support an Indian listed subsidiary while contracts still limit what that subsidiary owns or earns. Related-party arrangements deserve the same scrutiny as external supplier contracts.

Cockerill’s August 2026 discussion makes that boundary specific. Management distinguishes listed-group activities from technology owned elsewhere in the parent group, including Volteron intellectual property at John Cockerill SA. It also distinguishes pilot/R&D work from commercial orders. The stated longer-term revenue ambition includes acquisitions as well as organic growth. It should not be treated as a contracted revenue schedule or evidence that the listed entity owns every technology associated with the brand. August 2026 management call

India’s automation opportunity is real but must be scoped. The IFR’s September 2025 release reports 9,120 industrial robot installations in India during 2024, up 7%, ranking India sixth by installations. Automotive accounted for 4,070 units, about 45%; the metal industry recorded 420 installations. These are robot-adoption statistics, not a forecast for the sales of this mixed machinery roster. For investors, localisation has several possible meanings. It can shorten delivery, improve service access, adapt equipment to local feed or reduce imported content. It can also leave a company dependent on licensed controls and process designs. Establish which cost, capability or customer problem changes. A domestic address and a broad import-substitution theme are insufficient without evidence of qualification, accepted installations and commercially retained work.

Technology should ultimately be translated into customer economics. Ask whether it raises acceptable throughput, improves yield, lowers energy use, reduces changeover, or makes an otherwise difficult product feasible. Then identify how much of that benefit the supplier captures through price, repeat orders or service. A technical improvement can be useful to the customer without creating a durable profit pool for the machine builder if competitors can replicate it or the parent retains the economics.

There is also a support cost to complexity. More software, sensors and specialised components can improve operation while requiring better diagnostic capability and spare availability. A supplier expanding product breadth must fund the service network that accompanies it. The relevant evidence is reliable field performance and paid support, not simply a longer catalogue. Ask how old installations remain serviceable when controls or components are replaced by newer versions.

Policy, contracts and the cutoff date

The 2024 machinery omnibus technical regulation was withdrawn. Gazette S.O.239(E), dated 14 January and published 16 January 2026, rescinds that order with immediate effect. Its former implementation timetable must not be presented as an active future catalyst at this primer’s cutoff. This does not establish that every product-specific standard, safety obligation or certification requirement has disappeared. Those need to be checked for the actual product and destination. Gazette S.O.239(E), machinery omnibus order withdrawal, January 2026

For machinery companies, customer qualification and contractual compliance can matter alongside public regulation. A defence, nuclear or demanding process customer may require specific documentation, testing or approved procedures. Those requirements should be read from the applicable tender or customer programme, not inferred from a company’s use of a sector label. A new qualification can create an opportunity; actual orders, economics and repeat delivery still need to follow.

ATV provides an example of why dated updates matter. Its current filing reports that an appeal against a ₹21.59 crore GST demand was allowed on 17 July 2026, with a ₹0.7243 crore predeposit refund. An older annual-report description of the matter as pending must be read with that later update. The event changes the legal-status reading; it is not a recurring machinery revenue stream. June-result notes and subsequent-event update

Cockerill’s current filing also reports a customer arbitration concerning alleged performance of a cold-rolling mill. Management disputes the claim. The existence of a dispute is relevant to contract risk, but allegations should not be presented as an adjudicated finding of fault. The analytical request is for the amount, stage, security, provisions and possible execution or collection consequences, with the company’s position distinguished from the customer’s allegation.

Foreign-market access should be investigated at the product and contract level. Relevant questions include origin, destination, technical requirements, customer acceptance and the allocation of duty or delivery changes. This primer does not apply an uncited single tariff rate to every machine or component. Nor does it assume that a global parent can transfer technology to an Indian listed company without commercial terms or approvals. Those are specific diligence questions, not automatic benefits of belonging to an engineering group.

Order visibility is not margin visibility

An order book can contain long projects, bought-out content, conditional milestones or customers whose schedules move. It can grow because of acquisition, currency translation or consolidation rather than new organic demand. A useful comparison therefore states the date and perimeter, separates the parent from subsidiaries and associates, and asks how much work will execute in the next reporting period. Dividing an undated book by a mismatched revenue denominator creates a false appearance of precision.

Cockerill’s 2026 common-control acquisition of the Belgian metals business changes its group comparison. The disclosed consideration is €29.67 million, with €5 million paid in June and the balance linked to proposed financing arrangements. The planned US business addition discussed for later in 2026 must remain future and conditional at the June reporting point. This is why the enlarged group figures are labelled restated and the parent book is not added to the consolidated book.

Lloyds’ associate orders likewise do not become fully consolidated revenue simply because they appear beside the group backlog. Its latest quarter includes ₹13.13 crore associate contribution, with minority interests also separating group and owner profit. Pending restructuring and proposed share issuance require their own approval and dilution analysis. The operative question is the earnings and cash claim of the listed shareholder, not the largest aggregate order number that can be assembled from the group.

The constructive case is that specialised engineering, localisation and customer investment provide opportunities for these suppliers. The evidence that would strengthen that case is accepted output, stable operating margins excluding non-core items, and conversion of funded work into cash. The evidence against it would be repeated execution slippage, rising old balances, more financing without better returns, or growth dependent on changing accounting scope. Those tests are more useful than assigning the whole roster a single point on a cycle diagram.

Questions to ask management

Use these to request auditable bridges rather than broad reassurance. The answer should identify a period, a legal entity and the underlying source. A missing disclosure is a reason to limit precision, not to supply an assumed number.

What exactly is in the order book?

Give its date, legal entities, signed-versus-conditional status, customer concentration, cancellations and execution schedule. Separate parent, subsidiaries and associates. Explain whether acquired orders or currency changes contributed to growth.

Which milestone releases the final payment?

Describe factory tests, site readiness, commissioning and acceptance conditions. Show retention and guarantees. Identify who pays for a delay caused by the customer, the supplier or an adjoining production line.

How does revenue become cash?

Bridge opening contract assets, current work, invoices, collections and closing balances. Age the overdue amounts. Explain whether strong cash comes from profit, customer advances, delayed supplier payment or release of old assets.

What is your operating EBITDA definition?

Start with the filed result and show ordinary other income, exceptional items, associate/JV income and any adjustments. Make the bridge reproducible rather than giving a percentage with an undefined numerator.

Whose profit underlies EPS?

Reconcile group PAT to owner PAT, then show basic and diluted weighted shares. Lloyds needs a clear explanation of its annual numerator; Walchandnagar needs the printed annual share-count discrepancy resolved.

What customer output proves the technology?

Specify the feed, accepted quality, throughput, uptime and test conditions. Distinguish pilot demonstrations from commercial references. Identify the economic benefit captured by the listed company rather than by a parent or licensor.

Who owns the IP and service relationship?

Provide the relevant licence, royalty, manufacturing and service arrangements. Explain which entity can sell upgrades and replacements, who bears warranty, and whether rights continue if the group structure changes.

What has actually been commissioned?

Separate announced capacity, installed machinery, trial production, customer approvals and commercial utilisation. Show the remaining cash requirement and the expected bottleneck after the current expansion completes.

Which orders carry input-price risk?

Explain fixed-price versus adjustable contracts, purchase coverage, foreign exchange and design changes. Quantify the exposed schedule from company records rather than assuming a general metal-price move flows uniformly into margin.

How are project risks provided for?

Describe estimated completion costs, warranties, liquidated damages and disputed customer claims. For a troubled job, show what changed in the latest estimate and how that affected reported profit and future cash.

What does a repeat customer actually repeat?

Distinguish a new complete line, replacement machine, paid spare, service visit and warranty call. Give evidence of paid economics and customer retention, not merely a cumulative installation count.

What would make you slow expansion?

Identify the utilisation, collection, leverage and order-quality conditions that would change capex or hiring. A credible capital-allocation policy should explain what management does when demand or acceptance runs behind plan.

The most useful debate is between credible explanations that imply different next observations. Weak cash can be the temporary cost of a funded growth cycle, or evidence of poor order quality. Strong cash can reflect successful execution, or an advance that must fund unfinished obligations. Both interpretations should be tested against the next movement in the relevant balances. A narrative that cannot be falsified by future evidence is not a useful investment framework.

The decision rule

Prefer a thesis that can name the next acceptance, cash-conversion or ownership test. The evidence here supports opportunities in specialised machinery, with substantial differences in earnings quality and funding. It does not support valuing every backlog rupee, every reported PAT rupee or every global technology claim on the same basis.

Glossary: find a term

Equipment and production processes

OEM
Original equipment manufacturer: the business responsible for the equipment it sells, although some assemblies or technology may come from specialist suppliers.
Foundry
A plant that makes castings by pouring molten metal into moulds; equipment demand depends on the required process and customer output.
Moulding line
Equipment that forms moulds for castings and coordinates their movement through the production sequence.
Green sand
A moist sand mixture used to form casting moulds; process consistency matters to the quality of the resulting castings.
Matchplate
A moulding arrangement using a plate carrying corresponding pattern halves; an equipment category distinct from other moulding configurations.
Sand preparation
The treatment and control of moulding sand so it can provide the required properties in the casting process.
Cold rolling
Reducing or shaping metal below its recrystallisation temperature; machinery must meet the specified dimensions and material requirements.
Annealing
Heat treatment used to change material properties; line performance depends on controlled processing conditions, not just installed furnace size.
Galvanising
Applying a zinc coating to steel for protection; coating quality and process control form part of equipment acceptance.
JVD
Jet Vapour Deposition, a vacuum zinc-deposition technology discussed in the Cockerill portfolio; ownership and commercial contracts require separate verification.
Press brake
A machine that bends sheet metal using controlled tooling movement; accuracy and repeatability matter across the customer’s material range.
Shearing machine
Equipment that cuts sheet or plate through shearing action; capacity must be interpreted for the specified material and thickness.
Dispersion
Distribution of particles or another phase within a material; mixing equipment is selected for the required process outcome.
Throughput
Output per unit of time. Nameplate throughput differs from acceptable output achieved under real operating conditions.
Yield
Usable output relative to input; a higher production rate is less valuable if rejection or loss also rises.
Changeover
The work and time needed to move a machine or line from one product, setting or job to another.
Registration
Alignment of successive printing elements; an example of the process-quality requirements that distinguish printing machinery from generic equipment.
Installed base
Equipment already supplied and operating with customers; potential service access, but not proof of paid recurring revenue.

Contracts, delivery and technology rights

Backlog
Orders not yet recognised as completed revenue under the stated definition; always identify date, scope, conditions and execution schedule.
Order intake
New orders booked in a period; different from revenue and from the closing stock of unexecuted orders.
Factory acceptance test
A test at the supplier’s premises against agreed criteria; it may not eliminate later installation or site-acceptance obligations.
Site acceptance test
A test after installation that checks performance in the customer’s operating environment and may release payment milestones.
Commissioning
Preparing and proving installed equipment for operation, including integration, checks, trials and necessary adjustments.
Retention
A portion of payment held back until specified contractual conditions are met, potentially after revenue has been recognised.
Performance guarantee
A contractual assurance of specified performance; associated bank guarantees or remedies can create financial exposure for the supplier.
Liquidated damages
Contractually specified compensation for defined failures such as delay; applicability depends on the contract and circumstances.
Turnkey contract
A contract to deliver a functioning facility or system within the agreed scope; the label does not establish which risks were accepted.
EPC
Engineering, procurement and construction. A contractor can execute a project without owning the underlying machinery technology.
Bought-out component
An assembly or part purchased from another supplier and incorporated into the equipment or project.
Design freeze
The point at which agreed design requirements are fixed for execution; later changes can affect cost, schedule and acceptance.
Site readiness
Availability of foundations, utilities, access and adjoining systems needed for installation and trials.
Warranty
An obligation to remedy covered faults for the stated period and conditions; free warranty work is not paid service revenue.
Technology licence
Permission to use specified intellectual property under commercial terms; it does not necessarily transfer ownership.
Royalty
Payment for use of intellectual property or other rights; its basis and beneficiary affect the listed company’s retained economics.
Commercial reference
A demonstrated customer installation in commercial use; stronger evidence than a pilot alone, with the scope still needing verification.
Pilot project
A limited demonstration or development installation; success does not automatically establish a large commercial order.

Financial statements and shareholder claims

Other income
Income outside the main operating revenue line, such as treasury income; read its components before interpreting reported profit.
Exceptional item
A separately disclosed item whose nature or size deserves attention; classification does not by itself determine whether cash recurs.
Operating EBITDA proxy
The common bridge used here: pretax profit plus interest and depreciation, excluding ordinary other income, exceptional items and associate/JV results.
PAT
Profit after tax for the stated entity and period; group PAT can differ from the amount attributable to the listed company’s owners.
Owner PAT
Profit attributable to equity owners of the parent after separating non-controlling interests; the appropriate shareholder attribution needs checking.
Non-controlling interest
The share of a subsidiary’s net assets or earnings attributable to investors other than the parent group’s owners.
Associate
An investee over which the investor has significant influence; its revenue and orders are not automatically fully consolidated into the investor.
Joint venture
A jointly controlled arrangement whose accounting and ownership must be read from the statements; do not add its whole backlog without qualification.
Basic EPS
Earnings per share using the applicable earnings numerator and weighted ordinary shares for the reporting period.
Diluted EPS
Per-share earnings reflecting applicable potentially dilutive securities under the accounting calculation, not simply closing issued capital.
Weighted shares
Shares averaged according to their presence during the reporting period; issuance timing makes them different from the closing share count.
Rights issue
An equity financing offered through rights to existing holders; proceeds fund the company but are not operating revenue or operating cash.
Contract asset
A recognised right associated with contract work whose billing or collection depends on conditions beyond the passage of time.
Contract liability
An obligation associated with customer consideration received or due before the corresponding contracted goods or services are transferred.
Operating cash flow
Cash generated or consumed by operating activities after relevant working-capital movements; it can diverge materially from reported profit.
Working capital
Operating resources tied in inventories, customer balances and related items, net of operating funding such as supplier or customer balances.
Common-control transaction
A transfer between entities under common control; the reported comparative basis can change and requires explicit reconciliation.
Restated comparative
An earlier-period figure presented on a revised basis; it should not be mixed silently with an unrevised standalone history.

Investment and price-study vocabulary

Capital expenditure
Spending on longer-lived assets; announced capacity, cash spending and productive utilisation are separate observations.
Free cash flow
Cash remaining under a specified definition after investment needs; operating cash alone is not the same measure.
Capacity utilisation
Actual use of installed productive capability; useful capacity depends on bottlenecks, labour, product mix and acceptance requirements.
Operating leverage
The effect of changes in activity on profit where some costs remain fixed; it can work against a supplier when utilisation falls.
Margin mix
The effect on overall profitability of changing product, customer, project or service composition.
Revenue perimeter
The legal entities and activities included in a reported sales number; acquisitions and consolidation can change it.
Basis risk
Mismatch between a proxy or hedge and the actual exposure being assessed; a broad sector indicator may not track a specific supplier.
Equal weighting
Giving each available constituent the same monthly weight in this study, rather than weighting by market capitalisation.
Relative return
Basket return minus the benchmark return in percentage points over the same interval; outperformance can occur in a falling basket.
Hit rate
The number of observed periods with positive relative return divided by the total comparable periods; it is not a forecast probability.
Median
The middle observation in an ordered set; often less influenced by an extreme result than the arithmetic mean.
Monthly clipping
Capping each constituent’s monthly return at the pipeline’s stated limits before averaging; it changes the resulting basket path.
Survivorship bias
Distortion from studying names present in today’s universe while past failures, delistings or other absent companies are not represented.
Changing membership
The entry of companies when usable price history becomes available; the early basket is not identical to today’s full basket.
Price splice
Joining two exchange histories for the same security on a documented overlapping-price basis, after checking identity.
ISIN
International securities identification number; a useful identity check before joining exchange records or assessing apparently related securities.
Stale quote
A series whose last trade or available observation predates the study cutoff; carrying it forward would create an invented current observation.
Window drawdown
Distance from a high within a specified reference window; not necessarily the all-time drawdown or maximum loss experienced by an investor.

Check your understanding

Why are there 29 roster names but nine basket members?

The watchlist includes adjacent components, contractors, rentals, chemicals, consumer goods and short or stale histories. Every exclusion is visible; poor operating performance alone does not exclude an otherwise eligible machinery company.

Why is Manugraph’s positive PAT insufficient evidence of recovery?

The latest profit includes a property-sale gain, while the common operating bridge is negative. The gain and ordinary other income are separate lines.

Can Cockerill’s parent and group books be added?

No. The parent is already within the group. The date and accounting perimeter must accompany each order figure.

Why does Lloyds require both a cash and an EPS bridge?

Annual operations consumed cash while the rights issue supplied financing. Separately, the annual EPS note uses group profit despite a different owner-profit figure.

Does a 13/15 April hit rate establish a four-month trade?

No. April–July failed in six of fifteen observations, and costs, liquidity, changing members and clipping also limit the historical study.

What is wrong with using an old regulation timetable?

The machinery omnibus order was withdrawn in January 2026. Product-specific obligations need separate current checks; the rescinded timetable is not a future catalyst.

Sources and method

Primary result filings govern period labels, other income, profit and capital. Company decks and calls supply management claims and future plans, identified as such. Annual reports establish business classification and the cash/ownership notes. Screener’s raw tables are retained as locators and reconciliation diagnostics; a rounded or shifted web table does not override the original filing. All financial and policy evidence used for this snapshot is dated no later than 18 September 2026.

Figures in rupees lakh are divided by 100 to obtain crore; rupees million by ten. EPS is in rupees and share counts in crore shares. Latest quarterly and half-year profit checks preserve rounding and explicitly disclose unresolved shareholder-basis or weighted-share inconsistencies. Cockerill’s incomplete independently available restated quarterly set is not completed with unrelated standalone numbers.

The nine price series were checked for the fixed endpoint before calculation. The BSE/NSE splices, all-roster discontinuity scan, monthly sensitivities, individual calendar returns, annual locator bridges and EPS checks are preserved in the repository audit files. Statistics use the shared pipeline; 090’s display postprocessor recomputes the historical-high comparison for all nine selected members so smaller names remain visible. It does not alter earlier primers.
