Zinc, Lead & Silver

Zinc's job is to die so that steel does not. Coat a steel sheet in zinc and the zinc corrodes first, sacrificing itself for decades — which is why zinc demand is really galvanised-steel demand. Lead sits in the same ore body and goes almost entirely into batteries. And in the same rock, in quantities that matter enormously, sits silver. Hindustan Zinc is the world's largest integrated zinc producer and one of its largest silver producers, and in the year to September 2026 silver did something remarkable: it went from $29 an ounce to $115 and back to $67. This primer explains the metals, the company that is the entire Indian sector, and why a record quarter produced a falling share price.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

One company, three metals, and a silver problem

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Steel rusts if you leave it out in the rain. So we dip it in melted zinc, which forms a skin that rusts instead of the steel — like a bodyguard that takes the hit. Zinc comes out of the ground mixed with lead, which goes into car batteries, and with a little silver, which is very valuable. In Rajasthan there is one enormous mine that produces all three, and lately the silver in it has been worth so much that the zinc almost feels like a side business.

Say it to a client in one minute

A record quarter and a falling stock, and both make sense.

  • The quarter: revenue ₹13,747 cr (+77%), EBITDA ₹8,074 cr (+109%), profit ₹5,469 cr (+145%), operating margin 52% against 38%, with unit costs the lowest since the underground transition.
  • The reason: silver averaged $73.2 an ounce, up 117%, while zinc averaged $3,466 a tonne, up 31%. Volumes barely moved — silver output was flat at 149 tonnes.
  • The problem: silver peaked at $115 in January 2026 and was $67 by 18 September. A profit built on that price is not a run rate.
  • The overhang: the government holds 29.54% and is committed to selling it through an offer for sale.
  • The structure: after Vedanta's 2026 demerger, the residual Vedanta entity — not one of the four new companies — holds its roughly 63.4% of Hindustan Zinc.

The economics of a polymetallic mine are worth understanding. Zinc, lead and silver all come out of the same drilling, blasting and hauling; only the separation differs. That means the cost of producing silver here is not the cost of a silver mine — it is close to zero at the margin, because the rock had to be moved anyway. When silver more than doubles, almost all of that increase drops to the operating line. The same arithmetic works in reverse.

The chain in one breath

  • One ore body in Rajasthan yields zinc, lead and silver together.
  • Flotation splits them into separate concentrates.
  • Smelters turn concentrate into metal and make acid from the sulphur.
  • A refinery pulls silver out of the lead stream.
  • Galvanisers use the zinc to stop steel rusting for thirty years.
Fund-manager lens

Hindustan Zinc is among the lowest-cost zinc producers in the world, which is the durable part of the story: low-cost assets survive every cycle and take share when high-cost mines close. The volatile part is the by-product. In the June quarter, silver at $73.2 an ounce with output flat at 149 tonnes did most of the work in taking the operating margin from 38% to 52%. Model the zinc business on cost-curve logic and the silver as a separate, marked-to-market option — and never blend them into one multiple.

The audit: a sector of one

Of the five names in this watchlist section, one is a zinc and lead producer. Ardee listed six weeks ago; Lead Reclaim is a recycler with three years of history; Sizemasters and Grand Foundry are neither zinc nor lead businesses on any reading of their classification. The basket is therefore Hindustan Zinc alone, and every seasonal and cyclical number on this page is that single company's record. We say so rather than padding the basket with unrelated micro-caps, which would produce a chart that looks like an industry and measures nothing.

This matters beyond bookkeeping. India's zinc industry genuinely is one company — there is no second integrated producer — so the concentration in the data reflects the concentration in reality. The lead industry is different: it is large, and almost entirely unlisted and informal, built on recycling used batteries. Neither fact is visible from the watchlist.

The underground transition, and why cost matters more than price

Hindustan Zinc spent a decade converting Rampura Agucha — once one of the world's great open pits — into an underground mine, along with expanding its other underground operations. Underground mining costs more per tonne than open-pit, so unit costs rose through that transition and the market marked the company down for it. The current quarter's claim of the lowest cost since that transition is therefore a genuine milestone rather than a boast: it says the expensive part of the conversion is behind it.

Cost position is the thing worth owning in zinc. The metal's price is set globally and the company cannot influence it, but a producer in the bottom quartile of the cost curve makes money when the marginal producer does not, and gains share when high-cost mines close. That is exactly what happened in 2016, the best year in this series: the closure of the Century mine in Australia and Lisheen in Ireland took a large slice of world supply out, zinc roughly doubled off its low, and the stock rose 74.5%.

The dividend question

This company's distinguishing financial feature is that it pays out most of what it earns — the April interim dividend alone was ₹11 a share, about ₹4,648 crore. That is partly a function of ownership: Vedanta needs cash at the parent, and the government, holding 29.54%, receives a proportionate share. For a minority shareholder the practical consequence is that this is a yield instrument attached to a commodity price, and the yield is as cyclical as the commodity. Do not annualise a dividend declared in a silver spike.

5b · The silver problem

$29 to $115 to $67 in twelve months

The single most important number for this company in 2026 is not a zinc price. It is silver, and its path has been extraordinary. Silver ended 2024 at about $29.2 an ounce. It ended 2025 at $70.6. It peaked intraday-close at $115.5 on 26 January 2026. By 18 September 2026 it was back to $67.1. In the June quarter, between those two points, it averaged $73.2 — and that average is what produced a 145% rise in profit on flat silver volumes.

Read the earnings carefully

Saleable silver production was flat at 149 tonnes. Zinc volumes grew modestly. Essentially all of the incremental profit came from price, on a by-product, at a level that has since fallen by almost half from its January peak. A reported profit is a fact; a run rate is an assumption. Anyone building a forward model off this quarter should replace the realised silver price with a price they are willing to defend, and watch what happens to the 52% margin.

Why silver moved

Silver has an unusual dual character: it is both an industrial metal and a monetary one. On the industrial side, photovoltaic cells use a meaningful quantity per panel and solar installation has grown enormously; electronics use more each year. On the monetary side, it trades as a leveraged version of gold — smaller market, thinner liquidity, and therefore far larger percentage moves in both directions. A parabolic move to $115 and a halving within eight months is the signature of the monetary side taking over, and it is precisely why a mining company whose profit rides on it deserves a lower multiple, not a higher one, at the top.

What this means for the sector

Three practical implications follow. First, Hindustan Zinc is now partly a silver security, and should be compared with silver producers as well as with zinc ones. Second, the government's 29.54% stake sale becomes more likely at high metal prices, which puts a supply overhang above the stock exactly when the earnings look best. Third, the zinc business itself is doing fine and is being ignored — at $3,466 a tonne and falling unit costs, the base business is more profitable than it has been in years, which is what should survive when the silver price normalises.

5c · The zinc balance

The surplus that never arrived

While the market has been watching silver, the zinc side of this business has quietly done something more interesting: it has gone from an expected glut to a shortage inside one year, and almost nobody noticed. The sequence is a useful case study in how base-metal forecasts actually behave.

  1. The forecast said surplus. At the start of the year the International Lead and Zinc Study Group expected a comfortable oversupply of refined zinc.
  2. Mine supply disappointed. ILZSG preliminary data show world zinc mine production fell 2.6% in the first half, reversing the 1.1% growth its own monthly series had been recording through May.
  3. The balance flipped. The group swung its 2026 estimate by about 300,000 tonnes — from a 271,000-tonne surplus to a deficit — while forecasting a surplus in lead. Refined demand is expected around 14.00 million tonnes against output of 13.99 million.
  4. Stocks fell and the price followed. LME stocks dropped toward 95,000 tonnes and three-month zinc reached $3,949.50 a tonne on 26 August 2026, its strongest since June 2022 — all of it despite unremarkable Chinese demand.

Two lessons generalise well beyond zinc. First, base metals move on supply misses, not demand beats: a 2.6% shortfall in mine output was enough to turn a 271,000-tonne surplus into a deficit, because demand simply does not vary that much year to year. Second, the published balance is an estimate that gets revised by more than the balance itself — a 300,000-tonne revision on a 14 million-tonne market is small in percentage terms and decisive in price terms. This is why the cost-curve position of a producer matters more than anyone's forecast: low-cost tonnes are profitable whichever way the estimate is revised.

For Hindustan Zinc specifically, it means the base business is entering a tighter market than consensus expected at the start of the year, with regional premiums and local conditions increasingly dispersing the price that different producers actually realise. And it means the lead half of the business faces the opposite condition — a forecast surplus — which is one reason lead volumes and realisations deserve their own line in any model rather than being lumped in with zinc.

7 · The business calendar

Mines run all year; dividends do not

  • January–March: the fiscal year-end; production is pushed to hit annual guidance, and full-year output numbers land in early April.
  • April: historically the best month (8 wins of 15, +3.4 points), and often when an interim dividend is declared — as it was on 24 April 2026.
  • June: the weakest month (−2.6 average), and the monsoon slows galvanised-steel demand.
  • July–September: monsoon affects mining logistics more than smelting.
  • October–December: the construction season lifts galvanising, though the stock has won only 6 of 14 such windows.

8 · What the stock does

Seasonality test on TradingView prices

Reading a flat calendar honestly

  • No window wins more than 8 times in 15 years. April–July has worked only 4 times in 15, with a median of −4.0, yet its mean is +0.8 because of one enormous year (+94.5 in 2024).
  • The mean and median diverge everywhere, which is the statistical fingerprint of a single stock driven by occasional large re-ratings rather than a repeating seasonal demand pattern.
  • The largest single-window moves — 2024's April–July and 2016's February–March (+11.7) — both coincide with metal-price regime changes, not with the calendar.
How to use this — the honest answer

Do not use it. This is the one industry in the Metals family where the seasonal test produces no usable signal, and the reason is structural: zinc's Indian demand is galvanised steel, which is seasonal, but the stock's returns are dominated by the silver price, the dividend and the government stake sale, none of which follow a calendar. Reporting that clearly is more useful than manufacturing a pattern from fifteen observations of one company. The contrast with copper (073), where twelve of fourteen October–December windows worked across four companies, shows what a real signal looks like.

The anatomy of the cycles

  1. The commodity slide (2012–2015): silver fell from $30 to $13.8 and zinc with it; the stock lagged in three of four years.
  2. The supply shock (2016–2017): the Century and Lisheen mine closures took world zinc supply out and the stock rose 74.5% in a flat market — the clearest demonstration that zinc is a supply story.
  3. The underground years (2018–2023): the costly conversion of Rampura Agucha, a shelved demerger, a governance dispute over buying the parent's international zinc assets, and six years of going nowhere.
  4. The silver re-rating (2024–2026): silver from $29 to $70 to a $115 spike; two strong years, then a flat one as the market discounted the peak.

Patterns that repeat

  • Zinc rallies begin with mine closures, not with demand — 2016 is the template.
  • Governance events cost real money here — the 2022–23 dispute over related-party asset purchases coincided with two lost years.
  • The stock discounts peak by-product prices rather than capitalising them, which is why 2026 is flat on record earnings.

Zinc differs from copper in one important way: there is no shortage of smelting, but mine supply is unusually prone to sudden, large closures because so much of it comes from a handful of very large, very old orebodies that eventually run out. The 2016 episode — two mines ending their lives within months of each other — moved the world price more than a decade of demand growth had. For a low-cost producer, that is the asymmetry worth owning.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Deeper underground mining. Automation, battery-electric underground vehicles and remote operation to hold costs down as mines deepen.
  2. Silver thrifting in solar. Panel makers keep reducing silver per cell, which is a real long-term risk to industrial silver demand.
  3. Lithium displacing lead in batteries. Slower than expected, but it eventually shrinks the largest lead market; see 046.
  4. Zinc-based batteries. Zinc-air and zinc-bromine chemistries as cheap, safe grid storage — a potential new demand pool.
  5. Renewable power at mines. Displacing captive coal to cut both cost and carbon intensity.
  6. Zinc recovery from steel dust. Electric-arc furnace dust is zinc-rich, linking 066 and 071 to this chain.

What is priced in

A silver price well below the one in the reported numbers. That is the whole explanation for a stock that is flat in a year of record profits, and it is a rational discount rather than a market error: the June quarter's $73.2 average is 9% above the September spot price and 36% below the January peak, so neither the earnings nor the dividend they funded should be annualised. Notice too that the shares peaked in May 2024, well before silver did, and are 23% below that high.

What the price is not reflecting is the quieter half of the story: a bottom-quartile zinc producer that has finished an expensive underground conversion and is now running at its lowest costs in years, with a metal at $3,466 a tonne. If silver settles anywhere near current levels rather than collapsing, the base business alone supports a considerably better outcome than the share price implies.

The honest read

Separate the two businesses before deciding anything. Value the zinc and lead operation on cost-curve and volume logic, at a metal price you would underwrite through a cycle. Treat silver as a marked-to-market option whose contribution should be haircut heavily, not extrapolated. And hold the government's 29.54% stake sale as a permanent feature of the chart rather than a surprise: it will most likely be attempted when the numbers look best, which is precisely when the shares would otherwise be strongest.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. What is zinc actually for?

Coating steel. The zinc corrodes in place of the steel, which is why zinc demand is largely galvanised-steel demand.

2. Why does silver appear in a zinc primer?

It occurs in the same orebody and is recovered from the lead circuit — at near-zero marginal cost, because the rock was mined anyway.

3. What did Hindustan Zinc report in Q1 FY27?

Revenue ₹13,747 cr (+77%), EBITDA ₹8,074 cr (+109%), profit ₹5,469 cr (+145%), and a 52% operating margin against 38%.

4. Why is the stock flat despite that?

Silver averaged $73.2 an ounce in the quarter after peaking at $115.5 in January and falling to $67.1 by September; the market will not capitalise a spike.

5. Did volumes grow?

No — saleable silver was flat at 149 tonnes. The profit came from price.

With the residual Vedanta entity, which retains roughly 63.4% of it, rather than with any of the newly listed companies.

9. Why is the underground transition significant?

Underground mining costs more per tonne, so unit costs rose through the conversion; this quarter's costs were the lowest since it began.

Where the numbers come from

  • Hindustan Zinc: Q1 FY27 slides — record profit and lowest costs; revenue, EBITDA and the silver contribution; profit and the ₹11 dividend; production volumes, including flat silver and lead; the earnings call.
