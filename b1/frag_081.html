Metal Fabrication & Engineering

Fabrication is what happens to steel after the mill and before the machine: a plate is cut, a section is bent, two pieces are welded, the joint is ground and painted, and the result is a factory shed, a bus body, a motorcycle exhaust, a storage tank or an aircraft bracket. It is the least glamorous industry in the Engineering family and the one that contains the widest spread of outcomes. On their latest reported numbers, Omnitech Engineering earns a 29% operating margin and SM Auto Stamping earns 3.1% — both classified as metal fabricators, both cutting and joining steel. This primer explains what separates them, why India's three listed pre-engineered building makers all fell in 2026 while its two listed precision engineers doubled, and why a four-year high in steel prices in the week this data ends is not good news for anyone on this list.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

One skill, two opposite contracts

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Imagine two people who both make things out of cardboard. The first one says to a customer: "Tell me what box you want, and I'll tell you today what it will cost to build it by next winter." The second one says: "Test me for two years. If you decide my folding is good enough for your rocket, then we'll talk about price." Both of them cut, fold and glue. But if cardboard suddenly gets expensive, the first one has already promised a price and must pay the difference out of his own pocket, while the second one is one of only three people in the world allowed to fold for rockets, so the customer pays. Metal fabrication is exactly this. Cutting and welding steel is a skill almost anyone can learn — India has more than five thousand firms doing it — so the money is never in the welding. It is in whether you promised a price before you bought the steel.

Say it to a client in one minute

Twenty-three names, eight with enough history for a basket, and the widest internal spread of any section so far.

  • The precision engineers — Sansera (~₹24,775 cr) and MTAR (~₹20,915 cr): up 154% and 198% in 2026. Sansera's aerospace, defence and semiconductor backlog is about ₹5,750 cr; MTAR's order book is ₹5,143 cr after a record ₹2,895 cr of inflows in one quarter.
  • The pre-engineered building makers — Interarch, Epack Prefab, M&B Engineering: down 26%, 17% and 34%, all three peaking within a week of each other in November 2025. Revenue is growing 16–25%; margins are 8–9% and falling.
  • The auto sheet-metal shops — Automobile Corporation of Goa, Munjal Auto, Autoline, SM Auto Stamping: ₹250–700 cr of quarterly revenue at 3–7% margins, and in ACGL's case 93.7% of revenue from a single customer.
  • The structural and process fabricators — Salasar, Artson, Rishi Laser: the hardest place to be. Salasar's revenue grew 24% over two years while profit fell two-thirds; Artson's net worth is about ₹1 cr.
  • The drivers: steel prices and the lag before they can be passed on, India's private capital-expenditure cycle, data centres and semiconductor fabs, nuclear policy, and aerospace qualification.
  • The calendar: April has beaten the Nifty in 14 of 15 years; March in only 2 of 15.

The table is deliberately sorted by contract rather than by product, because in this industry the contract explains more than the product does. Read down the "who sets the price" column and the margin column moves with it almost perfectly. Where the customer fixes a price before the supplier buys its steel — PEB, towers, tanks, stampings — margins sit between 3% and 11%. Where the customer must first spend two or three years qualifying the supplier, and only then negotiates — nuclear assemblies, fuel-cell parts, aerospace machining — margins sit between 19% and 29%. Nothing about the welding is different. The critical variable is the order in which price and approval are settled.

The chain in one breath

  • Buy steel at a price nobody can predict three months out.
  • Draw the job so it uses as few tonnes as possible.
  • Cut and bend the plate, then weld it.
  • Machine it, if the customer's drawing calls for microns rather than millimetres.
  • Coat it so it survives twenty years outdoors.
  • Prove it — and then wait to be paid.
Fund-manager lens

Castings (078) set the Engineering family's first test — who writes the specification, and does the part wear out? Forgings (080) added a second — margin follows the weight class and the depth of machining, not the tonnes. Fabrication adds a third, and it is the bluntest of the three: who carries the price of steel between the quote and the delivery? Both 078's and 080's tests still apply here, and they point the same way. A pre-engineered building is made to the customer's design, from steel the customer's tender priced, and it does not wear out. A nuclear grid plate is made to a specification the customer spent three years approving, and a failure is unthinkable. That is the whole difference between an 8% business and a 24% business in one section of the watchlist.

The audit

Eight of 23 names form the basket: Sansera Engineering, MTAR Technologies, Automobile Corporation of Goa, Munjal Auto, Salasar Techno Engineering, Artson, Birla Precision and Autoline Industries, equal-weighted monthly from July 2011. Members join as they list: from 2011 the basket is five names, Salasar joins in 2017, and MTAR and Sansera in 2021. Read the pre-2017 years as a micro-cap sheet-metal basket and the post-2021 years as the section it has become. Three NSE series that began only in 2026 were repaired with BSE history back to 2006, scaled at the first overlapping day: Automobile Corporation of Goa (×0.992), IST Ltd (×0.998) and Birla Precision (×1.023). Artson trades on the BSE and its own history runs from 2005.

Four of the five largest names are excluded for being too new, and they are 85% of the section's market value. Aequs listed in December 2025, Omnitech in March 2026, Epack Prefab in October 2025, M&B Engineering in August 2025 and Interarch in August 2024. Together with the four basket newcomers this is the most recently listed section in the project so far: of about ₹79,500 cr of market value in these 23 names, only ₹5,400 cr — under 7% — belongs to companies with a listed price before 2021. Any statement about how "metal fabrication stocks" behaved before 2021 is a statement about micro caps.

IST Ltd is excluded as misclassified, and the exclusion changes a headline. Its June-quarter operating profit was ₹16 cr on ₹35 cr of revenue, while other income was ₹87 cr; in FY26 other income of ₹127 cr exceeded operating profit of ₹81 cr, and the balance sheet holds ₹1,449 cr of investments against a ₹746 cr market value. It is stated here rather than used, because the extra year comes from a stock whose profit has almost nothing to do with fabrication.

Three names are misclassified in the source file. Brady & Morris makes material-handling equipment and textile machinery, not fabricated steel, and belongs with Material Handling Equipment. Ameya Precision makes pump and valve components and belongs with pumps or valves. Misquita Engineering, a machining job shop, is filed under electronics distribution. All three are worth reclassifying. Separately, Omax Autos, a sheet-metal and chassis-frame maker, sits in Forgings in the source file and was flagged in 080 as belonging here.

Two same-group traps, both checked by ISIN. Profit that is not operations, four times over. IST Ltd, above, is the extreme case. The levers

  1. The steel lag. Steel is 55–70% of revenue here, the highest share in the Engineering family. A PEB or tower contract fixes the price at tender and the steel is bought over the following six to twelve months. When hot-rolled coil rises ₹5,000 a tonne in a month, as it did into September 2026, the fabricator absorbs it. When steel falls, the same asymmetry works in its favour for two quarters.
  2. Design efficiency. A PEB is priced per square foot but costed per tonne, so a design that uses 2 kg less steel per square foot is pure margin. This is the one genuinely proprietary skill on the fabrication side, and it is why Interarch's margins, even at 8.6%, sit above the industry's mid-single digits.
  3. Approval before price. The entire difference between 29% and 3%. A nuclear grid plate or a fuel-cell hot box is qualified over years; a stamped bracket is re-quoted every year with an expected price reduction built in.
  4. Customer concentration. ACGL takes 93.7% of revenue from Tata Motors; MTAR took more than 60% of FY25 revenue from Bloom Energy; Munjal Auto is built around Hero MotoCorp. Concentration is not automatically bad — MTAR's dependence is the reason it rose 198% — but it removes the ability to say no on price.
  5. Working capital and interest. Retention money, defect-liability periods and 200-day receivables are normal. Autoline's interest took 61% of operating profit in the June quarter; Salasar's ₹15.2 cr of quarterly interest against ₹21.9 cr of operating profit is the same story.
  6. Utilisation and overhead timing. Interarch's capacity is rising from 221,000 t to 336,000 t while utilisation runs at 80–88%; new sheds carry depreciation and overhead before they carry revenue. This is the stated reason its margin fell.
  7. Other income. In an industry earning 5% on operations, treasury and rental income routinely decide the sign of the bottom line. It flatters Birla Precision and Munjal Auto and, when it fell, flattened Interarch.
The test to apply

Ask of any company in this section one question: was the price agreed before or after the customer decided you were the only one who could do it? If before — a tender, a reverse auction, an annual price letter — the business earns 3% to 11% and will keep earning it however fast revenue grows. Salasar proves the point in the cleanest possible way: revenue up 24% in two years, profit down two-thirds. If after — a nuclear qualification, an AS9100 approval, a fifteen-year landing-gear agreement — the business earns 19% to 29%, and the approval is worth more than the plant. Growth in the first kind of business is a liability funded by debt; growth in the second is an annuity.

7 · The business calendar

The client's capital budget, and the fabricator's own year-end

  • April: Indian corporate capital budgets open, vehicle makers issue annual schedules and price letters, and steel contracts reset. April has beaten the Nifty in 14 of 15 years.
  • April–June: the strongest execution quarter for structural work before the monsoon; site clearance and erection run at full speed. April–July beat the Nifty in 12 of 15 years, median +15.1 points.
  • July–September: the monsoon stops site erection and pushes revenue recognition into the second half. Interarch blamed its June-quarter margin on exactly this. Order intake continues; revenue does not.
  • October–December: post-monsoon execution catches up and steel demand and prices usually firm. The basket's December has beaten the Nifty in 9 of 15 years, but October–December as a whole has beaten it in only 6 of 14, with a median of −4.8 points.
  • January–March: the fiscal year-end, when cost overruns are recognised, provisions are taken on jobs quoted a year earlier, and steel losses are booked. March has beaten the Nifty in only 2 of 15 years and January–March has a median of −13.8 points — the weakest first quarter of any multi-company basket in this project except salt (015).

9 · What moves margins

Nine drivers

  1. The steel price, and the lag. The single largest driver. Hot-rolled coil at a four-year high in September 2026 is a margin event for every fixed-price contract signed in the previous year.
  2. India's private capital-expenditure cycle. Factories, warehouses, data centres and fabs are the order book. The structural fabrication market is about $11.8 bn in 2026, growing 7.35% a year to 2031.
  3. Vehicle volumes. Two-wheelers for Munjal Auto, buses for ACGL, commercial vehicles and cars for Autoline, tractors for Pritika. See 017–020 and 023.
  4. Qualification wins. Every new approval — nuclear, aerospace, semiconductor equipment — permanently moves a company's margin bracket. Sansera's ADS backlog rose from ₹4,437 cr to about ₹5,750 cr on one order.
  5. Utilisation against new capacity. Interarch's expansion to 336,000 t is the clearest current case of overhead arriving before revenue.
  6. Interest and working capital. Retention money, long receivables and project finance. Autoline pays away 61% of operating profit in interest; Salasar pays 69%.
  7. Tender intensity. Reverse auctions for utility and telecom structures compress margins to mid single digits regardless of demand.
  8. Other income. Treasury and rental income, which in a 5% business decides the sign of the result.
  9. Export tariffs and the rupee. US Section 232 duties of 50% on steel and aluminium, 25% on many derivatives, and a 50% country tariff on Indian goods since August 2025, against Indian export exposure of about $5 bn a year in steel and aluminium derivatives.

The anatomy of the cycles

  1. The capex winter (2012–2013): the basket lagged a rising market in 2012 and fell 24% in 2013 as India's investment cycle stalled and the rupee broke.
  2. The election re-rating (2014–2015): +84% in 2014 on the promise of an industrial revival, most of which did not arrive; 2015 was flat.
  3. Demonetisation, GST and the small-cap boom (2016–2017): nothing in 2016, then +135% in 2017 — the second-best year — as GST pushed work from unregistered job shops to organised fabricators and micro caps re-rated.
  4. The double break (2018–2019): the small-cap unwind and the IL&FS credit squeeze, then the vehicle slump. −27% and then −41%, a 57% fall over two years against a Nifty that rose 15%.
  5. Rebound and mania (2020–2021): +30% then +140%, the best year in the record, on the capital-expenditure rebound and a micro-cap melt-up that took Birla Precision up 739%.
  6. The steel shock and the split (2022–2026): flat in 2022 as steel prices destroyed fixed-price contracts; +58% in 2023 as capital expenditure turned; then a section that stopped moving together — precision engineering up, pre-engineered buildings down.

Patterns that repeat

  • This is a credit-cycle basket dressed as an industrial one. Its three worst years — 2013, 2018, 2019 — are the three worst years for Indian industrial credit. Working capital, not welding, is the binding constraint.
  • Rallies are violent and fully given back. +135% in 2017 was followed by −27% and −41%. +140% in 2021 was followed by flat, and Birla Precision is still 55% below a high set in January 2008.
  • The micro caps carry the average in both directions. Birla Precision has managed +245%, +739%, −50% and −57% in this record. Its median annual move is far larger than its business ever justified.
  • Steel price spikes show up with a one-quarter lag. 2022 is the clearest instance: revenue rose everywhere, margins did not.
  • The three PEB names now move as one. All three peaked within a week in November 2025 and all three fell 17–51% from those highs. That is a multiple reset, not three company stories.

Why structural fabrication does not globalise

Heavy fabricated steel is expensive to move and cheap to make. A shipping container of welded structures is mostly air and freight, which is why structural fabrication is a regional business almost everywhere and why India's market is served by Indian firms even without tariffs. Precision machining is the opposite: a crate of machined valve trim or fuel-cell parts is worth a hundred times its freight, so it is globally contested and globally tariffed. This single physical fact explains why the low-margin half of the section is safe from imports and the high-margin half is not. It also explains the 2026 tariff pattern: the businesses exposed to US duties are precisely the ones earning 19–29%, while the businesses earning 5% face no import threat and no export opportunity either.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • From site-built concrete to factory-built steel (2000s–2020s): pre-engineered buildings replaced reinforced concrete for factories and warehouses because they go up in weeks rather than months.
  • From unregistered job shops to organised fabricators (2017–): the goods and services tax made invoicing unavoidable and shifted share to companies that could issue one.
  • From captive shops to specialists (2010s): vehicle and equipment makers closed in-house press and weld shops.
  • From build-to-print to qualified supply (2020s): the shift that separated this section in two — Indian machinists winning aerospace, nuclear and semiconductor approvals rather than quoting on drawings.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. CNC cutting over manual and oxy cutting. The fastest-growing process step in Indian fabrication, at about 8.6% a year, because it cuts scrap on a material that is two-thirds of cost.
  2. Robotic and automated welding. Removes the certified-welder bottleneck for repetitive joints and improves the consistency that pressure-vessel and nuclear work demands.
  3. Five-axis machining and in-process metrology. The technical gate to aerospace and semiconductor work, and the reason India's machine-tool import bill matters more than its steel bill at the top of the section.
  4. Design-led steel reduction. Optimisation software that takes kilograms per square foot out of a PEB is the only real intellectual property on the fabrication side.
  5. Modular and off-site construction. Custom plate-worked modules are the fastest-growing product category, at about 8.7% a year, as data centres and process plants move fabrication off site.
  6. Additive and hybrid manufacturing. Still marginal in India, but it is how aerospace and turbine work will be re-specified over the next decade — a threat to any machinist whose advantage is machining time rather than approval.

What is priced in

For MTAR, the whole of the next decade's nuclear and fuel-cell demand. At ₹7,202 the stock trades at roughly 160 times trailing earnings. The order book is real, the inflow of ₹2,895 cr in one quarter is real, and the FY27 guidance of 80% revenue growth at a 22–24% margin is specific enough to be checked. But the largest customer is a single American fuel-cell company whose own demand rests on AI data-centre construction, and the nuclear orders execute over five years and more. The stock is 14% off its June high already.

For Sansera, a mix shift that is real but eight times ahead of itself. ADS revenue of ₹145 cr a quarter sits against a backlog of about ₹5,750 cr executable to FY31, and the semiconductor order does not produce meaningful revenue until calendar 2027. At roughly 70 times earnings the price assumes the conversion happens on schedule and the auto business holds its 19% margin while it does.

For the pre-engineered building makers, the de-rating has done most of the work. Interarch at about 21 times earnings with a 23.7% return on capital, no debt and a ₹1,864 cr order book is a different proposition from Interarch at ₹2,623 in November 2025. The question is not demand — data centres, fabs and warehouses are as good an order book as this industry has ever had — but whether an 8.6% margin is the bottom or the new normal. With steel at a four-year high, the next two quarters will answer it.

For the bottom half, the market is right. Salasar at 2.1% return on equity after buying a bankrupt EPC business, Artson with ₹1 cr of net worth, Autoline with debt equal to its market value, Ahlada with revenue down 61% in two years: these are not cheap industrial assets waiting for a cycle. They are businesses whose growth has consumed capital, and the share prices say so.

The honest read

This section is where the Engineering family's three tests meet and give the clearest answer yet. Treat it as two industries and never own the average. Precision and safety-critical engineering — Sansera, MTAR, Omnitech, Aequs, Ameya — is a business with a real, checkable barrier in the form of customer qualification, and it is now priced as if delivery is certain. Fabrication proper — pre-engineered buildings, towers, tanks, stampings — is a business with no barrier, a 55–70% input it cannot price, and a customer who runs a tender; its good years come from capital-expenditure re-ratings rather than earnings, and they are given back. Within it, only design-led PEB has a defensible edge, and the current margin compression is the test of whether that edge survives ₹63,900 steel. The basket's own history is the warning: +135% and +140% in its two best years, and −57% across the two years that followed the first of them.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What share of your order book is on fixed prices, and what steel price was assumed when it was quoted?
  2. How many months pass between winning an order and buying the steel for it, and do you hedge or pre-buy?
  3. What is your realised price per tonne and your steel consumption per square foot, and how have both moved this year?
  4. How much of this quarter's profit is other income, and what generates it?
  5. What is your utilisation by plant, and how much overhead and depreciation from new capacity is already in the cost base but not yet in revenue?
  6. What is the cash conversion cycle, how much is retention money, and when is it released?
  7. For the precision names: how many new customer qualifications did you complete this year, how long did each take, and what margin does approved work earn against build-to-print work?
  8. What share of revenue comes from your largest customer, and what happens to fixed-cost absorption if that customer halves its offtake?
  9. For MTAR specifically: what is the split of the order book between nuclear, clean energy, space and defence, and what is the delivery schedule for each?
  10. For Sansera: what is the execution profile of the ₹5,750 cr ADS backlog by year, and what capex does it require?

  12. For the leveraged names: what is the plan for debt, and at what interest cover do lenders reset terms?

19 · Glossary

Every term you'll meet

Process

Fabrication
Cutting, forming and joining metal into a finished structure or assembly — as opposed to casting or forging it into shape.
Laser cutting
Cutting sheet and plate with a focused laser beam; precise, fast and the standard for thin material.
Plasma cutting
Cutting conductive metal with an ionised gas jet; cheaper than laser on thick plate.
Press brake
A machine that bends sheet metal along a straight line between a punch and a die.
Stamping
Pressing sheet metal between dies to cut and form a part in one or more strokes.
Deep drawing
Stretching sheet metal into a deep hollow shape, such as a fuel tank or a washing-machine drum.
Roll forming
Passing strip through successive rollers to make a constant profile, such as a purlin or a roof sheet.
Submerged-arc welding
High-deposition welding under a blanket of flux; the workhorse of heavy structural fabrication.
MIG and TIG welding
Gas-shielded arc welding; MIG for speed on structures, TIG for precision and thin or exotic metals.
Weld procedure specification
The documented recipe — material, wire, current, preheat — that a certified weld must follow.
Welder qualification
A certified test proving a named welder can make a specified joint; required for pressure, nuclear and aerospace work.
Radiography
X-raying a weld to find internal defects without cutting it open.
Non-destructive testing
Any inspection that checks a part without damaging it — radiography, ultrasonics, dye penetrant.
Shot blasting
Cleaning steel with high-velocity abrasive before painting or galvanising.
Hot-dip galvanising
Dipping fabricated steel in molten zinc to protect it outdoors for decades.
CNC machining
Computer-controlled cutting of metal to programmed dimensions.
Five-axis machining
Machining with the tool able to approach the part from five directions at once; the technical gate to complex aerospace parts.
Flow forming
Forming a rotating cylindrical part by pressing rollers against it; used for rocket and missile casings.
Nesting
Arranging cutting patterns on a plate so that the least material is wasted.
Jig and fixture
The purpose-built tooling that holds a part in position while it is welded or machined.

Products and structures

Pre-engineered building (PEB)
A steel building designed, fabricated and pre-drilled in a factory and bolted together on site.
Primary and secondary framing
The main columns and rafters of a PEB, and the purlins and girts that span between them.
Purlin
A horizontal member carrying the roof sheeting between rafters.
Girt
The horizontal member carrying the wall sheeting between columns.
Built-up section
A beam welded from three plates rather than rolled in one piece, so its depth can vary along its length.
Column-free span
The clear width a building achieves without internal supports — the reason data centres and warehouses use steel.
Self-supported roofing
A curved steel roof that spans without a supporting frame.
Monopole
A single tubular tower used for telecom or lighting where a lattice tower will not fit.
Lattice tower
A bolted framework of galvanised angles used for transmission and telecom.
Pipe spool
A prefabricated assembly of pipe, elbows and flanges made in a shop and lifted into a plant.
Pressure vessel
A tank designed and certified to hold contents above atmospheric pressure.
Body-in-white
The welded sheet-metal shell of a vehicle before paint and trim.
Bus body
The passenger structure built onto a chassis supplied by the vehicle maker.
Hot box
The insulated high-temperature assembly at the core of a solid-oxide fuel cell.
Grid plate
A precision-machined nuclear reactor component that positions and supports fuel assemblies.
Tool holder
The precision adaptor that grips a cutting tool in a machine spindle; AT3 is a high accuracy class.
Valve trim
The internal parts of a valve that control flow — plug, seat, stem and cage.

Business and contracts

Job work
Fabricating to a customer's drawing on a per-part basis with no long-term contract.
Build to print
Making exactly what the customer's drawing specifies, without owning the design.
Fixed-price contract
A contract where the price is agreed before the cost is known; the defining risk of this industry.
Price variation clause
A contract term that adjusts the price for movements in a named input, usually steel; its presence or absence decides the margin.
Reverse auction
A tender in which suppliers bid the price down against each other in real time.
Retention money
A share of the contract value held back by the customer until the defect-liability period ends.
Defect liability period
The months after handover during which the fabricator must fix faults at its own cost.
Order book
Contracted work not yet executed; the sector's main forward indicator.
Unexecuted backlog
The portion of the order book still to be delivered, usually quoted with an execution period.
First article inspection
The detailed approval of the first part off a new process before series supply begins.
Qualification
The multi-year process by which a customer approves a supplier for safety-critical work; the highest barrier to entry here.
AS9100
The aerospace quality-management standard.
ASME stamp
American Society of Mechanical Engineers certification allowing a shop to build coded pressure vessels.
IATF 16949
The automotive quality-management standard.
Safeguard duty
A temporary import duty to protect a domestic industry from a surge of imports; India levies one on flat steel.
Section 232
The US law used to impose tariffs on national-security grounds, including on steel, aluminium and their derivative products.
Derivative product
A finished good tariffed on the steel or aluminium it contains rather than on the good itself.
Special economic zone
A designated area with customs and tax concessions for exporters; Aequs's Belagavi aerospace cluster is one.
SME platform
The exchanges' board for small and medium enterprises, with lighter listing and reporting rules.
Other income
Income from outside operations — interest, dividends, rent, asset sales — which in a 5% margin industry frequently decides the bottom line.
Cash conversion cycle
The days between paying for material and being paid for the finished job.
Interest cover
Operating profit divided by interest; below about two, the lender effectively owns the growth.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. Why does one company in this section earn 29% and another 3.1%?

Because of when the price is fixed. Omnitech is qualified for safety-critical work and negotiates afterwards; SM Auto Stamping quotes a price on a drawing that hundreds of shops could make.

2. Why did the three pre-engineered building stocks fall in 2026 while revenue grew?

Margins fell — Interarch from 10.5% to 8.6%, Epack from 10.5% to 9.3%, M&B to 7.9% — as steel rose against fixed-price contracts and new capacity added overhead before revenue. All three had peaked within a week in November 2025.

3. What is the significance of ₹63,900 a tonne?

Mumbai hot-rolled coil in the week to 18 September 2026 — a four-year high, about ₹5,000 above a month earlier, and the input cost of every company in this section.

4. Why is Salasar's revenue growth not good news?

Revenue rose 24% over two years to ₹1,503 cr while profit fell from ₹53 cr to ₹18 cr, after it bought a bankrupt EPC company out of liquidation. Return on equity is 2.13% and interest takes about 69% of operating profit.

5. What actually drives MTAR's share price?

Two things: India's nuclear opening under the SHANTI Act, which produced a ₹504 cr Kaiga order, and Bloom Energy's fuel-cell demand from American AI data centres, which was more than 60% of MTAR's FY25 revenue.

6. Why is IST Ltd excluded from the basket?

Its June-quarter other income was ₹87 cr against ₹35 cr of revenue, and it holds ₹1,449 cr of investments against a ₹746 cr market value. It is an investment and property company, not a fabricator — and including it would have made April read 15 of 15 instead of 14 of 15.

7. What is the strongest and weakest calendar signal here?

April has beaten the Nifty in 14 of 15 years, failing only in 2019. March has beaten it in 2 of 15, and January–March has a median of −13.8 points.

8. Why does structural fabrication stay local while precision machining does not?

Fabricated structures are bulky and cheap, so freight kills the trade; machined components are small and valuable, so they are globally sourced — and globally tariffed.

9. What relationship should you check before writing about Pritika Engineering Components?

It is a 70.8%-owned subsidiary of Pritika Auto Industries, which the watchlist files under Castings (078). The ISINs are INE0MJQ01020 and INE583R01029.

10. How much of this section's market value has a price history before 2021?

About ₹5,400 cr of roughly ₹79,500 cr — under 7%. Four of the five largest names listed between August 2024 and March 2026, so any long-run statistic here describes micro caps.
