Switchgear & Grid Equipment

The equipment that lets a grid be switched, protected and controlled: circuit breakers that isolate faults in milliseconds, gas-insulated switchgear that fits a substation into a building, the power electronics that convert between AC and DC, and — at the small end — the switches and meters in every home. As India moves renewable power across the country and data centres multiply worldwide, this is where the technology content of the grid lives, and it is dominated by two companies with global parents.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

The grid's control system, owned largely by global engineering groups

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Think of the electricity grid as a gigantic set of water pipes. You need taps to turn the flow on and off, safety valves that snap shut if a pipe bursts, and meters that measure how much each house uses. Switchgear is the electrical version: breakers that cut the power in a fraction of a second when something goes wrong, so a fault in one place does not black out a whole city. Some of this equipment is simple, like the switches on your wall. Some is very advanced — machines that turn alternating current into direct current so electricity can travel a thousand kilometres with little loss. India is building a lot of new grid, and the world is short of this equipment, so the companies that can make the advanced kind are extremely busy.

Say it to a client in one minute

A two-speed sector: advanced grid technology is booming, commodity electrical products are squeezed.

  • Hitachi Energy India: revenue +68.6% to ₹2,494 cr, profit more than doubled to ₹294 cr, EBITDA margin +450 bps, record backlog ₹32,222 cr, and 33.6% of non-HVDC orders from exports. Data centres led its ordering.
  • GE Vernova T&D India: revenue +38% to ₹1,836 cr, profit +25% to ₹363 cr at a 25.1% EBITDA margin — but order inflows fell 30% and the backlog slipped to about ₹20,930 cr.
  • HVDC is the structural driver: 8–9 HVDC links planned through FY32 at about ₹1.9 lakh cr, including a ±800 kV, 6,000 MW Khavda–Nagpur link won by a Hitachi Energy–BHEL consortium.
  • Domestic small caps are being squeezed: HPL Electric's revenue rose 35% but its margin fell from 15.1% to 12.3% on metal and plastics costs; its stock is −30%.
  • Returns follow technology: Hitachi +79% and GE Vernova T&D +40% against HPL −30% and Salzer −20% in the same year.

Analogy

If transformers (see 042) are the grid's gearboxes, switchgear is its brakes, fuses and dashboard. A car can have a powerful engine, but without brakes that work in milliseconds it cannot be driven safely. As the grid carries more power over longer distances and from more intermittent sources, the brakes and the dashboard become more sophisticated — and more valuable per unit of capacity.

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Technology: the hardest designs belong to a few big international companies, which share them with their Indian branches.
  • Components: special chips, gases, copper and steel boxes are bought in.
  • Factories: Indian factories build the equipment — for Indian projects and, more and more, for customers abroad.
  • Integration: engineers make all the pieces of a substation or converter station work as one system.
  • Customers: power companies, solar farms, factories and data centres.
  • Service: the equipment lasts decades and needs looking after.
Fund-manager lens

The dividing line in this section is technology ownership. The two large companies are Indian arms of global groups — Hitachi Energy and GE Vernova — with access to HVDC, GIS and protection technology that very few firms possess, and with parents that increasingly source from India for export. That gives them pricing power in a global shortage and explains margins in the mid-20s. The domestic small caps make lower-technology products — wiring devices, low-voltage switchgear, meters — where competition is intense and input costs pass straight into margins, as HPL's 290 basis-point decline shows. Watch the order intake as well as the backlog: GE Vernova T&D's 30% fall in inflows is the first sign in this section that the ordering peak may have passed for some product lines.

Read the section in two halves

Two companies — Hitachi Energy India and GE Vernova T&D India — make up more than 95% of the section's market value and nearly all of its technology. The rest are domestic makers of lower-technology electrical products whose economics resemble consumer electricals more than grid infrastructure. An equal-weighted basket gives the small caps most of the weight, which is why it shows +12% while the two giants are up 79% and 40%. Several small caps also peaked in mid-to-late 2024 and are 58–83% below those levels.

Why the giants and the small caps diverged in 2026

The two multinational arms sell technology that few can supply, into a market where demand from grids and data centres exceeds global capacity. That lets them hold prices while their costs rise, and their Indian factories benefit twice: from domestic HVDC and substation orders, and from parents routing export orders to lower-cost Indian plants. Hitachi Energy India's EBITDA margin rose 450 basis points in the quarter. The domestic small caps sell products where many competitors exist and customers are price-sensitive, so rising copper, steel and plastics costs cannot be passed on quickly — HPL's revenue grew 35% while EBITDA grew 9%. The same grid cycle, in other words, produces very different outcomes depending on where a company sits on the technology ladder.

Analogy

It is the difference between a company that makes jet engines and one that makes seat belts for the same aircraft. Both are needed; both see more orders when airlines expand. Only one can raise prices when its costs go up.

7 · The business calendar

Orders in spring, execution through the year

  • April–June: new financial year budgets release orders from utilities and developers; the strongest months for these stocks.
  • July–September: execution continues; monsoon affects site work but not factory output.
  • October–December: commissioning season for substations; mixed for the stocks.
  • January–March: year-end dispatch pushes; historically weak share prices.
  • Parent reporting cycles: Hitachi and GE Vernova's global results and outlooks move the Indian arms, independently of the Indian calendar.

The exceptions, and why

How to use this

The record is only ten years long and the basket is dominated by small caps, so the pattern is suggestive rather than reliable. The larger point is that the two stocks that matter for most portfolios — Hitachi Energy India and GE Vernova T&D India — move with their global parents and order announcements more than with any Indian season. For them, the useful calendar is the parents' results and the award dates of HVDC projects.

The anatomy of this cycle

  1. A lean decade (2016–2020): thin utility ordering, losses at GE's Indian arm and a collapse in small-cap valuations — four negative years in five.
  2. The grid shortage (2021–2024): HVDC ordering, renewable integration and data centres coincided with global capacity shortages; the basket rose +103%, +20%, +196% and +204%.
  3. The split (2025–2026): the technology leaders kept rising on record backlogs and exports, while the domestic small caps de-rated as costs rose.

Patterns that repeat

  • Global parentage is a durable advantage: technology access and export sourcing gave the multinational arms both growth and margin.
  • Small caps overshoot in both directions: S&S +712% in 2023; Kaycee and Shivalic are each 83% below their peaks.
  • Order intake turns before revenue: backlogs sustain revenue for years after inflows slow, so falling inflows are the early signal.
  • Input costs separate the two halves in every year that metal prices rise.

11 · The global picture

A handful of global suppliers, and India as their factory

High-voltage grid technology is one of the most concentrated industries in the world. HVDC converter stations are supplied by a small group — Hitachi Energy, Siemens Energy and GE Vernova among the leaders — and the same firms dominate gas-insulated switchgear and advanced protection. Demand from grid reinforcement, offshore wind, cross-border interconnectors and data centres has outrun their global capacity, with order books stretching years ahead. That has made India valuable to them in two ways: as a large domestic market with its own HVDC programme, and as a cost-competitive manufacturing base for exports. Hitachi Energy India's export share — a third of non-HVDC orders, from Europe, North America and South Asia — and its 20th factory are the clearest evidence of that shift.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • From ABB and Alstom to Hitachi and GE Vernova: corporate reorganisations moved the Indian grid businesses into new parents, which then made India an export base.
  • From AC to HVDC for long distances: renewable zones far from demand made HVDC central to the grid plan.
  • From domestic supply to export hub: the global shortage turned Indian factories into suppliers to Europe and North America.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. VSC-HVDC: voltage-source converter technology, better for connecting renewables and weak grids.
  2. Higher HVDC voltages: ±800 kV links like Khavda–Nagpur carrying 6,000 MW.
  3. SF6-free switchgear: alternatives to a potent greenhouse gas, driven by regulation in Europe.
  4. Digital substations: fibre-optic process buses and software-based protection.
  5. Grid-forming inverters and power quality: keeping grids stable as synchronous generation declines (see 038).
  6. Smart metering and advanced metering infrastructure: the digitisation of distribution (see 039).

The honest read

This section is two industries that happen to share a name. The high-technology half — HVDC, gas-insulated switchgear, grid integration — is in a global shortage, has pricing power, and is becoming an export business run from Indian factories for global parents; Hitachi Energy India's results are about as strong as industrial results get. The low-technology half is a competitive electrical-products business whose margins fall whenever copper and plastics rise. The main risk for the leaders is not demand but valuation and the ordering cycle: GE Vernova T&D's inflows fell 30% this quarter, which may be lumpy timing or an early sign that the post-2023 ordering wave is maturing. A record backlog sustains revenue for years, so the time to worry is when inflows fall for several consecutive quarters — something worth tracking closely from here.

Concall checklist

  1. What is the mix of HVDC, high-voltage, service and export in the backlog?

Red flags

  • Several consecutive quarters of falling order inflows.
  • Rising royalty payments to the parent outpacing profit.
  • Margin decline despite revenue growth at the small caps.
  • Large receivables on government metering contracts.

19 · Glossary

Every term you'll meet

Equipment

Switchgear
The switches, fuses and circuit breakers that control, protect and isolate electrical equipment.
Circuit breaker
A device that automatically interrupts current when a fault is detected.
Gas-insulated switchgear (GIS)
Compact high-voltage switchgear sealed in insulating gas; used where space is scarce.
Air-insulated switchgear (AIS)
Outdoor switchgear insulated by air; cheaper but needs more space.
SF6
Sulphur hexafluoride, the insulating gas used in most GIS; a potent greenhouse gas.
Converter station
The facility at each end of an HVDC link that converts between AC and DC.
LCC and VSC
Line-commutated and voltage-source converter technologies; VSC suits renewables and weak grids.
IGBT
Insulated-gate bipolar transistor, the power semiconductor at the heart of VSC converters.
Bi-pole
An HVDC configuration with two conductors of opposite polarity, such as ±800 kV.
Protection relay
A device that senses abnormal conditions and trips breakers.
Digital substation
A substation using fibre-optic communication and software-based protection.
Reactive power compensation
Equipment such as STATCOMs that stabilises voltage on the grid.
STATCOM
A static synchronous compensator for voltage and power-quality control.
Miniature circuit breaker (MCB)
A low-voltage breaker used in homes and buildings.
Distribution board
The panel housing breakers in a building.
Advanced metering infrastructure (AMI)
The full system of smart meters, communications and data management.
Vacuum circuit breaker (VCB)
A medium-voltage breaker that interrupts the arc inside a vacuum bottle; the workhorse of distribution switchgear.
Moulded case circuit breaker (MCCB)
A larger low-voltage breaker for commercial and industrial feeders.
Residual current device (RCCB)
A breaker that trips on earth leakage to protect people from shock.
Disconnector (isolator)
A switch that visibly separates equipment for maintenance; it is not designed to break fault current.
Earthing switch
Connects isolated equipment to earth so it is safe to work on.
Ring main unit (RMU)
A compact, sealed medium-voltage switchgear unit used in city distribution networks.
Busbar
The thick conductor inside switchgear and substations that distributes power between circuits.
Instrument transformer
Current and voltage transformers that step values down so meters and relays can measure them.
Surge arrester
Diverts lightning and switching surges to earth to protect equipment.
Breaking capacity
The largest fault current a breaker can safely interrupt, in kiloamperes.
Fault clearing time
How quickly protection detects and removes a fault, typically a few cycles — tens of milliseconds.
Arc
The plasma that forms when current is interrupted; quenching it is the core engineering problem in a breaker.
Thyristor valve
The switching building block of classic LCC HVDC converters.
Back-to-back and multi-terminal HVDC
A converter pair at one site linking two unsynchronised grids, versus a DC network with three or more converter stations.
FACTS
Flexible AC transmission systems — SVCs, STATCOMs and series compensation that raise the capacity and stability of AC lines.
Synchronous condenser
A spinning machine that supplies inertia and reactive power without generating energy, increasingly used where renewables dominate.
Contactor and control relay
Industrial switching devices for motors and machines — Salzer's and Kaycee's core products.
Reed switch
A tiny magnetically operated switch sealed in glass; Switching Technologies Gunther's product.
Control panel
A cabinet of breakers, relays and meters assembled for a specific plant or substation; Shivalic's business.
Wiring devices
Switches, sockets and similar low-voltage products sold through retail.

Commercial and market

Order inflow
New orders won in a period — ₹5,096.5 cr at Hitachi, ₹1,137 cr at GE Vernova T&D this quarter.
Order backlog
Orders not yet executed — a record ₹32,222 cr at Hitachi.
Export sourcing
A global parent routing orders to its Indian factories.
Royalty and technical fee
Payments from an Indian subsidiary to its parent for technology and brand.
Type test and qualification
Proof that equipment meets a utility's specification; a barrier to entry (see 042).
Turnkey substation
A complete substation delivered as one package.
Price variation clause (PVC)
A contract term that passes changes in copper, steel or other input costs to the customer.
Voltage classes
Low voltage up to 1 kV, medium voltage 1–36 kV, high voltage above that, and extra- and ultra-high voltage at 400 kV, 765 kV and ±800 kV DC.
Consortium bid
A joint bid by several suppliers, such as Hitachi Energy with BHEL for Khavda–Nagpur.
Related-party transactions
Purchases, sales and fees between the Indian arm and its parent group; shareholders must approve the material ones.
Grid integration
Equipment and engineering to connect generators or large loads to the grid.
Demerger
How Hitachi Energy India came to be listed separately from ABB India in 2020 (see 028).
