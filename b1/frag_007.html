1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Fluorine is hard to handle, and that's the moat

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Fluorine is the most "grabby" element there is: it sticks to almost anything and, once stuck, almost never lets go. That's why a pan coated with a fluorine plastic is non-stick, why fluorine gases carry heat so well inside a fridge, and why medicines with fluorine last longer in the body. But because it is so grabby, it is dangerous to work with: its acid (hydrofluoric acid) can eat through glass. Only a few companies in the world know how to handle it safely at scale. It starts with a rock called fluorspar, dug up in South Africa, China, Mexico and Mongolia. Because some fluorine gases warm the planet, countries have agreed to limit how much can be made, and when making it is capped, the few makers who hold the permits earn more.

Say it to a client in one minute

Fluorochemicals start with fluorspar, which is turned into hydrofluoric acid (HF). From HF come refrigerant gases (R32, R125, R134a, blends), fluoropolymers (PTFE, PVDF, FKM), battery chemicals (LiPF₆, PVDF binder) and fluorinated intermediates for pharma and agrochemicals. Refrigerants are a quota-driven commodity: China capped HFC production from 2024, which pushed R32 up ~80% (H1 2026 vs H1 2023). India freezes in 2028 on a 2024–26 baseline and will stop clearing new HFC plants after 2027, so capacity built now counts: Tanfac is adding 20,000 t of R32, and SRF is building HFO and HF capacity in Odisha. All four listed players posted strong Q1 FY27 results. The long-term value is in fluoropolymers, battery chemicals, next-generation HFOs and CDMO. The risks are refrigerant price normalisation (SRF's own warning) and PFAS regulation in Europe, which is unlikely to bite before 2029.

Everyday examples

The gas circulating in your split AC is probably R32 (SRF, Navin, GFL); your car's AC uses R134a or, in newer European cars, R1234yf; the non-stick frying pan is PTFE (GFL); the asthma inhaler's propellant is a medical-grade HFC; the phone battery's electrolyte salt is LiPF₆; the fluorine atom in many modern crop sprays came through a fluorinated intermediate.

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Fluorspar: a shiny purple-green rock, crushed and cleaned.
  • HF: the rock is cooked with sulphuric acid to release fluorine as a dangerous acid; handling it safely is the hard part.
  • Refrigerants: fluorine is attached to small carbon molecules to make gases that soak up heat inside your AC and release it outside.
  • Fluoropolymers: long chains of carbon and fluorine that nothing sticks to and almost nothing can dissolve.
  • Specialty chemistry: fluorine carefully added to one exact spot on a big medicine or pesticide molecule, like a jeweller setting a stone.

Fund-manager lens

The key link is R22 → PTFE. R22 is being phased out as a refrigerant but remains allowed as a feedstock, so a producer with chloromethanes, HF and R22 can make PTFE cheaply. GFL built its fluoropolymer business on this integration. Refrigerant pricing is the swing factor in profits; fluoropolymers, battery chemicals and CDMO are the stable growth. The 2024–26 Indian baseline window is a once-only land grab: whatever capacity producers add now defines their future production rights.

Fund-manager lens: four ways to own fluorine

SRF is the diversified, lowest-risk route, but you also buy films and textiles, and its specialty chemicals face Chinese pricing. 5 · How money is made

Quota rent on commodities, approvals on specialties

  • Refrigerants: commodity pricing, but supply is capped by law (China's quota; the US AIM Act; India's freeze from 2028). When demand grows faster than quotas, spreads widen sharply, as in 2025–26.
  • Fluoropolymers: grades approved customer by customer (semiconductors, automotive, medical); EBITDA margins of 25–35% in good years.
  • CDMO and specialty: multi-year supply contracts; value depends on the molecule's success in the market.
  • HF and inorganics: cost-plus on fluorspar and sulphur; captive HF gives downstream players a cost edge.
Analogy

A refrigerant quota works like a taxi-licence system: when the city caps the number of taxis and demand keeps rising, the licence holders earn more, even though every taxi is the same. Fluoropolymers are more like a specialist surgeon: fewer customers, each one needing a lengthy approval, but much steadier fees once trusted.

Carbon credits, the forgotten chapter: until 2012–13, SRF and Navin earned large income from destroying HFC-23 (a potent greenhouse gas made alongside R22) under the UN's clean development mechanism. When carbon-credit prices collapsed and the EU stopped accepting industrial-gas credits (2013), that income disappeared, one reason the stocks lagged in 2012–13.

6 · The business calendar

A year shaped by air-conditioning season

  • October–February: AC and refrigerant makers stock up ahead of summer; refrigerant contract prices are set, and China announces next year's quota (usually October–December).
  • March–August: the northern summer; peak refrigerant demand in India (March–June) and the US/Europe (May–August). Service-market prices spike in heatwaves.
  • September–October: the season ends; destocking and weaker spot prices.
  • Year-round: fluoropolymers, CDMO and battery chemicals follow customers' capex and launches, not the weather.
  • Regulatory dates: China's quota announcement (Q4), India's baseline years (2024–26) and clearance cut-off (end-2027), EU PFAS milestones (SEAC opinion by end-2026).

How to use this

For fluorochemicals the calendar edge is real and has a business reason: refrigerant pricing firms into summer and Q1 results (July–August) report it. It works best when Chinese quotas are tight; in oversupply years (2018, 2023–24) it fades.

8 · What moves demand and margins

Seven drivers

  1. China's HFC quota: frozen at 2024 levels; the 2026 quota was flat to slightly lower. This is the single biggest driver of refrigerant prices.
  2. India's baseline race: capacity added in 2024–26 sets future rights; clearances for new HFC production stop after 2027. Expect more announcements (Tanfac R32, SRF, Navin, GFL) before then.
  3. AC penetration and heatwaves: India's room-AC penetration is only ~10%; the market grows double digits in hot years; data-centre cooling is a new demand pool.
  4. Fluorspar, sulphur and HF costs: fluorspar ~$550/t; sulphur records in 2026 hit HF makers (Tanfac).
  5. Battery supply chains: LiPF₆ and PVDF demand from cell makers outside China.
  6. Agro and pharma intermediate cycles: the 2023–25 agrochemical destocking hurt SRF's and Navin's specialty businesses.
  7. PFAS regulation: EU and US moves against "forever chemicals" could restrict some fluoropolymers; the EU decision is unlikely before Q3 2027 and would apply from 2029 at the earliest.

The anatomy of a fluorochemical cycle

  1. Regulation creates scarcity (quotas, phase-outs, environmental closures) → refrigerant spreads widen.
  2. Producers race to add capacity before baselines lock (China before 2024; India before 2027).
  3. Oversupply until the freeze bites (China's 2021–23 volume race crushed prices).
  4. The freeze bites → scarcity pricing (2025–26); producers use the windfall to build fluoropolymers, HFOs and battery chemicals, the next leg.

Patterns that repeat

  • Specialty chemistry, not refrigerants, made the long-term money. SRF's 2014 and 2019 surges and Navin's 2015 and 2020 surges were driven by specialty and CDMO, not gases.
  • Policy windfalls end. Carbon credits (to 2012) and Chinese quotas (from 2024) both created income that the market learned to discount.
  • The HF owner wins in upcycles. Tanfac, the smallest, has been the basket's best performer in most recent years (2021, 2022, 2023, 2026).

10 · The global picture

China makes most of it; the West holds the patents

  • China dominates HF, refrigerants and fluoropolymers. Zhejiang Juhua alone holds ~38% of China's HFC quota; six producers hold over 90%.
  • Chemours, Honeywell, Daikin, Arkema, Solvay/Syensqo, 3M (exiting PFAS) are the Western majors; they own HFO patents and many specialty grades.
  • India is the main non-Chinese alternative for refrigerants and fluoropolymers; Western buyers are building supply chains outside China, especially for batteries and data-centre cooling.
  • Fluorspar: China's shift from exporter to net importer tightens the global market; Mexico, South Africa and Mongolia fill the gap.
  • Country leadership shift: the US and Europe invented CFCs, HFCs and HFOs; China took over volume production in the 2000s; India is now taking share in high-value niches (CDMO, fluoropolymers) and adding refrigerant capacity before its 2028 freeze.

16 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What share of profit comes from refrigerants, and what margin do you assume at "normal" pricing?
  2. How much capacity will count toward India's Kigali baseline, and what's planned before the 2027 cut-off?
  3. What is your HF and fluorspar integration and sourcing?
  4. Where are fluoropolymer and battery-chemical approvals, and with which customers?
  5. What exposure do you have to PFAS restrictions?

Red flags

  • Extrapolating peak refrigerant prices.
  • Battery capex without firm offtake.
  • Heavy dependence on a single CDMO molecule.
  • A crowded rush of new R32 capacity before 2028.

19 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. Why did refrigerant prices hit decade highs in 2026?

China capped HFC production in 2024 and kept 2026 quotas flat while demand grew; six producers control 90% of quotas.

2. Why is R22 still made if it's being phased out?

It's allowed as a feedstock for PTFE (non-emissive use).

3. Why does 2024–26 output matter for Indian producers?

It sets India's Kigali baseline, the cap from 2028.

4. What makes Gujarat Fluorochemicals different from SRF?

It's mainly a fluoropolymer company building a battery-chemicals business; SRF is a conglomerate with specialty chemicals, packaging films and textiles.

5. Which months have a real edge, and why?

April, July and August–September: the air-conditioning season and Q1 results.

6. What income did SRF and Navin lose in 2012–13?

Carbon-credit income from destroying HFC-23, after CER prices collapsed and the EU stopped accepting industrial-gas credits.

7. Why is Tanfac building an R32 plant now?

To have capacity counted before India's 2028 freeze and the end-2027 clearance cut-off, using its own HF.

8. When could EU PFAS restrictions actually apply?

Not before 2029; the Commission is unlikely to decide before Q3 2027.

9. Why did SRF's stock fall despite a record quarter?

Management warned refrigerant prices would normalise; specialty chemicals face Chinese pricing; Middle East exports were hit.

10. What's the next growth leg for each company?

SRF: HFOs and HF (Odisha); GFL: battery chemicals; Navin: CDMO and liquid-cooling fluids; Tanfac: R32 and solar-grade HF.
