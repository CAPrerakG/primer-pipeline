Motorcycle Helmets & Safety Accessories

A ₹1,000 product that decides whether someone survives a fall at 40 km/h. India puts more than two crore two-wheelers on the road every year, two-wheeler riders account for roughly 44% of all road deaths, and from January 2026 every new two-wheeler must be sold with two certified helmets. This is a small industry sitting on top of an enormous, newly-compulsory demand pool — with exactly one listed pure play to express it.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

A regulated consumer product with one listed owner

Explain it to a 10-year-old

A helmet is a hard plastic shell with a thick layer of special foam inside. If your head hits the road, the shell spreads the blow over a wide area and the foam squashes slowly so your brain does not get shaken. The squashing is the whole trick: a helmet is designed to be destroyed once, so that you are not. India has a government test that a helmet must pass before it can be sold, and helmets that pass get an ISI mark. Many helmets sold at traffic lights never took the test — they look like helmets and are really just plastic hats. From January 2026 every new motorbike or scooter has to come with two real, tested helmets: one for the rider and one for the person sitting behind.

Say it to a client in one minute

This is a regulatory-transition story, not a growth story you can buy cheaply today.

  • The rule: from January 2026, every new two-wheeler must be supplied with two BIS-certified helmets, alongside mandatory ABS (see 018). That converts a discretionary purchase into a compulsory one at the point of sale.
  • The prize: more than 30% of the domestic market has been unorganised and largely uncertified. Rules that are actually enforced move that volume to certified makers.
  • The only listed pure play is Studds Accessories — India's largest helmet maker by revenue and, by volume, the world's largest — which listed in November 2025.
  • The catch: its first listed year has been a squeeze, not a windfall. Q1 FY27 revenue rose 13.7% but profit fell 39% and EBITDA margin dropped 880 basis points to 11.5%, because styrene-based raw materials rose faster than prices could be passed on. The stock is down 24% this year.

The interesting question is not whether demand arrives — the rule guarantees it — but whether a plastics processor selling a ₹1,000 product can hold a consumer-brand margin while it does.

Analogy

A helmet is a crumple zone for the head. A car protects you by destroying its own bonnet slowly over a metre; a helmet has about two centimetres of foam to do the same job, which is why the foam density and the shell's stiffness matter far more than how the helmet looks. And like a crumple zone, it works once — a helmet that has taken a real impact should be replaced even if it looks fine.

Everyday examples

The ₹600 helmet sold at a traffic signal is usually a thin shell with almost no energy-absorbing liner — a fine-avoidance device rather than safety equipment. The helmet that now comes in the box with a new Splendor is a certified one, because the dealer must supply it. The ₹35,000 carbon helmet on a weekend rider's head is an imported product this industry does not yet compete for.

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Polymers: the hard plastic starts as a chemical made from oil, so when oil-based chemicals get expensive, helmets get expensive to make.
  • Shell: hot plastic is squirted into a mould shaped like half a head and cooled.
  • Liner: a thick layer of foam — the same family as the white foam in a packing box, but much denser — is glued inside. That foam does the actual saving.
  • Certification: sample helmets are dropped on anvils and hit with a spike in a laboratory. If they pass, the maker gets a licence to print the ISI mark.
  • Channels: helmets reach riders through bike showrooms, shops and the internet. The new rule means the showroom now hands you two.
Fund-manager lens

Two structural facts define this industry. First, it is a polymer converter wearing a consumer badge: roughly the cost structure of a plastics processor with the distribution of a branded good, which is why a styrene spike can take 880 basis points of margin out in a single quarter. Second, its demand is manufactured by regulation and sustained by enforcement. A rule that is passed but not policed moves nothing; a rule embedded at the point of sale — as the two-helmet rule is — moves volume the day it starts. Judge this sector by the enforcement mechanism, not by the announcement.

How the market size is quoted, and why the numbers disagree

Published estimates of the Indian helmet market vary by an order of magnitude. One widely-quoted study puts the two-wheeler helmet market at about US$2.25 bn in 2025, growing to US$3.82 bn by 2034 at roughly 6% a year. An industry article describing the unorganised sector's share puts the domestic market at about ₹2,500 cr. Both cannot be describing the same thing. A sanity check helps: Studds, the largest maker by revenue, reported Q1 FY27 revenue of ₹169.7 cr — an annual run-rate of roughly ₹650–700 cr. A market in which the leader holds a large share and the whole organised trade is a few thousand crore is consistent with the ₹2,500 cr figure for branded, certified helmets; the dollar figure must be counting the entire roadside trade, accessories and replacement at retail prices. Use the smaller number when sizing a listed company's opportunity, and the larger one only when discussing the total volume pool that regulation might formalise.

The arithmetic of the quarter

Q1 FY27 is worth walking through, because it is the clearest lesson this industry has offered a public investor. Revenue rose 13.7% to ₹169.7 cr. EBITDA fell 35.3% to ₹19.6 cr and the margin fell 880 basis points to 11.5%. Consolidated net profit fell 39.3% to ₹12.3 cr. Nothing went wrong with demand: the cause was styrene-linked input costs, incremental wage costs, and the lag between announcing a price increase and collecting it. A 9% increase that realises 5% in the quarter leaves the balance to arrive later — but the cost arrived immediately. That gap is the entire result.

Analogy

It is the tyre problem (025) in miniature, with one difference: a tyre maker sells to fleet buyers who accept indexed prices, while a helmet maker sells through thousands of small shops where the printed price on the box is sticky. The smaller and more fragmented the channel, the longer the lag — and the lag is the loss.

7 · The business calendar

Three demand triggers, none of them evenly spread

  • The festive quarter (roughly September–November): two-wheeler sales peak, and with them the original-equipment helmet channel — now doubled by the two-helmet rule.
  • Pre-monsoon (April–June): riders replace worn visors and buy rain gear; the accessories adjacency is at its most seasonal.
  • Enforcement drives: state-level campaigns and automated challan systems create sharp, geographically uneven spikes in replacement demand. This is the least predictable and most powerful trigger.
  • Rule commencement dates: January 2026 is the clearest example — a single date that changes the attach rate on every new vehicle sold thereafter.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Eight drivers

  1. Two-wheeler sales: the original-equipment channel, now at two helmets per vehicle instead of an informal one (see 018).
  2. Enforcement intensity: the replacement channel is effectively a policing function; automated challans and state drives move volume.
  3. The certified-versus-uncertified shift: quality control orders and BIS licensing squeeze the roadside trade, transferring volume to organised makers.
  4. Styrene and polymer prices: the cost line that decides the quarter.
  5. Price-increase realisation: how much of an announced increase actually reaches the invoice, and when.
  6. Premiumisation: the mix shift toward full-face, composite and comms-ready helmets, where value per unit is several times the mass segment.
  7. Exports: Indian makers ship to Asian, African and Middle Eastern markets; standards conformity (ECE, DOT) decides which markets are open.
  8. Capacity and utilisation: moulding is fixed-cost heavy, so utilisation swings margin hard in both directions.

10 · How the rules were built

Four decades of regulation, and one decisive year

With no price history to trace, the honest history of this industry is the history of the rules that created it. Each step below expanded the market or raised the barrier to entry.

Why the pillion helmet is the point

The safety case for the rule is specific rather than general. Two-wheeler users are roughly 44% of India's road deaths, and pillion riders account for close to 30% of two-wheeler fatalities — yet a study in Bengaluru found pillion riders about 2.3 times less likely to be wearing a helmet than the rider in front of them, despite facing the same collision. The rule attacks exactly that gap by putting the second helmet in the box, where the buyer has already paid for it. Industry voices have put the potential reduction in two-wheeler fatalities as high as a quarter; that figure is an industry estimate rather than a measured outcome, and the honest position in September 2026 is that the compliance data needed to test it does not yet exist.

What the record does not yet tell us

  • Whether the second helmet is worn or simply left at home — the difference between a volume event and a safety outcome.
  • Whether dealers bundle quality or minimum-price certified product, which decides whether the rule lifts revenue or only units.
  • Whether the roadside trade shrinks or finds a way to supply the dealer channel at the entry price point.
  • Whether the demand pull-forward matters: a rider given two helmets in January 2026 will not buy a replacement for several years.

11 · The global picture

India makes the volume; Europe and Japan set the standard

The global helmet business splits between a premium tier — Shoei, Arai, AGV, Shark, HJC and similar brands, sold on certification level, weight and comfort — and a very large volume tier serving Asian commuter markets, where India, China and South-East Asia dominate production. India is estimated at roughly 24% of the Asia-Pacific helmet market and among its fastest-growing shares. The competitive question for an Indian maker is which standard it can certify to, because that determines which export markets are open.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • From leather and cork to thermoplastics (20th century): injection moulding made a certified helmet affordable for a commuter market.
  • From impact-only to rotational protection (2020s): ECE 22.06 and slip-plane liner technologies moved the definition of safety beyond the straight-line drop test.
  • From imports to Indian volume manufacture: India became a global volume base — Studds is the largest helmet maker in the world by volume — while premium value remained with European and Japanese brands.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Rotational-impact protection: low-friction slip layers between liner and head that reduce twisting forces on the brain; standard on premium helmets and moving down-market.
  2. Composite shells: fibreglass, aramid and carbon shells that are lighter for the same strength — lighter helmets get worn more.
  3. Multi-density liners: layered EPS tuned to handle both low and high-energy impacts, an ECE 22.06 requirement in practice.
  4. Communication and electronics: integrated Bluetooth intercoms and camera mounts, which link this industry to the electronics supply chain in 024.
  5. Visor technology: anti-fog, photochromic and sun-visor systems, the most common reason a commuter upgrades.
  6. Traceability and anti-counterfeiting: QR-coded BIS licences and hologram marking, to make a counterfeit ISI mark harder — the single most useful technology for this industry's formalisation.

The honest read

The investment case and the quarterly reality have separated, which is the most interesting position a business can be in. The case is intact and improving: a compulsory two-helmet attach rate, an unorganised third of the market under regulatory pressure, new capacity landing in October, and a leader with the scale and SKU depth to supply a dealer channel nationally. What 2026 has established is the bill: this is a polymer converter first, and its margin is decided by styrene and by how fast a price increase moves through thousands of small shops. The guidance to 18–20% by the March quarter is the number to hold management to, and it carries its own explicit condition — stable commodity prices — which is exactly the thing nobody in this chain controls. One listed company and eleven months of history mean position sizing should reflect that this is a single-stock bet on execution, not a diversified exposure to a rule.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What share of volume now comes through the dealer channel because of the two-helmet rule, and at what average realisation compared with retail?
  2. Of the roughly 9% price increase, how much is in the invoice today, and what is left to realise?
  3. What is the current raw-material cost per helmet, and what is hedged or contracted?
  4. What utilisation is the existing 9.04 million-unit capacity running at before the October expansion?
  5. What is the premium (SMK) share of revenue, and where is it going?
  6. How many SKUs actually earn their tooling cost — is the 19,000-plus SKU count an asset or working capital?
  7. What is the export mix by standard (IS, ECE, DOT), and which market is growing?
  8. What is the replacement cycle assumption, given a cohort of buyers who received two free helmets in 2026?
  9. What specifically has to happen for the 18–20% Q4 FY27 margin run-rate to be met if styrene does not fall?

Red flags

  • Volume growth from the dealer channel at falling realisation — the rule filled by the cheapest certified product.
  • A margin guidance repeated but pushed out by a quarter each time.
  • Inventory and receivable days rising with the SKU count.
  • Capacity commissioned into a demand pull-forward.

18 · Myths and live debates

What you'll hear, and the evidence

ClaimEvidence
"The two-helmet rule is a guaranteed windfall."It is a guaranteed volume event, not a guaranteed profit event. It began in January 2026; by the June quarter the listed player's profit was down 39% on input costs.
"The unorganised sector will disappear."Certification has been mandatory for years and the informal trade has been estimated at more than 30% of the market. Enforcement at the roadside, not the rule itself, is what shrinks it.
"Helmets are a commodity."Partly true on cost, false on demand: brand, fit, visor quality and weight drive repeat purchase, which is why a leader can hold an 18–20% margin ambition that a moulder cannot.
"Free helmets with the bike will kill the replacement market."They pull demand forward and reset the replacement clock for a cohort. Over a full cycle it is volume-positive; over the next two years it may be mix-negative.
"It is a safety stock, so demand is recession-proof."Compulsory at purchase, discretionary at replacement. The replacement half moves with enforcement and with household budgets.
"One listed company is enough to represent the industry."It is the only one, which is not the same thing. There is no peer set to check margins, multiples or claims against, and eleven months of price history supports no statistical inference at all.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. What changed in January 2026?

Every new two-wheeler must be supplied with two BIS-certified helmets — rider and pillion — alongside mandatory ABS.

2. Which part of a helmet actually protects the head?

The EPS liner. It crushes and absorbs energy; the shell spreads the load and resists penetration.

3. Why did Studds' profit fall 39% in a quarter when revenue rose 13.7%?

Styrene-based raw materials and wage costs rose faster than prices could be passed through: EBITDA margin fell 880 basis points to 11.5%.

4. Why is the pillion helmet the focus of the rule?

Pillion riders are close to 30% of two-wheeler deaths but were found about 2.3 times less likely than riders to wear a helmet in a Bengaluru study.

5. Why does this primer contain no seasonality table?

The section has one listed company with eleven months of price history. Any statistic from that would be an artefact.

6. How big is the Indian helmet market?

It depends what is counted: about ₹2,500 cr for the branded, certified market, against roughly US$2.25 bn in studies that include the whole roadside trade at retail prices. The leader's run-rate of ₹650–700 cr is the sanity check.

7. What margin has management guided to, and on what condition?

14–15% EBITDA in Q2 FY27 and 18–20% on a Q4 FY27 run-rate, explicitly conditional on stable commodity prices.

8. What is the main risk to the replacement market from the new rule?

Pull-forward: buyers who receive two helmets with the vehicle will not replace them for years.

9. Which standard raised the global engineering bar, and how?

ECE 22.06, by adding rotational-acceleration and multi-impact testing to the traditional straight-line drop test.

10. What should be tracked to read this industry between results?

Monthly two-wheeler sales, styrene prices, and enforcement or challan news — in that order.
