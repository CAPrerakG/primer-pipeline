Semiconductors & Electronic Components

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

A national priority with almost nothing to buy

Explain it to a 10-year-old

A chip is a tiny slab of silicon with billions of microscopic switches on it. Making one takes four steps. First, engineers draw the design on computers — like an architect drawing a building. Second, a factory called a fab "prints" the design onto round silicon wafers using light, chemicals and machines that cost more than a jumbo jet. Third, the wafer is cut into tiny pieces and each piece is sealed in a small plastic or ceramic package with metal legs — that is "assembly". Fourth, every chip is tested. India is very good at the first step, is now starting the third and fourth, and is building its first factory for the second.

Say it to a client in one minute

India's semiconductor build-out is real, but the listed pure-plays are tiny and mostly not the companies building it.

  • The milestones are happening: Micron started commercial production at Sanand in February 2026; CG Semi shipped its first qualified chips to Renesas in July; Kaynes Semicon shipped its first module in October 2025; Tata's Assam packaging plant was commissioning from April and its Dholera fab targets first silicon in late 2026.
  • The money is large: Semicon 2.0 has a ₹1.27 lakh cr outlay after the first ₹76,000 cr programme.
  • The global cycle is extraordinary: WSTS expects about $1.5 trillion of chip sales in 2026, up about 90%, with memory more than tripling.
  • The listed pure-plays are small and struggling: MosChip's revenue −14% and profit −78% in Q1; SPEL plans a rights issue; RIR is commissioning a ₹618 cr silicon-carbide plant.
  • Where the exposure really sits: CG Power, Kaynes, Tata Elxsi, Cyient and others in other sections; Tata Electronics and Micron are unlisted in India.

Analogy

Think of book publishing. The author writes the book (design); the printing press prints it (fab); the binder cuts, folds and binds the pages into a book (packaging); and a checker looks at each copy for errors (test). India has always had many of the world's best authors. It is now building binderies, and it is building its first printing press. The press is the hardest and most expensive part — and whoever owns it controls supply.

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Materials and machines: ultra-pure silicon discs and the most complicated machines humans make.
  • Design: engineers draw the chip's map on computers — India does a lot of this.
  • Fab: a giant clean factory prints the map onto the silicon.
  • Packaging: each tiny chip is cut out, wired up and sealed.
  • Helpers: other small parts, like capacitors, sit next to chips on a board.
  • Assembly: everything is soldered onto boards inside phones and computers.
Fund-manager lens

The critical distinction for an investor is between the national story and the listed opportunity. The national story — packaging plants running, a fab under construction, a second programme funded — is credible and funded. But the value created so far sits with Micron (a US company), Tata Electronics (unlisted), and divisions of larger listed groups (CG Power, Kaynes). The three listed pure-plays here are small, earn little, and trade largely on the theme: their 2021 and 2024 surges came with policy announcements, not with profits. The right way to own Indian semiconductors in 2026 is mostly through companies in other sections, and to treat this section's names as options on specific projects — RIR's silicon-carbide plant, SPEL's revival — to be judged on execution.

The role of subsidies

Semiconductor manufacturing everywhere depends on state support. India's scheme pays up to half of eligible project cost for fabs and packaging plants from the centre, with states such as Gujarat, Odisha and Assam adding more — so a company's economics depend heavily on approvals and disbursement timing. The design-linked incentive scheme supports chip-design start-ups. This makes the policy calendar unusually important for the stocks.

Analogy

A fab is like an airline: huge fixed costs, and profits only when the planes are full. That is why governments pay half the cost of the plane and why the first question for any new fab is who the anchor customers are.

7 · The business calendar

Policy dates and global cycles

  • February budget: customs duties and incentive allocations for electronics and semiconductors.
  • Semicon India (usually September): the government's annual showcase, often accompanied by announcements.
  • Cabinet approvals: new fab and OSAT projects are approved in batches through the year.
  • Global memory and chip price cycles: monthly WSTS data and memory-price trackers.
  • Customer design wins: lumpy and unannounced; they drive design-services revenue.

8 · What the stocks do

Seasonality test on TradingView prices

The exceptions, and why

  • April–July failed in 2013 (−28.1), 2019 (−23.0), 2018 (−20.1), 2022 (−16.6) and 2017 (−5.3) — small-cap sell-offs and, in 2022, the post-2021 comedown. Its best runs were 2023 (+60.6) and 2024 (+53.7), when Micron and then the Tata, CG and Kaynes projects were approved.
  • October–December failed in 2025 (−35.8), 2019 (−25.8), 2024 (−9.2) and 2012 (−1.9); 2025's fall came as expectations ran ahead of revenue.
  • February–March worked only in 2021, 2024, 2015 and 2020; it failed badly in 2022 (−32.1) and 2025 (−22.5).
How to use this

With three small stocks, the pattern is suggestive rather than reliable. The more useful observation is that the biggest moves — 2021, 2023 and 2024 — came with government approvals, and the falls came when revenue failed to follow. Watch the policy calendar and the companies' own execution milestones rather than the month.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Eight drivers

  1. Government incentives and approvals: ISM 1.0 and Semicon 2.0.
  2. Global chip cycle: 2026's AI-led memory boom lifts demand for packaging and test capacity.
  3. Design-services demand: global chip companies' R&D budgets and outsourcing.
  4. Anchor customers: who commits volume to Indian plants.
  5. Electric vehicles, railways and renewables: demand for power semiconductors.
  6. Supply-chain diversification away from China and Taiwan: the "China plus one" logic applied to chips.
  7. Electronics manufacturing in India: local demand for chips from phones, meters and cars (052).
  8. Execution: yields, qualification and ramp-up schedules.

The anatomy of the cycles

  1. Forgotten small caps (2012–2019): three obscure companies with occasional speculative spikes (MosChip +704% in 2016).
  2. The theme is born (2020–2021): the pandemic chip shortage and the ₹76,000 cr mission; the basket +107% and +206%.
  3. Projects approved (2023–2024): Micron, then Tata, CG and Kaynes; +72% and +94%.
  4. Waiting for revenue (2025–2026): real chips shipped — but by other companies; the listed trio fell or went sideways.

Patterns that repeat

  • Policy announcements move these stocks far more than earnings do.
  • Thin trading magnifies everything: single years of +400% and −60%.
  • The name "semiconductor" has been enough to re-rate a stock — which is exactly why misclassified names appear in this section.

11 · The global picture

An AI super-cycle, and a race to diversify supply

Global semiconductor sales reached a record $791.7 bn in 2025 (+25.6%), and WSTS now forecasts about $1.5 trillion for 2026 — roughly 90% growth — with memory sales expected to rise from about $230 bn to about $804 bn as AI accelerators consume high-bandwidth memory. Monthly sales hit $120.6 bn in May 2026, more than double a year earlier. Manufacturing remains concentrated: TSMC makes most of the world's leading-edge logic, Korea dominates memory, and China has built large capacity in older nodes. Governments in the US, Europe, Japan and India are all paying to build domestic capacity for security reasons.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • From the US and Japan to Taiwan and Korea (1990s–2010s): the foundry model and memory scale.
  • From integrated makers to fabless plus foundry: design separated from manufacturing, which is why India could build a design industry without fabs.
  • From efficiency to security (2020–2026): the pandemic shortage and geopolitics made domestic capacity a national priority everywhere.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Advanced packaging: chiplets and 2.5D/3D packaging raise the value of the OSAT step — India's entry point.
  2. High-bandwidth memory: stacked memory for AI accelerators, the heart of the 2026 boom.
  3. Silicon carbide and gallium nitride: power chips for EVs, chargers, solar inverters and railways (RIR, SiCSem).
  4. Mature nodes for industrial and automotive chips: the 28–110 nm range Tata's fab targets.
  5. Indian-designed chips with local IP: a stated priority of Semicon 2.0.
  6. AI in chip design: automation in verification and layout, changing design-services economics.

The honest read

India's semiconductor programme has moved from announcements to output faster than many sceptics expected: packaging plants are shipping chips to global customers, and a fab is being built. But the listed pure-plays in this section are not the vehicles for that success, and their share prices already discounted a lot of it in 2021 and 2024. Each needs to be judged on its own execution: MosChip on whether its turnkey chip projects convert to steady revenue; RIR on whether its silicon-carbide wafers qualify with customers; SPEL on whether new capital brings anchor volume. For broad exposure to the theme, the larger listed groups with semiconductor subsidiaries are the more direct route.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. Who are the anchor customers, and what volumes are committed?
  2. What subsidies have been approved and what has actually been disbursed?
  3. What is the qualification timeline with each customer?
  4. (Design) What share of revenue is time-and-materials versus turnkey, and how lumpy are milestones?
  5. (Packaging) What are utilisation and yield, and what package types can you run?
  6. (SiC) What wafer sizes and defect densities, and who are the first customers?
  7. How much more capital is needed before the business funds itself?
  8. What technology partners and licences does the business depend on?

Red flags

  • Share-price moves on announcements with no customer names.
  • Repeated capital raises without revenue milestones.
  • "Semiconductor" in the name without a semiconductor business.
  • Delays in subsidy disbursement funded by short-term debt.

19 · Glossary

Every term you'll meet

Technology

Semiconductor
A material, usually silicon, whose conductivity can be controlled — the basis of chips.
Integrated circuit (IC)
A chip containing many connected transistors.
Transistor
A microscopic switch; modern chips hold billions.
Wafer
A thin round disc of silicon on which chips are made.
Die
A single chip cut from a wafer.
Node (nm)
A label for a manufacturing generation; smaller numbers mean denser, more advanced chips.
Mature node
Older processes, roughly 28 nm and above, used for industrial, automotive and power chips.
Lithography
Printing circuit patterns with light.
EUV
Extreme-ultraviolet lithography, needed for the most advanced nodes.
Clean room
A room with almost no dust, where wafers are processed.
Yield
The share of chips on a wafer that work.
Logic chip
A processor or controller.
Memory chip
DRAM or NAND flash that stores data.
DRAM and NAND
Working memory and storage memory — Micron packages both at Sanand.
HBM
High-bandwidth memory, stacked DRAM used with AI accelerators.
Analog and mixed-signal chip
Chips that handle real-world signals such as power, sound and sensors.
Power semiconductor
Devices that switch and convert high power: thyristors, diodes, MOSFETs, IGBTs.
Thyristor
A high-power switch used in rectifiers and railway traction — RIR's legacy product.
Silicon carbide (SiC)
A material for efficient high-voltage power chips in EVs and chargers.
Gallium nitride (GaN)
A material for fast, efficient power and radio chips.
Epitaxial wafer
A wafer with a precisely grown crystal layer on top — RIR's first SiC product.
Capacitor
A component that stores and releases charge; used by the dozen on every circuit board.
Harmonic filter and power factor
Equipment that cleans up distorted current and improves how efficiently a plant draws power — Cospower's business.
Chiplet
A small die combined with others in one package.
Advanced packaging
2.5D and 3D techniques that connect dies densely.
Wire bonding and flip chip
Two ways of connecting a die to its package.

Industry structure

Fab
A chip fabrication plant.
Foundry
A fab that makes chips designed by others (TSMC, PSMC).
Fabless
A chip company that designs but does not manufacture.
IDM
Integrated device manufacturer — designs and makes its own chips (Intel, Micron).
OSAT
Outsourced semiconductor assembly and test.
ATMP
Assembly, test, marking and packaging — the Indian scheme's term for OSAT-type plants.
EDA
Electronic design automation — the software used to design chips.
IP core
A licensed, reusable design block.
ASIC
Application-specific integrated circuit — a custom chip.
SoC
System on chip — a whole system on one die.
Turnkey ASIC
A design service delivering finished chips, paid by milestones and royalties.
Tape-out
Sending a finished design to the fab.
Design win
A customer choosing a chip for its product.
Qualification
Testing that a supplier's chips meet a customer's standards.
Utilisation
How much of a plant's capacity is being used.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. What are the four steps in making a chip?

Design, fabrication, assembly and packaging, and test.

2. Which step is India strongest in?

Design — a large share of global chip-design engineering is done in India.

3. When did Micron's Sanand plant begin commercial production?

February 2026, packaging and testing DRAM and NAND.

4. What node range will Tata's Dholera fab make?

28 to 110 nm — mature nodes for automotive, industrial and power chips.

5. Why is the listed section a poor proxy for India's chip build-out?

The companies building it are unlisted (Tata Electronics, Micron in India) or in other sections (CG Power, Kaynes).

6. What is an OSAT?

An outsourced assembly and test company — SPEL is the listed Indian example.

7. What is RIR building in Odisha?

India's first vertically integrated silicon-carbide semiconductor plant, starting with epitaxial wafers.

8. Why did MosChip's profit fall?

Turnkey ASIC milestones slipped and product-engineering orders converted slowly; revenue −14%, profit −78%.

9. What is driving the 2026 global boom?

AI accelerators' demand for high-bandwidth memory; memory sales are forecast to more than triple.

10. Why was Cospower excluded from the basket?

It makes capacitors and power-quality panels, not semiconductors, and its thinly traded share rose 5,300% since 2020.
