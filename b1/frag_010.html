Aroma & Flavour Chemicals

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Selling smell and taste

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Every shampoo, soap, perfume, biscuit and toothpaste has a smell or taste that someone designed. The designers are called perfumers and flavourists. They mix dozens or hundreds of ingredients, like a musician combining notes into a song. Some ingredients come from plants: mint grown in Uttar Pradesh, oil tapped from pine trees, lemon peel, cloves. Others are made in factories to copy nature cheaply, like making vanilla taste without vanilla pods. Camphor, the white tablet lit in temples, is made from pine-tree oil. When the mint harvest is poor or pine oil gets expensive, the ingredient makers' costs jump. The designers, whose recipes are secret, can usually pass the cost on.

Say it to a client in one minute

Two layers with opposite economics. Fragrance and flavour (F&F) houses design formulations and earn high, sticky margins because their recipes are locked into customers' brands. Globally these are Givaudan, DSM-Firmenich, IFF and Symrise; in India, S H Kelkar and Sacheerome. Ingredient makers produce aroma chemicals, camphor and mint derivatives (Oriental Aromatics, Mangalam Organics, Kanchi Karpooram, Gem Aromatics, and Privi in the specialty list). They are price-takers exposed to crop and pine-resin cycles and to Chinese competition. India dominates natural mint and is a big camphor consumer and maker. In 2026 alpha-pinene rose 70–80%: Oriental's profit shrank to ₹2.5 cr while S H Kelkar's rose 78%. The stocks moved the other way (Oriental +77%, Kelkar −24%), a reminder that this small basket trades on sentiment and debt more than on the layer model.

Naturals vs synthetics

A single rose note can be made three ways: steam-distil tonnes of petals (costly, varies by harvest), synthesise the key molecules (geraniol, phenylethyl alcohol) from petrochemicals, or brew them with engineered microbes (fermentation). Most mass-market products use synthetics because they are cheap, consistent and don't depend on the weather. Naturals are used where labels matter ("natural flavour") and in fine fragrance. The ingredient maker's business depends on which route wins for each molecule.

Analogy

A fragrance is like a song. The ingredients are the notes (some from nature, some from a synthesiser), the perfumer is the composer, and the brand is the singer who makes it famous. Once a song is a hit, nobody wants to change the tune, so the composer keeps earning. The people who make the instruments do not.

Everyday examples

The cooling hit of Colgate or a Vicks rub is menthol, likely from UP mint. The fresh "pine" smell of a floor cleaner is terpineol from turpentine. The vanilla in a biscuit is usually synthetic vanillin (see 008: Camlin). The kapur in a puja thali is synthetic camphor, from Mangalam, Oriental or Kanchi. The smell of a Lux or Dove soap is a compound designed by an F&F house under a secret brief.

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Farms and forests: mint is grown like wheat, planted in late winter and cut in early summer. Pine trees are cut gently, like tapping a rubber tree, and sticky resin drips into cups.
  • Extraction: the mint leaves are steamed in big drums, and the oil floats on the water. The pine resin is heated and splits into a solid (rosin) and a liquid (turpentine).
  • Aroma chemicals: chemists turn those oils into pure, single-smell molecules. Menthol crystals are frozen out of mint oil; camphor is built from turpentine in four chemical steps.
  • F&F houses: the perfumer mixes those molecules into a secret recipe for a brand ("make our new soap smell like the monsoon"). Brands run a competition called a "brief", and the winner supplies that smell for years.
  • Brand owners: the brand adds a small dose of the recipe to every bar of soap. The fragrance costs very little per bar but decides whether people buy it again.
Fund-manager lens

A fragrance costs a brand a tiny share of the product's price but defines the product, so brands rarely switch. That gives F&F houses pricing power and 20%+ margins globally. Ingredient makers have none: they sell molecules with global prices and absorb crop swings. Before judging margins, know which layer a company sits in, and whether it is moving up (Oriental and Gem building fragrance and flavour arms) or down (S H Kelkar making its own ingredients).

How an F&F house wins business

  1. A brand issues a brief ("a fresh, marine scent for a new detergent, costing no more than X per kg").
  2. Several houses on the brand's core list submit samples. Large brands keep only two to four houses on the list.
  3. Consumer tests pick a winner, who then supplies that compound for the life of the product, often five to ten years.
  4. Revenue grows with the brand's volumes and with new wins. Raw-material costs are passed on with a lag of one to two quarters.
Analogy

A fragrance house is like a songwriter who earns royalties every time a hit is played. A camphor maker is like a wheat farmer: good harvests (cheap resin) mean fat margins until everyone expands, and bad ones squeeze them. The songwriter's royalty doesn't care about the wheat price.

Fund-manager lens

For camphor makers, the spread over alpha-pinene is everything. 7 · The business calendar

Crops and festivals

  • Mint: planted January–March and distilled May–July in Uttar Pradesh. Prices move when the crop size becomes clear (June–August), and a weak monsoon start hurts yields.
  • Pine resin: tapped in the warm months in China, Indonesia and Brazil. Chinese export pricing sets world turpentine prices, with a lag.
  • Camphor: demand peaks around Navratri, Diwali and the South Indian temple seasons (Sabarimala, November–January). Stock-building happens in August–October.
  • FMCG: briefs and launches run all year; summer lifts deodorants and drinks flavours; winter lifts balms and rubs (menthol, camphor).
  • Global F&F: results come half-yearly (Givaudan) or quarterly (IFF, Symrise). Their volume comments are the best read on global FMCG demand.

8 · What the stocks do

Seasonality test on TradingView prices

The exceptions, and why

  • April misses: 2018 (−6.7) and 2019 (−0.3). 2018 was the small-cap sell-off after the LTCG tax and fund reclassification; 2019 was the camphor downcycle and small-cap bear market. The best Aprils were 2021 (+29.9), 2015 (+29.8) and 2024 (+22.1).
  • April–July failures: 2017 (−3.6), 2018 (−19.4), 2019 (−24.1), 2022 (−16.9). 2018–19 were the camphor bust plus the small-cap bear market; 2022 was raw-material inflation. 2017 was only a pause in a +106% year. The biggest runs were 2025 (+46.5) and 2021 (+46.0).
  • February–March beat the Nifty only in 2014 (+5.2) and 2017 (+4.5). The worst were 2023 (−19.9) and 2016 and 2025 (about −16 each).
  • October–December splits 6 of 12, with huge outliers: 2015 (+60.1) and 2017 (+54.1), both camphor-margin surges, against 2019 (−24.1) and 2022 (−14.6).
  • Sample warning: until 2025 the basket held two or three stocks, so one company's results can flip a month. Treat every pattern here as a hypothesis.
How to use this

The consistent message, across a small sample, is weak February–March and strong April and September. For a stock picker the calendar matters less than the layer and the input spread: in 2026 the best stock rose 77% and the worst fell 24%, and they moved against their own quarterly profits.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Seven drivers

  1. Natural raw-material prices: alpha-pinene and turpentine for camphor, mint oil for menthol, clove oil, citrus oils. These are weather- and crop-driven and hard to hedge.
  2. Chinese pricing and capacity: China is the largest maker of camphor, terpene chemicals and many synthetic aroma molecules. Its environmental drives (2017) and export pricing set the ceiling.
  3. Domestic camphor capacity: too many Indian plants chasing a festival-driven market repeatedly compresses margins (2019, 2023, 2026).
  4. FMCG volumes: soaps, detergents, oral care and packaged foods in India and emerging markets. Rural down-trading in 2022–23 hurt; recovery lifts F&F volumes.
  5. Synthetic substitutes: synthetic menthol (BASF from citral, Symrise from m-cresol, Takasago via asymmetric catalysis) caps natural mint prices; lab-made vanillin caps vanilla.
  6. Regulation and "natural" demand: the EU's expanded allergen-labelling rules, IFRA standards, and brands' push for natural and traceable ingredients.
  7. Balance sheets and capex: new plants (Oriental's Mahad, Gem's Dahej, S H Kelkar's expansions) add depreciation and debt before they add profit.

The anatomy of a camphor cycle

  1. Spread widens: cheap pine resin, or Chinese supply cut (2017), while temple and pharma demand stays steady. Camphor makers' margins double and stocks rise severalfold (2017: Mangalam +373%, Kanchi +387%).
  2. Everyone expands: new Indian capacity arrives within two years.
  3. Spread collapses: camphor prices fall or resin rises. Stocks fall 35–50% (2019, 2022).
  4. Diversify: companies move into fragrance compounds, pharma-grade camphor and specialty aromas to escape the cycle (Oriental's Mahad plant).

Patterns that repeat

  • F&F houses are steadier than ingredient makers in profit, but not always in price: S H Kelkar fell 42% in 2018 and 27% in 2025.
  • Stock moves often contradict the quarter. In 2026 Oriental rose 77% on a ₹2.5 cr profit, while S H Kelkar fell 24% on a 78% profit jump. The market pays for expected spread recovery and punishes debt.
  • New listings arrive near good times: Sacheerome and Gem listed in 2025 after two strong years. Gem is ~53% below its post-listing high.
  • Pandemics and hygiene scares help (2020: pine-oil disinfectants, soaps).

11 · The global picture

A Swiss–European oligopoly, Asian ingredients

Modern perfumery was born in Grasse (France) with natural extracts. It became an industry when chemists learned to make aroma molecules: coumarin (1868), vanillin (1874) and aldehydes, famous in Chanel No. 5 (1921). Consolidation over the last 30 years left four houses controlling most of the market. The ingredients have moved to Asia: China for terpenes and many synthetics, India for mint, some terpenes and a growing share of specialty molecules.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • Natural → synthetic (1870s–1950s): chemistry made smells cheaper than farming them.
  • Many houses → four (1990s–2023): scale in R&D and customer coverage. Givaudan's purchase of Quest (2007) and the DSM–Firmenich merger (2023) are examples.
  • Europe → Asia for ingredients (2000s): cost and environment. China took terpenes and many synthetics; India kept mint and built camphor and specialty aromas.
  • Natural mint → synthetic menthol, partly (2012 onwards): BASF's large synthetic-menthol plant and Symrise and Takasago capacity now cap natural mint prices after crop failures.
  • Supply shocks shift business: BASF's citral plant fire (October 2017) created a global citral and vitamin shortage in 2017–18 and pushed buyers to diversify suppliers.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Biotech (fermentation) ingredients: microbes brewing vanillin, nootkatone (grapefruit), patchoulol and ambergris-type notes (Givaudan's Ambrofix). This is a threat to crop-based ingredients and an opening for anyone who licenses the strains.
  2. AI-assisted perfumery: Givaudan's Carto and Symrise's Philyra (built with IBM) suggest formulas and speed up briefs, which strengthens the big houses' scale advantage.
  3. Upcycled and green chemistry: aroma chemicals from paper-mill turpentine, citrus peel and other waste streams; the base of Privi's model.
  4. Encapsulation: fragrance in microcapsules that burst on rubbing (long-lasting laundry scent). It adds value but faces microplastic rules in the EU.
  5. Headspace capture: analysing the air around a flower or a food to recreate its smell exactly.
  6. Pharma-grade and specialty camphor: the camphor makers' way out of the commodity cycle (Oriental's USFDA-approved grade).

16 · Adjacent industries and proxies

Where else the cycle shows up

  • FMCG (personal care, home care, packaged foods): the customers; their volumes lead F&F demand.
  • Incense and puja products (Ambica Agarbathies; Cycle Pure is unlisted): camphor and fragrance buyers.
  • Specialty chemicals (008): Privi, Camlin, Atul's aroma intermediates.
  • Paper and pulp: crude sulphate turpentine comes from kraft pulp mills.
  • Agriculture (UP mint belt): mint competes with paddy and maize for land.
  • Pharma OTC (balms, cough drops): menthol and camphor buyers.
  • Petrochemicals (002): feedstocks for synthetic aromas.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What share of revenue is compounds (fragrance and flavour) versus ingredients? How is that changing?
  2. (Camphor makers) What is today's spread over alpha-pinene versus the five-year average? How quickly are camphor prices catching up?
  3. How many months of raw-material inventory do you hold, and at what cost?
  4. Which customers are on your core list, and how many briefs did you win this year?
  5. What is the payback plan for the new plant (Mahad, Dahej): utilisation and margin targets, and when?
  6. (S H Kelkar) When does the inventory build unwind, and what is the net-debt target?
  7. (Gem) How much of mint-derivative demand comes from pharma, oral care and tobacco? How exposed are you to synthetic menthol?
  8. How much of your portfolio is affected by the EU allergen rules or IFRA amendments?

Red flags

  • Rising inventory with flat sales.
  • Consolidated losses hidden behind standalone profits.
  • Rising debt at an F&F house (the model should be cash-generative).
  • Stock rallies when margins are falling.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. Why do F&F houses earn higher, steadier margins than ingredient makers?

Their recipes are locked into brands through briefs, and switching is risky for the brand; ingredient makers sell molecules at global prices.

2. How is synthetic camphor made?

From alpha-pinene (in turpentine) via camphene and isoborneol to camphor.

3. What squeezed camphor makers in 2026?

Alpha-pinene rose 70–80% in five months, and domestic overcapacity slowed the pass-through to camphor prices.

4. Why did S H Kelkar's stock fall 24% despite a 78% profit jump?

Net debt rose to ₹864 cr on capacity spending and a deliberate inventory build; the market worries about cash conversion.

5. What caps natural mint prices when the UP crop fails?

Synthetic menthol from BASF, Symrise and Takasago; buyers switch when natural becomes too expensive.

6. What drove the 2017 camphor boom?

China's environmental inspections cut Chinese terpene and camphor supply while Indian demand held; Mangalam rose 373% and Kanchi 387%.

7. Why did Gem Aromatics report a consolidated loss while its standalone business made a profit?

Depreciation of ₹9.1 cr on the new Dahej facility, before it reached scale.

8. What EU rule changes F&F labels from July 2026?

Regulation 2023/1545 expands mandatory allergen labelling from 26 to about 80 substances (new products from July 2026, all by July 2028).

9. Which company in another primer arguably belongs in this one, and why?

Privi Speciality (in 008), because it is almost entirely aroma chemicals from turpentine, with a Givaudan joint venture.

10. Why should you distrust this basket's seasonal pattern?

Until 2025 it held only two or three small caps, so single-company events drive monthly results.
