Oilfield & Drilling Services

The contractors who do the physical work of the oil industry: drilling the wells, servicing them when they tire, compressing the gas, running the boats and divers offshore, shooting the seismic and making the pipe that goes down the hole. They sell days and hours, not barrels — which means they are paid whether or not the well finds anything, and they prosper when producers spend rather than when prices rise.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Selling days, not barrels

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Suppose your neighbour wants a well dug in his garden. He does not own a digging machine, and he would not know how to use one. So he hires a man with a machine, and pays him for every day the machine works — whether or not water is found. That man is an oilfield services company. He does not care much what water sells for; he cares that people keep wanting wells dug, and that there are not too many other men with machines competing for the job. When everyone wants a well at once, his daily price goes up. When nobody does, his machine sits idle and he still has to pay for it.

Say it to a client in one minute

Services are the better way to own an upstream capital-expenditure cycle, because they take activity risk instead of price risk.

  • The demand is visible in advance. These companies carry order books: Deep Industries ₹3,047 cr, Asian Energy ₹1,754 cr with a ₹3,000–4,000 cr bid pipeline. Producers give no such visibility.
  • The cycle is turning on activity, not price. ONGC has tendered for deepwater rigs in a programme worth $18–20 bn and is committing over ₹40,000 cr to Western Offshore (see 028).
  • Tightness has become the constraint. ONGC cancelled a major offshore rig tender because the market wanted $97,750 a day against the $55,000 it was prepared to pay. Good for rig owners, awkward for the programme.
  • 2026 is broad: basket +36%, median +39%, against a Nifty −11%.
  • The tail is dangerous. Aban Offshore, once the sector's giant, is in insolvency proceedings; Revathi is 80% below its peak. Asset-owning contractors die of debt, not of demand.

Analogy

An upstream producer is a farmer who owns the field; the services sector is the entire machinery-hire trade around it — the tractor on daily hire, the man who repairs the irrigation, the surveyor who maps the soil, and the shop that sells the pipe. In a good season the farmer earns more, but the hire firms fill their order books first, because the farmer has to hire before he can harvest.

Everyday example

When ONGC decides to drill in the Arabian Sea, it does not own most of what it needs. It charters a jackup rig from a contractor at a day rate, hires a support vessel to carry people and supplies, engages divers to inspect the platform's legs, buys drill pipe from a tubular maker, and pays a service firm to cement and log the well. Each of those is a listed company in this section.

Utilisation is the whole business

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • The plan: the oil company decides how many wells it wants this year.
  • The tender: it asks contractors to bid, and the cheapest qualified one wins.
  • The order book: winning means a promise of work for several years — that is what investors pay for.
  • Mobilisation: the contractor has to move the rig or boat there first, at its own expense.
  • Execution: it gets paid for each day the equipment is working, so a breakdown or a storm is its problem.
  • Renewal: when the job ends, the equipment either finds new work or sits idle, still costing money.
Fund-manager lens

Three things separate the good contractors here from the dangerous ones. Order book against revenue — Deep Industries' ₹3,047 cr book on ₹1,100 cr of guided FY27 revenue is nearly three years of visibility, which is what justifies a services multiple. Asset ownership versus asset-light service — owning rigs and vessels earns more in an upcycle and kills you in a downcycle, which is the entire history of Aban Offshore. Customer concentration — most of this sector ultimately invoices one customer, so an ONGC tender cancellation is a company event, not just an industry one.

5 · How money is made

Day rate times utilisation, minus a heavy fixed cost

The four numbers that matter

  1. Day rate: what a rig or vessel earns per working day. ONGC's cancelled tender is the cleanest data point available — willing to pay $55,000, market asking $97,750, against a global average of $112,900.
  2. Utilisation: days worked as a share of days available. SEAMEC's entire 41% revenue increase came from 110 additional deployment days.
  3. Order book and its tenure: Deep's ₹3,047 cr book with more than 60% executing over two to two-and-a-half years is what a services multiple is actually paying for.
  4. Mobilisation and refurbishment cost: spent before revenue begins — Jindal's Pioneer is being refurbished now for a contract starting in Q3 2027.
Analogy

A rig is a hotel that can be moved. Its costs barely change whether it is full or empty, so profit is almost entirely a function of occupancy and room rate. And because moving the hotel takes months and costs a fortune, a contractor will accept a poor rate rather than have it sit empty — which is why day rates overshoot in both directions.

7 · The business calendar

Tenders, monsoon and mobilisation

  • April–June: the new financial year opens budgets and tender cycles; this is when order books are refreshed.
  • June–September: the monsoon curtails offshore operations in the Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal, so deployment days fall and maintenance is scheduled.
  • October–December: the post-monsoon campaign season — the busiest offshore window, and the reason this group's fourth quarter beats the market in 11 of 14 years while the producers' does not.
  • January–March: year-end execution and settlements, historically the weakest window for the stocks.
  • Tender events: awards, cancellations and re-tenders move these shares more than any macro number, and they follow no calendar at all.

8 · What the stocks do

Seasonality test on TradingView prices

The exceptions, and why

  • April–July failed five times in fifteen years: 2019 (−22.8), 2013 (−21.3), 2018 (−16.9), 2022 (−14.1) and 2012 (−5.2). Every one of them was a year in which upstream spending plans were being cut or deferred.
  • The best runs were 2021 (+49.4) and 2016 (+31.1), both the first year of an activity recovery.
  • The fourth quarter is the distinguishing feature: October–December beat the Nifty in 11 of 14 years, with only 2014 (−30.1), 2012 (−14.7) and 2022 (−7.9) failing — and 2014 was the crude collapse.
  • February–March is brutal and consistent: 4 of 15 years, median −8.7, with 2016 (−19.0), 2025 (−15.7) and 2013 (−13.3) the worst.
How to use this

This is the most usable seasonality in the Oil & Gas family so far, because it maps onto something physical: the monsoon stops offshore work and the post-monsoon campaign restarts it, so activity — and news about activity — clusters in the fourth quarter. The combination that has worked is to own the group from April and through the October–December campaign season, and to be cautious in February–March. As always, an order-book collapse or a tender cancellation overrides the calendar.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Eight drivers

  1. Upstream capital expenditure: the demand side entirely, and it follows crude with a lag of roughly a year.
  2. Day rates: set globally for mobile assets; India competes for rigs with every other basin.
  3. Utilisation and deployment days: the operating leverage.
  4. Order book additions and tenure: visibility is what the market pays for in this sector.
  5. Tender outcomes: awards, cancellations and re-tenders — ONGC's cancelled offshore rig tender is the live example.
  6. Asset availability: a tight global rig market lifts rates but also makes expansion expensive, as SEAMEC's $70 mn vessel purchase shows.
  7. Balance sheet: leverage determines who survives the down-leg; see Aban Offshore.
  8. Diversification into mining and minerals: Asian Energy at roughly 40% mineral services and South West Pinnacle's coal and water work smooth the oil cycle.

The anatomy of a services cycle

  1. Crude rises and producers approve budgets. Nothing happens to contractors yet.
  2. Tenders are floated and awarded, typically six to eighteen months later. Order books fill and the stocks re-rate — this is the phase that produced +154% in 2021 and +116% in 2023.
  3. Assets tighten and day rates rise, which is where the profit is, and where the buyer starts to resist. ONGC's cancellation at $97,750 a day is precisely this moment.
  4. Crude falls, budgets are cut and tenders are deferred. Order books run off, assets go idle and the leveraged operators fail: 2015, 2018–19 and, mildly, 2025.

Patterns that repeat

  • Services lag producers into a cycle and beat them through it. In 2021 and 2023 this basket rose 154% and 116% against the producers' 63% and 67%.
  • The median tells you what kind of year it is. 2022 had a +12% basket and a −18% median — contract wins, not the sector. 2026 has +36% and +39%: genuinely broad.
  • Leverage decides survival, not skill. Aban Offshore and Garware Offshore were both large, competent operators; both were undone by debt against idle assets.
  • Tubulars amplify everything: Oil Country Tubular fell 68% and 61% in consecutive years and rose 123% in another.

11 · The global picture

India rents from a global fleet

The world's oilfield services industry is led by SLB, Halliburton and Baker Hughes in technical services, and by a separate set of owners — Transocean, Valaris, Noble, Seadrill and Borr among them — in offshore rigs. Those rigs are mobile, so India does not have a domestic rig market so much as a domestic share of a global one. That is why ONGC's tender economics are set in Houston and Oslo as much as in Delhi: with global day rates around $112,900 on average and the Indian tender asking for $55,000, the rigs simply went elsewhere.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • The 2008 boom and bust: Aban Offshore's leveraged fleet expansion made it one of India's most valuable companies and then destroyed it — the defining event in this sector's local history.
  • From day-rate hire to integrated contracts: production enhancement contracts, where the contractor is paid for the incremental output it delivers, shift risk and reward toward the service provider. Deep Industries' long-tenure order book is anchored on one.
  • From oil-only to multi-mineral services: Asian Energy and South West Pinnacle have deliberately diversified into mineral and coal exploration to dampen the hydrocarbon cycle.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Deepwater capability: drillships and semi-submersibles able to work in ultra-deep water — the capability India is tendering for and does not domestically own.
  2. Production enhancement contracts: paying a contractor for incremental barrels rather than for days, which aligns incentives and raises the contractor's share of the upside.
  3. Directional and horizontal drilling: more reservoir contact per well, and higher-value work per rig day.
  4. Digital drilling and predictive maintenance: reducing non-productive time, which is pure margin in a day-rate business.
  5. Subsea inspection by remote vehicles: replacing some diving work, and a capability SEAMEC and Dolphin are both building.
  6. Gas infrastructure services: compression, dehydration and processing, growing with the gas share of the energy mix (see 033 and 034).

The honest read

Services are where this cycle is actually being paid out. The producers in 028 have record profits and static share prices; here, the order books are visible, the revenue growth is real and the market is paying for both. The specific thing to watch is the tension the ONGC cancellation exposed: the buyer's price and the asset market's price are roughly $40,000 a day apart, and one of three things must give — ONGC pays more, the programme shrinks, or global rates soften. The first is good for rig owners, the second is bad for everyone here, and the third depends on crude. Meanwhile the asset-light and long-contract names — Deep's compression and production-enhancement book, Asian Energy's mineral diversification — are the ones that do not need the bottleneck resolved to keep growing. And the tail of this section is genuinely uninvestable: one company in insolvency, one up 341% for no verifiable reason, two that belong in other industries.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What is the order book, what was added this quarter, and over how many years does it execute?
  2. What proportion of the book is firm versus optional extensions?
  3. What are current day rates on renewals compared with the expiring contract?
  4. What was utilisation or deployment days, and what caused any downtime?
  5. What is the mobilisation and refurbishment spend still to come before revenue starts?
  6. What share of revenue is from a single customer, and what are receivable days on it?
  7. What is net debt against EBITDA, and how would that look at 60% utilisation?
  8. (Deep Industries) What are the economics of the production enhancement contract, and how is incremental output measured and paid?
  9. (Asian Energy) What changes after the Oilmax merger completes, and what is the pro-forma order book?

Red flags

  • An order book that grows while revenue does not — awards that never mobilise.
  • Debt raised to buy assets at the top of the day-rate cycle.
  • Receivables stretching on public-sector contracts.
  • Guidance that depends on a single tender being concluded.

What you'll hear, and the evidence

19 · Glossary

Every term you'll meet

Equipment and operations

Drilling rig
The machine and derrick that drills a well; onshore rigs are trucked in, offshore rigs float or stand on the seabed.
Jackup rig
An offshore rig with legs that jack down to the seabed, used in shallow water. Jindal Drilling's Pioneer is one.
Semi-submersible
A floating rig held in place for deeper water.
Drillship
A ship-shaped drilling vessel for the deepest water; the type ONGC is tendering for.
Workover rig
A smaller rig used to repair or re-complete an existing well rather than drill a new one.
Well intervention
Any operation on a live well to restore or raise production.
Offshore support vessel (OSV)
A ship carrying crew, supplies and equipment to offshore installations.
Multi-support vessel
A vessel equipped for diving, construction and maintenance work offshore; SEAMEC's core asset type.
Subsea and diving services
Underwater inspection, repair and maintenance of platforms and pipelines.
Remotely operated vehicle (ROV)
An unmanned submersible replacing divers for many inspection tasks.
Gas compression
Raising gas pressure so it can flow into a pipeline; Deep Industries runs more than 80 compressors.
Dehydration
Removing water from produced gas before it enters a pipeline.
OCTG
Oil country tubular goods: the casing, tubing and drill pipe used in wells.
Casing and tubing
The steel lining of a well, and the pipe through which fluids are produced.
Drill pipe
The pipe string that turns the bit and carries drilling fluid.
Mud engineering
Designing and managing drilling fluid, which cools the bit and controls well pressure.
Cementing and logging
Sealing the casing in place, and measuring the rock and fluids around the well.
Directional drilling
Steering a well away from vertical to reach more reservoir.
Seismic acquisition
Recording sound reflections to image the subsurface (see 028); Alphageo's business.
Non-productive time
Paid or unpaid hours when the rig is not drilling; the main margin leak in a day-rate business.

Commercial terms

Day rate
The daily charge for a rig or vessel with crew. ONGC's cancelled tender contrasted $55,000 offered with $97,750 asked.
Effective day rate (EDR)
The rate after adjustments and allowances; Jindal's Pioneer award is about ₹45.8 lakh, roughly $47,900.
Utilisation
Working days as a share of available days.
Deployment days
Days a vessel was actually on hire; SEAMEC gained 110 of them year on year.
Mobilisation
Moving and preparing an asset for a contract, usually at the contractor's cost and before any revenue.
Cold stacking / warm stacking
Laying up an idle rig with minimal crew, or keeping it ready to work at higher cost.
Order book
Contracted future revenue; the sector's key disclosure. Deep's stood at ₹3,047 cr at 30 June 2026.
Book-to-bill
Order additions divided by revenue executed in the same period; above one means the book is growing.
Production enhancement contract (PEC)
A contract paying a service provider for incremental output from a field rather than for days worked.
Charter hire
Payment for the use of a vessel, with or without crew.
Tender qualification
The technical and financial criteria a bidder must meet before price is even considered.
Customer concentration
Dependence on one buyer — in Indian oilfield services, usually ONGC.
Cabotage
Rules reserving coastal movements for domestically flagged vessels.
CIRP
The corporate insolvency resolution process under India's bankruptcy code; Aban Offshore is in one.
NCLAT
The appellate tribunal for insolvency matters, which rejected Aban's one-time settlement.
One-time settlement
A negotiated lump-sum repayment to creditors in place of full dues.
Non-convertible redeemable preference shares
Preference shares repayable in cash; ₹281 cr of Aban's 2014 issue remained unredeemed at April 2026.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. What are these companies actually selling?

Days and jobs — rig days, vessel days, seismic surveys, tubulars. They take activity risk, not oil-price risk.

2. Why did ONGC cancel an offshore rig tender?

Day rates: it was willing to pay $55,000 a day while the market was asking $97,750, against a global average of $112,900.

3. What is the most important disclosure in this sector?

The order book and its tenure. Deep Industries' ₹3,047 cr against ₹1,100 cr of guided FY27 revenue is close to three years of visibility.

4. Why did SEAMEC's revenue rise 41%?

110 additional vessel deployment days — utilisation, not price.

5. Why is Jindal Drilling up only 4% in a year when peers are up 60%?

Its three-year ONGC award for the Jindal Pioneer starts in Q3 2027 after refurbishment. An award is not revenue until the asset mobilises.

6. What distinguishes this sector's seasonality from the producers'?

The fourth quarter. October–December has beaten the Nifty in 11 of 14 years here, because the post-monsoon campaign season drives offshore activity, while for producers it is the weakest window.

7. What happened to Aban Offshore?

A leveraged fleet expansion before 2009, then collapse. Its one-time settlement was rejected by the NCLAT, insolvency continues under a new resolution professional, and ₹281 cr of 2014 preference shares remain unredeemed.

8. Which two companies are misclassified in this section?

Revathi Equipment, which makes mining blasthole drills, and Starlog Enterprises, a ports and logistics business.

9. What does a production enhancement contract change?

The contractor is paid for incremental output rather than for days worked, taking more risk and more of the upside.

10. Why was 2022 a misleading year for this basket?

The basket rose 12% while the median stock fell 18%: two contract winners carried it. Always check the median here.
