Power Electronics

Power electronics are the circuits that change electricity from one form to another: alternating current to direct current and back, one voltage to another, a fixed frequency to a variable one. They sit inside every UPS in a data centre, every solar inverter, every EV charger and every modern electric train. India's listed pure-plays are few — one sixty-year-old railway supplier, one young UPS and battery-system maker, and a small industrial firm — but the industry around them is being reshaped by railways, data centres and batteries.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

The gearbox of electricity

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Electricity comes in different "flavours". The power in your wall socket wiggles back and forth fifty times a second; a battery's power flows steadily in one direction; a train motor needs power that changes speed as the train speeds up. Power electronics are clever boxes that change one flavour into another, very efficiently, using special switches that turn on and off thousands of times a second. When the power goes out in a hospital and the lights stay on, a power-electronics box switched it to batteries without anyone noticing.

Say it to a client in one minute

Power electronics demand is rising with railways, data centres, batteries and renewables, but the listed pure-play universe is tiny.

  • Railways: modern trains use IGBT-based three-phase propulsion; Hirect's own propulsion system has finished 9,000–10,000 km of trials and won a ₹60 cr MEMU order in July.
  • Exports: Hirect won its first US traction-motor order and IGBT converter orders for US mines.
  • Data centres and batteries: Prostarm's revenue +38%, profit +150% in Q1; a 1.2 GWh battery plant is due by 30 September.
  • Investment costs now: Hirect's consolidated EBITDA fell 45% as it builds its propulsion business.
  • The big players are elsewhere: ABB, Siemens, Schneider, Hitachi Energy, BHEL, CG Power, Exicom and V-Guard sit in other sections.

Analogy

A power converter is the gearbox of an electrical system. An engine turns at one speed; the gearbox lets the wheels turn at whatever speed the road needs. The grid delivers one kind of power; the converter delivers exactly what the motor, battery or computer needs — and, in a modern train, sends braking energy back to the grid like an engine braking downhill.

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Switches: special chips that turn huge currents on and off very fast.
  • Helpers: coils and capacitors that smooth the power.
  • Design: engineers decide how to connect everything and how to keep it cool.
  • Building and testing: the box is built and tested — a train part may run thousands of kilometres first.
  • Fitting: it goes into a train, a data centre or a solar farm.
  • Service: it is looked after for many years.
Fund-manager lens

The critical barrier in power electronics for railways and utilities is field-proven qualification. Indian Railways buys propulsion systems only after long trials, and once a design is approved it can be ordered for years across many trainsets — so the long, expensive development period that is depressing Hirect's consolidated margins today is also what could create a durable franchise. The opposite applies to commodity UPS and inverters, where many suppliers compete on price and components are imported. When valuing a company here, separate the qualified, engineered products from the commodity ones.

The development-cost phase

Hirect's quarter illustrates a common pattern. Its established rectifier and transformer business earns a steady margin (10.6% standalone), while the new propulsion subsidiary absorbs engineers, trials and certification before orders arrive in volume — dragging the consolidated margin to 5.1%. If the propulsion system is adopted across Indian Railways' EMU, MEMU and metro fleets and wins export orders, the economics invert: development costs are spread over years of production. If it is not, the costs remain. Investors are effectively valuing an option on that outcome.

Analogy

It is like a doctor finishing years of specialist training: income is low during residency, but a qualified specialist earns far more than a general practitioner for the rest of their career — provided they pass the final exam.

Each step used to be a separate purchase; a "propulsion system" contract bundles steps 3 to 6 with the control software, which is why winning one is far more valuable than selling a single transformer. Indian Railways has moved suburban, mainline and metro trains, and the Vande Bharat trainsets, to IGBT-based three-phase propulsion with regenerative braking, so the size of this market grows with every new trainset ordered.

UPS systems are the single largest power-equipment item in a data centre by one industry estimate (about 31% of the Indian data-centre power-equipment market in 2025), and the shift from lead-acid to lithium batteries (046) is changing how they are built. Prostarm sells into this chain, mainly to commercial and industrial customers; the global leaders — Schneider, Vertiv, Eaton, ABB and Delta — dominate the largest data-centre projects.

7 · The business calendar

Railway budgets and data-centre build cycles

  • February budget: railway capital expenditure and rolling-stock plans.
  • Railway tenders: coach factories (ICF, MCF, RCF) issue propulsion and equipment tenders through the year.
  • October–March: the heaviest execution and billing period for railway suppliers.

  • Summer: demand for home inverters and UPS rises with power cuts.

With one company making up most of the record, this is closer to Hirect's own calendar than an industry pattern. It does line up with railway ordering and billing — orders and strong results in the second half, heavy year-end billing priced in early — but position sizes should reflect how thin the evidence is.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Eight drivers

  1. Railway rolling-stock programmes: Vande Bharat, EMU/MEMU and metro fleets with IGBT propulsion.
  2. Localisation of propulsion: replacing imported systems with Indian designs.
  3. Data-centre construction: UPS and power distribution (see 039, 043).
  4. Battery storage adoption: commercial and industrial users adding storage (046).
  5. Renewables: solar inverters and grid-support converters (038, 045).
  6. Industrial electrolysis: rectifiers for aluminium, chlor-alkali (003) and green hydrogen.
  7. Exports: converters and motors for overseas mining and rail.
  8. Semiconductor costs: IGBT and SiC module prices and availability.

11 · The global picture

A global oligopoly in high-power systems

High-power converters — for trains, grids and industry — are dominated by a handful of global engineering groups: Siemens, ABB, Hitachi, Alstom, Mitsubishi Electric, Toshiba and CRRC in China for railway propulsion; ABB, Siemens, Schneider, Vertiv, Eaton and Delta for UPS and industrial power. Their advantage is decades of field data and qualification. India has long relied on them for railway propulsion, often through technology transfer. The push for indigenous propulsion — by BHEL, CG Power, Medha and now Hirect — is an attempt to own the core of the country's fast-growing rolling-stock programme. Data-centre growth, battery storage and electrification are lifting demand for power electronics everywhere, and silicon carbide is changing converter design by cutting losses and size.

Export markets are where an Indian supplier's credibility is tested hardest. Mining companies and railways in the Americas, Africa and South-East Asia buy converters and traction motors on reliability records, and Chinese suppliers such as CRRC compete aggressively on price. Hirect's first US traction-motor order and its IGBT converter orders for US mines are small in value, but they matter because they are won against that competition and outside the protection of Indian procurement preferences. If they lead to repeat business, they would give the company a second demand cycle alongside Indian Railways — and reduce its dependence on the railway budget that has driven its share price for a decade.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • From thyristors to IGBTs: modern converters switch faster and more efficiently; older rectifier makers had to redesign.
  • From imported to indigenous railway propulsion: policy and cost push Indian designs.
  • From silicon to silicon carbide: smaller, more efficient converters for EVs and chargers.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Silicon carbide and gallium nitride devices: higher efficiency, smaller converters (see 050).
  2. Regenerative braking everywhere: trains and lifts returning energy to the grid.
  3. Lithium-ion UPS: replacing lead-acid batteries in data centres (046).
  4. Grid-forming inverters: stabilising grids with high renewable shares (038).
  5. Digital controls and remote monitoring: predictive maintenance on converters.
  6. High-power rectifiers for green hydrogen electrolysers.

The industrial side of the rectifier business deserves a mention too. Large rectifiers power electrolysis — smelting aluminium, making chlorine and caustic soda (003), plating metals and, increasingly, splitting water for green hydrogen. Each new smelter pot line or electrolyser plant needs rectifier capacity measured in tens of megawatts, which makes this a lumpy but valuable order stream that is largely independent of the railway cycle.

The honest read

The market has paid up for Hirect's propulsion story before the profits arrive, and the evidence so far — long trials, a first coach-factory order, export wins — supports the direction. The key test is whether propulsion orders scale across fleets fast enough to lift consolidated margins back toward the standalone business's 10–11%. Prostarm is an earlier, smaller bet on UPS and storage whose shares have fallen despite growing profits, as the market questions how much margin battery integration can earn. Beyond these two, investors wanting exposure to Indian power electronics will find more depth in the larger electrical-equipment companies in other sections.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What trials and approvals remain before fleet-wide propulsion orders?
  2. What is the pipeline of coach-factory and metro tenders you are qualified for?
  3. When will the new business break even, and what spending is still ahead?
  4. What share of converter cost is imported semiconductors, and can it be localised?
  5. What is the export order book, and in which markets?
  6. (UPS/storage) What margin do you earn on battery systems, excluding cell pass-through?
  7. How much revenue is service and spares?
  8. How are copper and component price changes passed through?

Red flags

  • Repeated slippage in trial and approval timelines.
  • Rising receivables from railway customers.
  • Battery-system revenue growing with shrinking margins.
  • Share-price moves on announcements without order values.
