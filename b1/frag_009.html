Dyes & Pigments

The chemistry of colour. Dyes soak into fabric, leather and paper; pigments sit on top as tiny coloured particles in paint, ink and plastic; food colours tint sweets and drinks. India is one of the world's two big colour factories, concentrated in a handful of Gujarat industrial estates, and it swings between boom and bust with China's pollution rules.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Making colour stick

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Think of a white T-shirt, a red wall and an orange sweet. The T-shirt got its colour from a dye: a coloured molecule that dissolves in water and grabs the cotton threads so tightly it doesn't wash out. The wall got its colour from a pigment: a coloured powder so fine it is mixed into paint and sits on the surface like glitter in glue. The sweet got its colour from a food colour, a dye clean enough to eat. All three usually start as black, smelly chemicals from coal tar and oil (naphthalene and benzene). Factories in Gujarat turn them, step by step, into bright colours. Every step makes dirty, acidic water, so whenever a government cracks down on pollution, factories shut, colour runs short, and the factories still running make a lot of money.

Say it to a client in one minute

This is three businesses under one name. Dyes and dye intermediates are textile-linked and cyclical: India leads reactive dyes for cotton, China leads disperse dyes for polyester, and profits swing with pollution enforcement. China's closures in 2014–17 made Indian makers rich; India's own green-tribunal curbs in 2019 hurt them; the 2026 H-acid spike (about 40,000 to 60,000–70,000 yuan/t) has brought the boom back, and Bodal's profit nearly tripled. Pigments are steadier and more global. Sudarshan became the world's number two by buying Heubach, and profit doubled in Q1 FY27, but its margin is still only about 10%. Food colours (Vidhi, Dynemic, Deepak Chemtex) face a US push away from petroleum-based dyes. Two special situations dominate the headlines. Kiri got $689 m for DyStar and is spending it on a ₹12,000 cr copper complex, which the market punishes. Several micro caps have tripled with no news, which is a warning sign rather than a thesis.

The dye families, matched to the fibre they colour

  • Reactive dyes: form a chemical bond with cotton and viscose, so they don't wash out. They are India's biggest dye family and the reason H-acid and vinyl sulphone matter.
  • Disperse dyes: tiny particles that dissolve into polyester at high temperature. China dominates, supplying about 47% of world disperse-dye exports in 2024.
  • Acid dyes: for nylon, wool, silk and leather; metal-complex versions give deep, fast shades.
  • Direct, sulphur and vat dyes: cheaper cotton dyes. Sulphur black colours cheap black cottons; indigo, a vat dye, colours denim.
  • Basic (cationic) dyes: for acrylic fibres and paper.
  • Optical brighteners: not colours, but molecules that absorb invisible ultraviolet light and give off blue light, which makes whites look whiter. They are in detergents and paper.

The pigment families

  • Organic azo pigments: the yellows, oranges and reds of printing inks and plastics. They are cheap and made in volume.
  • Phthalocyanines: the blues (Pigment Blue 15) and greens (Pigment Green 7) in almost every ink and paint. They start from CPC blue (Asahi Songwon, AksharChem, Ishan, Kesar).
  • High-performance pigments: quinacridones, DPP reds and perylenes for car paints and outdoor plastics. They survive years of sun and heat and earn the best margins.
  • Inorganic pigments: ultramarine blue (Ultramarine & Pigments), iron oxides, chrome yellows, and titanium dioxide, the white (see 004).
  • Effect pigments: pearlescent and metallic flakes for cosmetics and car finishes (Sudarshan).
Analogy

A dye is like tea staining a cup: the colour goes into the material. A pigment is like paint on a fence: the colour sits on top and needs a binder to stay there. A food colour is a tea you are allowed to drink.

Everyday examples

The blue printed on many soft-drink cans and the cyan cartridge in an office printer are phthalocyanine blue. The red of a new car is often a DPP or quinacridone pigment. A maroon cotton kurta was almost certainly dyed with a reactive dye whose backbone started as H-acid. Your jeans are indigo. Traditional laundry blue (neel) is ultramarine. The orange of a jelly sweet in the US may be Yellow 6, one of the six colours the FDA wants out of the food supply by end-2026.

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Feedstocks: the flour and sugar. Naphthalene is the white stuff in old-fashioned mothballs; it comes from tar left over when coal is baked into coke for steel mills.
  • Intermediates: the dough. Naphthalene is soaked in strong acid (sulphonation), treated with nitric acid (nitration, see 008), then reduced and cooked again until it becomes H-acid. Every step leaves acidic, coloured water that must be cleaned before it can be released. That is why this step closes first when inspectors arrive.
  • Dyes: the dough becomes a dish. Two intermediates are joined in an ice-cold reaction (diazotisation and coupling, see 008) to make an azo dye. A "hook" (vinyl sulphone) is then attached so the dye can grab cotton.
  • Pigments: a separate kitchen that makes colours which never dissolve. The skill is grinding and finishing the particles to exactly the right size, because size decides the shade and the strength.
  • Food colours: the same kind of chemistry, but cleaned to pharmaceutical purity and certified batch by batch.
  • Users: a dyeing house in Surat buys a 25 kg drum of reactive red, dissolves it in hot water with salt and soda ash (see 003), and pushes cotton cloth through it.
Analogy

Think of a bakery chain. Most of the flour mess is made at the dough stage. When the city inspector shuts down messy dough-makers, bread makers across town have to pay whatever the surviving dough-makers ask. That is dye intermediates in 2016–17 and again in 2026.

Fund-manager lens

Profit pools at the intermediate step when supply is disrupted, because it is the dirtiest and most regulated step. It also pools in pigments when a company has approvals with global paint, ink and plastics makers. Pure dye makers sit in the middle, squeezed between intermediate costs and thousands of fragmented, credit-hungry textile processors. The best position is integration, making both the intermediate and the dye (Bodal, Kiri), because it captures whichever margin is fat at the time.

Three things that make or break a year

  1. Who else is allowed to run. The industry's supply is not set by capacity on paper. It is set by capacity that the pollution board lets operate. A single closure order in Zhejiang or Vapi changes the price more than a year of demand growth.
  2. Working capital. Dye makers sell to thousands of small processors through dealers, on long credit. Receivables of three months or more are common, so a textile slowdown shows up as unpaid bills before it shows up as lower sales.
  3. Effluent capacity. A plant's output is capped by how much waste water its treatment plant and the cluster's common plant (CETP) can take. A company that has built zero-liquid-discharge (ZLD, see 008) and handles its own spent acid can run while neighbours are shut.
Analogy

Intermediates are like a toll bridge. When a rival bridge closes (a pollution shutdown in China or Gujarat), everyone has to use yours and you can raise the toll. When the other bridge reopens, the toll falls back, but you still have to maintain your bridge. Bodal's profit tripling in Q1 FY27 is a toll-raising quarter; the question is always when the other bridge reopens.

Fund-manager lens

In 2026 input costs rose almost everywhere, yet the intermediate makers made record profits because they could pass costs on. 7 · The business calendar

Textile seasons drive dyes

  • July–October: processors dye fabric for Indian festive (Diwali) and wedding demand, and for Western autumn–winter orders. This is the dye makers' busiest shipping stretch.
  • January–March: spring–summer export orders and summer cottons.
  • Chinese New Year (January–February): Chinese plants shut for two to four weeks, so dye and intermediate prices often firm. Longsheng's February 2026 price rise is a typical example.
  • Monsoon (June–September): Gujarat's effluent systems are under most strain, and discharge checks and temporary closures cluster in these months.
  • Pigments follow paint seasons (pre-Diwali repainting, see 013), global packaging and ink demand, and car production.
  • Food colours follow confectionery and beverage seasons (summer drinks, festive sweets) and US and EU regulatory calendars.

Seasonality test on TradingView prices

The exceptions, and why

  • April misses (4 of 15). Each had a market-wide cause:
    • 2012 (−3.8): Kiri's collapse.
    • 2013 (−6.0): the run-up to the rupee crisis.
    • 2019 (−4.1): a small-cap bear market plus pollution curbs in Gujarat's clusters.
    • 2025 (−6.4): the US "reciprocal" tariffs announced on 2 April hit textile-linked stocks.
    The best Aprils were 2026 (+19.9), 2016 (+19.3) and 2014 (+17.9), all years when Chinese or Indian supply was tight.
  • April–July failures: 2012 (−19.9), 2013 (−15.1), 2018 (−11.7), 2019 (−22.8), 2022 (−0.4). Each was a balance-sheet shock (Kiri 2012), a rupee or small-cap shock (2013, 2018), a regulatory shock (the NGT order of July 2019) or a demand slump (2022). The biggest April–July runs were 2016 (+58.0) and 2014 (+46.6), both during China's pollution war.
  • May is the weakest month (3 of 15). The one big exception, 2025 (+13.8), was a rebound from the April tariff shock.
  • February–March beat the Nifty only in 2014 (+16.1) and 2021 (+5.0). The worst were 2023 (−12.3) and 2024 (−11.7).
  • October–December is a coin toss (6 of 14), but its outliers are large: 2015 (+40.3) and 2013 (+22.6), each ahead of a big following year. The big misses were 2020 (−9.6) and 2022 (−9.9).
  • December beat the Nifty in 12 of 15 years.
How to use this

The edge is April–July: a median of +13.9 points in 10 of 15 years. The weak stretch is February–May outside April. But the annual result is set by who is allowed to run plants (China's and Gujarat's regulators) and by dispersion: in 2026 the best stock rose 331% and the worst of the basket fell 27%. Use the calendar for timing, and intermediate prices and company results for selection. Micro caps that triple without news are not part of the seasonal case.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Eight drivers

  1. Pollution enforcement in China: the 2014 Environmental Protection Law, the 2016–18 central inspections, the 2019 Xiangshui explosion (which closed a Jiangsu chemical park) and the 2021 energy curbs each cut dye and intermediate supply. This is the single biggest swing factor.
  2. Pollution enforcement in India: Gujarat's pollution board and the National Green Tribunal (NGT). The July 2019 NGT order froze expansion in critically polluted areas, including Vapi, Ankleshwar and Vatva. Closure notices hit whole clusters at once.
  3. Intermediate prices: H-acid, vinyl sulphone and CPC blue. In 2026 H-acid rose from ~40,000 to 60,000–70,000 yuan/t in China. Reactive dyes take over 80% of H-acid demand, so reactive-dye prices follow.
  4. Textile demand: Indian, Bangladeshi and Vietnamese processing volumes; Western retailer inventory cycles (the 2022–23 destocking); festive demand at home.
  5. US trade policy: 50% tariffs on Indian goods from 27 August 2025 hit textile exporters and so dye demand. They were cut to 18% in February 2026 and to about 10% after the Supreme Court ruling (see 008).
  6. Input and energy costs: naphthalene, benzene, sulphuric acid and coal. The 2026 Hormuz disruption lifted all of them (see 002, 004).
  7. Paint, ink, plastics and auto demand for pigments; restructuring of the global pigment industry (Clariant → Heubach → Sudarshan; BASF → DIC).
  8. Regulation of food colours: the US FDA's April 2025 plan, EU warning labels and state bans shift demand from synthetic to natural colours.

The anatomy of a colour cycle

  1. A regulator shuts capacity (China 2014–17, 2021; Gujarat 2019). Intermediate prices jump first, then dyes.
  2. Survivors re-price. Integrated makers with their own effluent systems earn scarcity margins. Stocks double in a year (2014, 2016, 2026).
  3. Capacity comes back. Chinese plants relocate to compliant chemical parks and restart; Indian makers add capacity. Prices fall faster than costs, and stocks give back 25–40% (2018–19, 2022).
  4. A long, quiet middle. Commodity pricing, with rallies led by special situations (Kiri's DyStar case, Sudarshan's Heubach deal) rather than by the industry.

Patterns that repeat

  • Price spikes are short; closures are the signal. The best years started with a regulatory action, not with demand.
  • The average lies. In 2025 the basket's average was +12% but the median stock fell 14%, because one micro cap (Indokem) rose 537%. Always check the median.
  • Special situations dominate large caps. Kiri (DyStar, then copper) and Sudarshan (Heubach) moved on corporate events far more than on dye prices.
  • Food colours move on regulation, not on the textile cycle: Vidhi rose on packaged-food demand in 2020–21 and fell on the FDA plan in 2025.
  • Unexplained micro-cap rallies end badly often enough to matter: Bhatia Colour Chem went from ₹430 to about ₹27.

11 · The global picture

Germany invented it, China and India make it

The synthetic dye industry began in 1856, when 18-year-old William Perkin made mauveine by accident while trying to make quinine. Germany's BASF, Bayer and Hoechst and Switzerland's Ciba, Geigy and Sandoz dominated for over a century. From the 1990s, environmental costs pushed the dirty steps to Asia, first the intermediates, then the dyes. Today Europe keeps brands, patents and high-performance pigments; Asia does the chemistry.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • Europe → China (1990s–2000s): cost and pollution rules. Many developed countries stopped making H-acid and vinyl sulphone because the effluent is toxic and hard to break down.
  • China → India, partly (2014–21): China's crackdowns, then China+1 buying. Estimates from the 2010s put world vinyl sulphone output at about 10,000 t a month, with China making about 6,000 t and India, mainly Gujarat, most of the rest. Chinese dye makers even imported Indian intermediates.
  • Ownership moves east (2010–25): DyStar went to Longsheng and Kiri in 2010 after its insolvency. BASF's pigments went to DIC in 2021, Clariant's pigments to Heubach in 2022, and Heubach to Sudarshan in 2025. Europe's colour heritage now largely belongs to Asian companies.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Digital textile printing: inkjet printers put dye inks straight onto fabric, replacing screens and long dye runs. This favours ink makers (Jaysynth) over bulk dye sellers, and it uses less water.
  2. Low-water and waterless dyeing: supercritical CO₂ dyeing of polyester and dope dyeing (colouring the polymer before spinning, with pigments rather than dyes) cut effluent. This is a slow threat to disperse-dye volumes.
  3. Cleaner intermediates: recovering and reusing spent acid, and joining vinyl sulphone and H-acid production to recycle by-products. Research shows large waste cuts; this is the licence to operate in Gujarat.
  4. Liquid dyes and brand chemical lists: ZDHC and brand restricted-substance lists favour documented, tested suppliers over the anonymous small maker.
  5. High-performance pigments for cars and electronics: heat- and weather-resistant colours, and near-infrared-reflective blacks that let car sensors "see" dark vehicles.
  6. Natural and bio-based colours: plant extracts (anthocyanins, curcumin, annatto, spirulina) and fermentation-made colours replacing synthetic food dyes. This is a risk for pure synthetic sellers and an opportunity for those with natural ranges (Vidhi).

16 · Adjacent industries and proxies

Where else the colour cycle shows up

  • Textile processing and garments: the biggest dye customers. A dye price spike squeezes processors, and weak processing hurts dye volumes.
  • Paints and inks (013): pigment customers. Pigment costs are a meaningful slice of a paint's raw-material bill.
  • Coal-tar chemicals (Himadri): the naphthalene supply.
  • Sulphuric acid and bromine (004): shared inputs.
  • Chlor-alkali and soda ash (003): used in the dye bath.
  • Fertilisers (014): Shree Pushkar's spent acid becomes SSP; Kiri's planned refinery has a fertiliser tie-in.
  • Specialty chemicals (008): the same unit processes, and Atul's colours division.
  • Copper (non-ferrous metals primer): Kiri's future.
  • Water treatment and effluent engineering: compliance spending rises with each crackdown.
  • Food and beverage brands: reformulation away from synthetic colours.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What share of your intermediates is captive? What are today's H-acid and vinyl sulphone spreads versus the five-year average?
  2. How much of this quarter's growth is price and how much is volume?
  3. What is your effluent treatment capacity, is it ZLD, and when was your consent to operate last renewed? Any closure notices in five years?
  4. What happens to your spent acid and gypsum?
  5. What is the export share, and to which countries? How much depends on US-bound textiles?
  6. What are debtor days with processors and dealers, and how did they move this year?
  7. (Sudarshan) When do Heubach margins reach group targets, and what restructuring costs remain?
  8. (Kiri) How will the copper complex be funded beyond the DyStar cash? Why no dividend? Who are the inter-corporate loans to?
  9. (Vidhi, Dynemic) What share of sales is to US food customers, and how fast is the switch to natural colours?

Red flags

  • Closure notices or pollution-board penalties.
  • Profit driven by other income rather than operations (Kiri's Q1 FY27).
  • Large diversification away from the core business.
  • A stock that triples with no results, orders or filings to explain it.
  • Steady daily falls with no disclosure (Bhatia Colour Chem).
  • Auditor changes at small companies. These are sometimes routine, but check why.

18 · Myths and live debates

What you'll hear, and the evidence

ClaimEvidence
"Kiri is cheap because of its cash."The cash is real ($689 m received), but the market marks it down because management is spending it on an unrelated ₹12,000 cr copper project and pays no dividend. Q1 FY27 profit was mostly other income. It is cheap only if copper earns its cost of capital.
"Sudarshan is now a global specialty pigment play."It has the scale (#2 globally) and profit doubled, but a ~10% EBITDA margin is still well below specialty levels, and restructuring costs continue.
"Dyes are a structural China+1 winner."Only when China tightens supply. Between shocks (2018–19, 2022–25) Chinese pricing dominated and the basket lagged.
"The 2026 rally means the industry has turned."The intermediate makers' results support it (Bodal, Bhageria), but much of the stock-price gain is in micro caps with no news. The median stock is up 30%, not 48%.
"The FDA plan will kill synthetic food colours."It is voluntary, covers the US only, and runs to end-2026. Vidhi's revenue still grew 66% in Q1 FY27. The real risk is slow erosion of volumes and mix, not a cliff.
"Pollution rules are bad for Indian dye makers."Bad for small, non-compliant ones; good for large integrated players, whose rivals shut. The 2014–17 boom was built on rules, not demand.

19 · Glossary

Every term you'll meet

Colour chemistry

Dye
A soluble colourant that bonds with or soaks into the material.
Pigment
An insoluble coloured particle held on a surface by a binder.
Colourant
The umbrella term for dyes and pigments.
Chromophore
The part of a molecule that absorbs light and gives colour (for example, the azo group).
Azo
A nitrogen–nitrogen double bond; the most common family of dyes and organic pigments.
Coupling
The step after diazotisation (see 008) that joins two intermediates into an azo dye.
Sulphonation
Treating a molecule with sulphuric acid or oleum (see 004) to add sulphonic groups, which make dyes water-soluble; the main source of spent acid.
Reactive dye
Forms a chemical bond with cotton; India's main dye family.
Disperse dye
Dissolves into polyester at high temperature; China dominates.
Acid dye
For nylon, wool, silk and leather.
Direct dye
A simple, cheaper cotton dye with lower wash fastness.
Vat dye
Insoluble dye made temporarily soluble for dyeing; indigo for denim.
Sulphur dye
A cheap dye mainly for black and dark cotton.
Basic (cationic) dye
For acrylic fibres and paper.
Metal-complex dye
An acid dye with a metal atom for deeper, faster shades.
Optical brightener
A molecule that turns ultraviolet into blue light so whites look whiter.
Fastness
How well a colour survives washing, light, rubbing or sweat.
Fixation rate
The share of dye that ends up on the fibre; the rest goes to effluent.
Colour Index (C.I.) name
The standard code for a colourant, such as Pigment Blue 15 or Reactive Red 195.

Intermediates and pigments

Naphthalene
A coal-tar aromatic; the starting point for H-acid and other naphthalene acids.
H-acid
1-amino-8-naphthol-3,6-disulphonic acid; the key intermediate for reactive and direct dyes, and one of the most polluting to make. Reactive dyes take over 80% of demand.
Gamma acid, J-acid, K-acid
Other naphthalene-based dye intermediates.
Vinyl sulphone (VS) ester
Para-base ester; the "hook" that lets a reactive dye bond with cotton.
Beta-naphthol
An intermediate for azo pigments and dyes.
CPC blue
Crude copper phthalocyanine, made from phthalic anhydride, urea and a copper salt; the base for blue and green pigments.
Phthalocyanine blue / green
Pigment Blue 15 and Pigment Green 7; the most used blues and greens in inks and paints.
High-performance pigment (HPP)
Pigments with superior heat, light and weather resistance (quinacridones, DPP, perylenes).
Quinacridone / DPP
High-performance reds and violets; DPP is the "Ferrari red" family.
Effect pigment
Pearlescent or metallic flakes.
Ultramarine
A blue inorganic pigment made from clay, soda ash, sulphur and carbon.
Lake
A food colour fixed on aluminium hydroxide so it does not bleed; used in tablets and coated sweets.
FD&C / E-number colours
US and EU codes for approved food colours (for example, FD&C Red 40 = E129).
Natural colours
Plant- or microbe-derived colours: anthocyanins, curcumin, annatto, spirulina.
Dope dyeing
Colouring synthetic fibre before it is spun.
Digital textile printing
Inkjet printing of dye inks directly onto fabric.

Regulation and business terms

ETP / CETP
Effluent treatment plant; common effluent treatment plant serving a whole industrial cluster.
Spent acid
Dilute, contaminated acid left after sulphonation; must be reused, neutralised or sold (for example, into SSP fertiliser).
Gypsum sludge
The solid waste made when spent acid is neutralised with lime.
Consent to operate (CTO)
The pollution board's permission to run a plant; withdrawal means closure.
CEPI
Comprehensive Environmental Pollution Index; CPCB's score for industrial clusters. "Critically polluted" areas face expansion curbs.
NGT
National Green Tribunal, India's environmental court.
ZDHC / MRSL
Zero Discharge of Hazardous Chemicals, a brand-led programme, and its Manufacturing Restricted Substances List.
REACH azo-amine restriction
EU ban on textiles and leather dyed with azo dyes that release any of 22 listed aromatic amines.
Central environmental inspection
China's 2016–18 national pollution campaign that shut thousands of chemical plants.
Scarcity margin
Extra profit earned while rivals are shut (see 002).
Other income
Income from outside operations (interest, treasury gains); Kiri's Q1 FY27 profit was mostly this.
Inter-corporate loan (ICD)
A loan from one company to another; a governance question when large.
Minority oppression
The legal claim that controlling shareholders treated a minority unfairly; the basis of Kiri's DyStar case.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. What's the difference between a dye and a pigment?

A dye dissolves and bonds with or soaks into the material; a pigment is an insoluble particle held on the surface by a binder.

2. Why do dye intermediates earn the biggest profits in a boom?

They are the dirtiest, most regulated step, so closures hit them first; survivors with their own effluent systems can raise prices (the toll-bridge effect).

3. What set off the 2014–17 boom in Indian dye stocks?

China's pollution war: the 2014 Environmental Protection Law and the 2016–18 central inspections shut thousands of Chinese dye and intermediate plants.

4. Why did the basket fall 25% in 2019 even though China's Xiangshui explosion tightened supply?

India's own NGT order froze expansion in critically polluted areas like Vapi and Ankleshwar, demand slowed, and small caps were in a bear market.

5. What drove Bodal's record Q1 FY27?

Higher volumes and realisations, with rising raw-material costs fully passed on as H-acid prices in China rose from ~40,000 to 60,000–70,000 yuan/t; its new Saykha benzene unit also contributed.

6. Why did Kiri's stock fall after it received $689 m?

It is spending the cash on an unrelated ~₹12,000 cr copper complex, pays no dividend, and its Q1 FY27 profit was mostly other income.

7. What did Sudarshan buy, and what is the remaining question?

Germany's Heubach Group (which included Clariant's pigments), making it the world's #2 pigment maker; the question is whether a ~10% EBITDA margin can reach specialty levels.

8. Why was the 2025 basket average (+12%) misleading?

One micro cap, Indokem, rose 537%; the median stock fell 14%.

9. What does the FDA's April 2025 plan ask for, and who is exposed?

A voluntary phase-out of six petroleum-based food dyes by end-2026 (plus revoking two others); synthetic food-colour exporters like Vidhi, Dynemic and Deepak Chemtex.

10. Which window has been reliable for these stocks, and which has not?

April–July (10 of 15 years, median +13.9 points); February–March beat the Nifty in only 2 of 15 years.

11. Why does Shree Pushkar also sell fertiliser?

It routes spent sulphuric acid from dye-intermediate making into single super phosphate, turning a waste problem into a product.
