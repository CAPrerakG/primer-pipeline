Every family in this series has a section called "everything else", and in Metals this is it. 1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Five different businesses under one heading

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Some rocks are useful not because we melt metal out of them but because of what they are. A special clay swells up when it gets wet, so it seals ponds and holds foundry sand together. Chalk-like powder makes paint white and plastic strong. Brown coal burns in factory boilers. A company that digs these up is like a farmer, but the farm never grows back: every tonne sold is a tonne gone. The farmers who do best are the ones who either own a field nobody else has, or who take ordinary rock and grind, wash and cook it into something a paint factory will pay extra for.

Say it to a client in one minute

The table sorts into three groups, and the grouping matters more than any single mineral. Bulk minerals sold close to the pit — lignite, ore-grade bauxite, aggregates — are priced like commodities and win on cost and freight. Functional minerals — bentonite, kaolin, calcium carbonate — are bought for a property such as swelling, whiteness or particle size, and value is added in the processing plant rather than the mine. Mineral-derived chemicals — synthetic rutile, ferric chloride, calcined bauxite — are really chemical-process businesses whose feedstock happens to be a mineral. The deeper into that third group a company goes, the less its fortunes depend on what it owns in the ground.

The chain in one breath

  • Explore to prove a deposit and its grade.
  • Secure a lease, increasingly by auction.
  • Extract at the lowest cost per tonne.
  • Beneficiate to a saleable grade.
  • Process into a product a paint, pigment or foundry customer will pay a premium for.
  • Ship it — and for a low-value mineral, the freight often decides whether it can be sold at all.
Fund-manager lens

For any industrial-minerals company, find the stage of this chain where it earns its margin, then ask whether that stage is protected. A lease is protected by law until the law changes — as Cochin Minerals found in 2019 and GMDC could find if lignite's reservation is ever reopened. A processing skill is protected by customer qualification, which is slower to build and slower to lose. A logistics position is protected by infrastructure: Ashapura's Guinea margin exists because it built and expanded a port. Revenue, tonnage and reserves all come after this question.

Three of the sixteen are not mining companies. D & H India makes welding electrodes, so it belongs in Welding & Cutting Equipment, which is still queued. Garnet International is an investment company with a garment subsidiary; the only link to minerals is its name. International Combustion builds screens, crushers and gear motors for mineral processors. It is kept out of the basket for the same reason Foseco was kept out of castings (078): a supplier to an industry is not the industry. All three are worth reclassifying in the source file.

Four have no operating business worth pricing. Neelkanth Rockminerals reported nil operating revenue in every year from FY21 to FY26, yet it is up 226% this year and trades near its all-time high. That is a takeover story in a shell, not a mining signal. Kachchh Minerals had nil sales in FY26, and Rajdarshan had ₹0.57 cr. Three margin structures, side by side

Ashapura's Guinea business earns a margin per tonne. On a seaborne bauxite price of about $38–39 a tonne FOB, it earned $6.3 of EBITDA per tonne in Q1 FY27, and it guides to $5.5–6 until freight normalises. That is a trading-and-logistics margin on someone else's commodity. It scales with tonnes and with port capacity, and it is exposed to freight, diesel and the Guinean state. The group's 11.7% EBITDA margin is simply what $6 on a $40-plus landed price looks like.

20 Microns earns a conversion margin. Its raw material is limestone, marble or clay that costs little at the pit. What the customer pays for is fineness, whiteness and consistency, and that is what supports a steady 12–14% operating margin through FY21–FY27, despite a doubling of revenue. It is a small version of a specialty chemicals business (008), and it should be valued like one: on customer approvals and capacity, not on reserves.

GMDC earns a reserved-mineral rent. Lignite in Gujarat is mined almost entirely by the state company, and its price follows imported coal. When imported coal spiked in FY23, GMDC's operating margin reached 38% and profit ₹1,204 cr. When coal normalised, the margin fell to 18% in FY26. The rent is real, but it is set by a coal price GMDC does not control, and its power segment loses money.

The test to apply

Ask of any company in this section: if its best lease were taken away tomorrow, what would be left? For 20 Microns, most of the business — its plants, approvals and customers would survive and it would buy clay elsewhere. For Cochin Minerals, which already buys all its ilmenite, the same. For Ashapura, the Guinea concession is almost everything. For GMDC, lignite is almost everything. Mining rents are real, but they are political assets, and in India they have been reallocated before.

Three consequences follow for anyone using this section as an "industrial minerals" exposure.

  1. The basket is not a mineral-price trade. No single commodity drives it. Lignite follows imported coal, Guinean bauxite follows Chinese alumina demand, and calcium carbonate follows the paint and plastics cycle. That is why no commodity-price column appears in the history table — there is no honest candidate.
  2. The best decade-long returns came from moving away from Indian mining. Ashapura's +298% (2012), +301% (2020), +339% (2023) and +126% (2025) are the biggest single-name years in the basket. The last three all trace to Guinea, where volumes, port capacity and freight matter far more than anything happening in Kutch.
  3. The critical-minerals theme is mostly a promise here, not a revenue line. GMDC's rare-earth project and Midwest's Sri Lankan sands are development projects. When the market paid for them — GMDC's run to ₹747 in April 2026, Midwest's to ₹1,804 in January — it was paying for an option. Both stocks have since given back a quarter to a half of those highs.

This is the Metals family's closing lesson restated for non-metallic minerals. On 077 it read: interchangeability is the risk, not proximity to the commodity. In this section the least interchangeable products are 20 Microns' qualified grades and Midwest's single-belt granite. The most interchangeable are Guinean bauxite and bought-in ilmenite. Returns over the full period line up with that ranking less well than in metals, because Ashapura's Guinea build-out was a volume story large enough to swamp everything else.

What the listed names actually hold

  • GMDC is developing Ambadungar, one of India's largest rare-earth deposits, with pilot validation under way. It has signed agreements with NMDC for the rare-earth value chain and, according to its Q1 FY27 disclosures, with IREL. It has also announced a coal-to-chemicals tie-up with GNFC. None of these produces revenue yet.
  • Midwest holds four exploration licences in Sri Lanka for heavy mineral sands, including monazite, the usual rare-earth source mineral. Construction is planned from October 2026.
  • Cochin Minerals & Rutile is the one company already working in the beach-sand chain, and it is the one the policy shut out. After the Centre's 2019 order reserving atomic-mineral beach-sand mining for the public sector, Kerala cancelled the mining contract of its subsidiary in December 2023 (see Onmanorama's account).
How to use this

Separate the three layers every time. Policy is real and well funded: ₹34,300 cr for the mission and ₹7,280 cr for magnets. Projects are real but early. Auction success has run at 63%, which means more than a third of blocks offered found no acceptable bidder, and a rare-earth deposit takes years to become a separated oxide and longer to become a magnet. Revenue in this section today is lignite, bauxite, fillers, pigment feedstock and stone. When GMDC traded at ₹747 in April 2026, the market was capitalising layer two as if it were layer three. The stock is 25% below that level, and the gap is a fair measure of how the market has since discounted the option.

7 · The business calendar

The monsoon, the Chinese year, and the Indian fiscal year

  • January–February: Chinese New Year shuts buyers of bauxite and many functional minerals; Indian customers start running down inventory before 31 March. February has beaten the Nifty in only 2 of 15 years.
  • March: fiscal year-end destocking and small-cap tax-loss selling; 5 of 15.
  • April: new annual contracts and fresh budgets, and the dry season for quarrying; 13 of 15, averaging +8.1 points.
  • June–September: the monsoon. Opencast and stone quarries slow or stop. ASI Industries' September quarter shows it plainly — ₹20.56 cr of revenue and an operating loss in Q2 FY26, against ₹50.70 cr in the December quarter. Guinea also has a rainy season that slows bauxite loading.
  • October–December: quarries reopen, construction restarts and Chinese buyers restock before their New Year; 10 of 14, and December 11 of 15.

8 · What the stocks do

Seasonality test on TradingView prices

The exceptions, and why they matter

  • February–March's three good years were 2012, 2015 (+7.2) and 2022. 2012 was the start of a sharp rebound from a depressed base. 2022 was the energy squeeze that followed the invasion of Ukraine, when GMDC's lignite suddenly looked cheap against imported coal. In each case something outside the calendar overwhelmed it.
  • Its worst were 2013 (−27.4), 2024 (−18.9) and 2025 (−17.5). The last two were the small-cap corrections of early 2024 and early 2025, which hit a basket of small and mid caps harder than the index.
  • April's two failures were 2019 (−7.6) and 2013 (−0.2), both in years when the whole basket was falling. Its best were 2026 (+29.2), 2021 (+22.5) and 2016 (+16.9). April 2026 was GMDC's rare-earth run to its all-time high.
How to use this

This is the same first-quarter weakness found in almost every metals basket (064–077), and here it is stronger than in any of them: 3 of 15 for February–March. The mechanism is plausible and has two parts — Chinese New Year for export minerals, and Indian fiscal-year-end destocking for domestic ones — and both are checkable in company volumes. Two cautions. First, this is a five-name basket with two small caps, and a large part of the effect is small-cap seasonality: the same April strength appears in castings (078), recycling (076) and specialty metals (075). Second, a calendar signal is for timing a decision already made on fundamentals, never for making one. The practical use is to avoid adding to these names in February, and to expect their news flow to disappoint then.

9 · What moves margins

Nine drivers

  1. Imported coal prices. Set the ceiling for GMDC's lignite price — FY23's 38% margin and FY26's 18% are the two ends.
  2. Seaborne bauxite price and Chinese alumina demand. Ashapura's revenue per tonne; prices near four-year lows in 2026 on Guinean oversupply.
  3. Ocean freight. The difference between $6 and $10 of EBITDA per tonne at Ashapura.
  4. Diesel. Every quarry and opencast mine; cited by Midwest this quarter.
  5. Paint, plastics and paper demand. 20 Microns' end markets; see paints (013).
  6. Titanium pigment demand. Synthetic rutile prices follow the TiO2 cycle.
  7. Real estate and infrastructure activity. Kota stone, granite and aggregates.
  8. Royalty and statutory levies. Set by states; changes go straight to margin.
  9. Other income. For GMDC and ASI, a large enough share of profit that it needs its own line in any model.

The anatomy of the cycles

  1. Boom and bust from a low base (2012–2014): +123% in 2012, then −26% in 2013 and a flat 2014 while the Nifty rose 31%. Mining stocks missed the 2014 election rally entirely.
  2. The small-cap cycle (2015–2019): strong in 2015 and 2017, then two losing years in 2018–19 in which every name fell. For this basket that period says more about liquidity than about minerals.
  3. The Guinea and energy years (2020–2023): Ashapura's bauxite exports and the 2022 coal squeeze produced four straight winning years, ending in +129% in 2023.
  4. The rare-earth premium and its fade (2024–2026): China's controls re-rated GMDC and Ashapura in 2025; 2026 has taken much of it back.

Patterns that repeat

  • One company's big year often is the basket's year. Ashapura supplied the largest single contribution in 2012, 2020, 2023 and 2025. With five equal-weighted names, a +300% year adds about 60 points to the basket on its own.
  • GMDC trades on its dividend and its themes, not its tonnes. It rose 99% in 2022 (coal squeeze), 178% in 2023 (PSU re-rating) and 86% in 2025 (rare earths). It fell 45% in 2013 and 47% in 2018.
  • 20 Microns is the low-volatility name. Its worst year since 2014 was −28%, and its operating margin has stayed in a 12–14% band for six years.
  • Cochin Minerals moves on its own history. It rose 56% in 2018, when everything else fell, and 136% in 2022 on its record FY23. It is often negatively correlated with the rest of the basket.

Guinea deserves its own paragraph, because it is now the single largest driver of this section's biggest mover. Its exports have grown so fast that bauxite has moved into oversupply, with prices falling to four-year lows in the $32–38 a tonne range. The state has also shown it will act: two operators had licences revoked in May 2025 before resuming late in the year. An Indian investor holding Ashapura therefore holds three things at once — a volume growth story, a commodity price near a cyclical low, and West African sovereign risk. The cut to 9–11 Mt for FY27 is the first sign that the growth story can slip.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Finer and treated fillers. Nano-sized and surface-treated calcium carbonate and kaolin replacing costlier titanium dioxide in paint and plastics. This is 20 Microns' whole growth thesis, and why its value is in the plant.
  2. Rare-earth separation. Turning monazite and other ores into individual separated oxides — the step China dominates and India's magnet scheme depends on.
  3. Chloride-route titanium pigment. Chloride-process TiO2 needs high-grade feedstock such as synthetic rutile, which supports demand for Cochin Minerals' product.
  4. Electric haulage and diesel substitution. Midwest used part of its IPO proceeds for electric mining trucks; diesel is the cost every quarry cites.
  5. Quartz for solar and electronics. High-purity quartz for glass and silicon is Midwest's second growth leg.
  6. Drone and satellite exploration. Faster, cheaper mapping ahead of auctions — Matrix Geo's business, and part of CMPDI's.

What is priced in

For Ashapura, a lot less than in January. At ₹525 against a ₹902 high, the market has repriced the stock for the FY27 volume cut and for EBITDA per tonne stuck near $6. The FY28 target of 15 Mt, or about $1 bn of sales, has been kept. If it is met at the $10 a tonne management wants once freight normalises, Guinea EBITDA would be roughly $150 m. At today's $6, it would be about $90 m. That gap is the whole debate, and it depends on freight and a new jetty (GSM, due Q4 FY27), not on geology. Promoter holding of 48% and a history of large single-year moves argue for sizing this as the volatile name it is.

For GMDC, an operating business worth less than its reported earnings suggest. Strip out the exceptional GST credit and the trailing profit falls sharply. The mining segment earned ₹607.50 cr in FY26 and the power segment lost ₹79.61 cr. The ₹9.50 dividend, the 74% state holding and the rare-earth option support the stock, but the 23.8% revenue growth in Q1 FY27 came with a two-point fall in margin. The test is whether lignite volumes grow toward the company's stated ambitions without that margin eroding further.

For 20 Microns, a small business doing what it says. Six years in a 12–14% margin band, a withdrawn FY27 guidance and a ₹100 cr capex plan weighted to Malaysia. It is the cleanest business in the section and the least exciting, which is probably why it trades flat.

The honest read

This section closes the Metals family with a warning about labels. Of sixteen names, three are not miners, four have no business, four are too new, and the five that remain earn from lignite rent, African bauxite logistics, mineral processing, titanium chemistry and floor stone. Treat each as its correct peer group, not as "industrial minerals". The two that fit the family's through-line best — businesses a customer cannot easily switch away from — are 20 Microns, with its qualified grades, and Midwest, with a single-belt granite nobody else can supply. Neither is the one the market got most excited about. That was the rare-earth option, and it has already been marked down by a quarter to a half.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What were tonnes sold by mineral, and realisation per tonne?
  2. What is the operating profit excluding other income and exceptional items?
  3. How long is the remaining life of each lease, and when is each renewal or auction due?
  4. What is the strip ratio, and how has it moved?
  5. What share of cost is diesel, and what share freight?
  6. For exporters: what EBITDA per tonne was earned, and what freight rate is assumed in guidance?
  7. What share of revenue comes from processed or value-added grades, and at what margin?
  8. Which customers have qualified your grades, and how long did qualification take?
  9. For rare-earth projects: what is the resource, the pilot result, the capex and the date of first separated oxide?
  10. What environmental clearances are pending, and what volume depends on them?
  11. For Guinea: what is the status of your concessions, and what royalty or export-policy changes are under discussion?

19 · Glossary

Every term you'll meet

Minerals and products

Industrial mineral
A mineral valued for its physical or chemical properties rather than for a metal extracted from it.
Lignite
Brown coal: low in energy content, high in moisture, burned close to where it is mined.
Bauxite
The ore of aluminium, refined to alumina and then smelted.
Metallurgical bauxite
Bauxite of a grade suitable for alumina refining.
Calcined bauxite
Bauxite fired at high temperature for refractories and abrasives.
Bentonite
A clay that swells when wet; used in foundry sand, drilling mud, pelletising and liners.
Sodium bentonite
The high-swelling variety, the benchmark for sealing and drilling uses.
Bleaching clay
Activated clay used to decolourise edible oils.
Kaolin
China clay: a white clay used in paper, paint, ceramics and rubber.
Calcined kaolin
Kaolin heated to raise brightness and opacity; a partial substitute for titanium dioxide.
Ground calcium carbonate
Finely milled limestone or marble used as a filler.
Ilmenite
An iron-titanium oxide mineral found in beach sands.
Synthetic rutile
Ilmenite chemically upgraded to a high titanium-dioxide content.
Rutile
A naturally high-grade titanium dioxide mineral.
Monazite
A beach-sand mineral containing rare earths and thorium; treated as atomic in India.
Zircon
A heavy mineral used in ceramics and foundries.
Heavy mineral sands
Beach or dune sands rich in ilmenite, rutile, zircon, garnet and monazite.
Rare earth elements
Seventeen elements, including neodymium and dysprosium, used in permanent magnets.
Rare earth oxide
The separated, purified form in which rare earths are traded.
Fluorspar
Calcium fluoride, the starting mineral for fluorochemicals (see 007).
Kota stone
A fine-grained limestone from the Kota region of Rajasthan, used for flooring.
Black Galaxy granite
A black granite with gold-coloured flecks, quarried in one belt in Andhra Pradesh.
M-sand
Manufactured sand made by crushing rock, replacing river sand.
Ferric chloride
A water-treatment chemical; a by-product line at Cochin Minerals.

Mining and processing

Opencast mining
Removing overburden to reach a deposit from the surface.
Overburden
The waste rock and soil above a deposit.
Strip ratio
Tonnes of waste removed per tonne of mineral; the main driver of mining cost.
Beneficiation
Washing, sorting or separating ore to raise its grade.
Micronising
Grinding a mineral to particle sizes measured in microns.
Calcination
Heating a mineral to drive off water or change its structure.
Surface treatment
Coating mineral particles so they disperse in plastics or paint.
Dimension stone
Natural stone cut into blocks and slabs, rather than crushed.
Aggregates
Crushed rock and gravel used in concrete and roads.
G1–G4 exploration
India's stages of exploration confidence, from reconnaissance (G4) to detailed (G1).

Policy and business terms

MMDR Act
The Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act, 1957 — the central mining law.
Major mineral
A mineral regulated by the Centre and auctioned under the MMDR Act.
Minor mineral
Building stone, sand and similar, regulated by state rules.
Atomic mineral
A mineral such as monazite reserved for the public sector above a threshold grade.
Exploration licence
A licence introduced in 2023 for private reconnaissance and prospecting of deep-seated and critical minerals.
Critical mineral
A mineral judged essential to the economy with a concentrated or risky supply.
NCMM
National Critical Mineral Mission, ₹34,300 cr over seven years from 2024–25.
REPM
Rare-earth permanent magnet; the ₹7,280 cr scheme targets 6,000 tonnes a year.
DMF
District Mineral Foundation — a levy on miners for local development.
NMET
National Mineral Exploration Trust — a levy that funds exploration.
Royalty
The state's per-tonne or ad valorem charge on extraction.
FOB
Free on board — the price at the loading port, before ocean freight.
EBITDA per tonne
Operating profit per tonne shipped; Ashapura's key metric.
Exceptional item
A one-off gain or loss reported separately, such as GMDC's GST credit.
Offer for sale
An IPO in which existing holders sell shares and the company raises nothing — as in CMPDI.
SME listing
A listing on the exchanges' small and medium enterprise platforms, with lighter requirements.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. What is an industrial mineral?

One valued for its properties — swelling, whiteness, hardness, heat — rather than for a metal extracted from it.

2. Why is GMDC's reported FY26 profit misleading?

It includes ₹522.65 cr of exceptional GST credit, and trailing other income (₹945 cr) is roughly double operating profit (₹465 cr).

3. Where does most of Ashapura Minechem's revenue come from?

Bauxite from Guinea — about 84% of Q1 FY27 revenue.

4. Why did Ashapura's EBITDA stay flat despite 14% more tonnes?

Higher fuel, ocean freight and other input costs held EBITDA per tonne near $6 against a $10 aim.

5. What happened to Cochin Minerals' captive ilmenite supply?

A 2019 central order reserved atomic-mineral beach-sand mining for the public sector, and Kerala cancelled its subsidiary's contract in December 2023.

6. Which three members are not mining companies?

D & H India (welding electrodes), Garnet International (investments and garments) and International Combustion (equipment for mineral processors).

7. What is the weakest calendar window for this basket?

February–March: 3 wins in 15 years, median −6.9 points — Chinese New Year and fiscal year-end destocking.

8. Why does 20 Microns belong with specialty chemicals?

Its value is in processing cheap minerals to qualified grades, reflected in a 12–14% margin band, not in its reserves.

9. What unit trap appeared in this primer?

A news summary printed GMDC's quarterly revenue as ₹9,066.4 cr and profit as ₹1,634.3 cr; the correct figures are ₹906.64 cr and ₹163.43 cr.

10. What is the difference between critical-mineral policy and critical-mineral revenue in this section?

The policy is funded — ₹34,300 cr and ₹7,280 cr — but no listed member here earns revenue from critical minerals yet; GMDC and Midwest hold development projects.
