Defence Services

Armies train with simulators because live ammunition, fuel and wear on tanks and aircraft are expensive, and because some situations cannot be rehearsed safely any other way. The same company that builds a firing-range simulator can build a system that detects and knocks down drones — and then earn maintenance fees on both for years. This section contains one listed company, Zen Technologies. This primer explains what it makes, how the business earns and why its revenue swings, why anti-drone systems became urgent after 2025, and what a stock that rose more than tenfold in two years and then fell 62% in six weeks is pricing now.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

High margins, lumpy orders, one listed company

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Before a pilot flies a real plane, they practise in a flight simulator — a room that looks and moves like a cockpit, with a screen instead of a sky. Soldiers do the same: they practise driving tanks, firing rifles and handling dangerous situations in simulators, which is cheaper and safer than using real bullets. Zen Technologies builds those simulators for the Army and police. It also builds machines that spot enemy drones in the sky and stop them, a bit like a goalkeeper for the air. Once it sells these machines, Zen is also paid to look after them for many years.

Say it to a client in one minute

Zen is a high-margin defence technology company whose revenue depends on when a few large orders are converted.

  • Products: training simulators (weapons, vehicles, tactics, live ranges), anti-drone systems, and new robotics such as interceptor drones and unmanned ground vehicles; plus annual maintenance contracts.
  • Economics: gross margin about 73%; operating margins swing with volume.
  • The problem: FY26 revenue fell 29% to ₹688 cr and adjusted profit 31% to ₹193 cr as order conversion slipped; Q1 FY27 revenue fell another 10.5%.
  • The promise: order book ₹1,239 cr plus ₹177.5 cr in July; target of ₹4,000 cr revenue over FY27–FY28 and ₹2,000 cr+ of anti-drone orders.
  • The stock: +331% in 2023 and +208% in 2024, −44% in 2025, +23% in 2026; about 78 times FY26 adjusted profit.

Most of the value in a simulator is software and system design — the visual scenes, the physics, the scoring — built into hardware that is largely bought in. That is why Zen's gross margin is around 73%, closer to a software company than a manufacturer. Anti-drone systems combine radar, radio-frequency detectors, cameras and jammers (and increasingly guns or interceptor drones) with software that fuses the sensors and decides how to respond.

The chain in one breath

  • Hardware is bought in.
  • Software — scenarios, physics, sensor fusion — is where Zen adds value.
  • Integration and trials turn it into a qualified product.
  • The forces buy after long procurement processes or through fast-track routes.
  • Maintenance contracts earn money for years after delivery.
Fund-manager lens

The critical feature of this business is that almost all its cost is fixed — engineers and software — while revenue depends on when orders are converted. When a large order is delivered, margins soar (EBITDA margin was 54.7% in Q1 FY26); when deliveries slip, revenue falls but costs do not, and margins drop (40.9% in Q1 FY27, and only 27.3% excluding other income). That operating leverage explains why Zen's profits, and its share price, swing so much more than its order book.

Competitors and adjacent listed companies

In anti-drone systems, Zen competes with BEL (058), which supplies DRDO-designed systems, and with Paras Defence, Solar Industries (060) and many start-ups. In simulation, foreign companies such as CAE, Thales and L3Harris supply flight and naval simulators, often with Indian partners, and HAL makes some aircraft simulators. Other "defence services" — overhaul, spares, refits — sit inside HAL (057), the shipyards (059) and BEL. Zen is the only listed pure play, so this section's statistics describe one company.

Zen's accounts show the pattern plainly. In FY25, when large orders converted, revenue was ₹973.6 cr and adjusted profit ₹280.2 cr. In FY26, when order conversion "extended beyond expectations", revenue fell to ₹687.7 cr and profit to ₹193.5 cr, even though the order book grew to ₹1,336 cr and Q4 alone brought ₹431 cr of new orders. Gross margin stayed around 73%; it was operating leverage — fixed engineering and staff costs spread over less revenue — that cut profit.

For investors this means two numbers matter more than any quarter's revenue: the order book and its composition, and the pace at which orders convert. Zen's order book at June 2026 was ₹920.6 cr of equipment and ₹318.4 cr of maintenance — the latter a steadier stream that cushions the swings.

The FY27–FY28 target

Management's target of ₹4,000 cr of revenue across FY27 and FY28 — roughly three times FY26's annual rate — depends on converting today's order book, winning the anti-drone orders it expects (at least ₹2,000 cr), and ending FY27 with an order book of about ₹2,500 cr. With Q1 FY27 revenue at ₹141.6 cr, most of the target falls in later quarters, and management has said revenue will be weighted to the second and third quarters.

5b · Why anti-drone demand surged

Cheap drones, expensive targets

Drones costing a few thousand dollars can find, film and strike tanks, radars and aircraft worth millions. The war in Ukraine made this obvious; Operation Sindoor in May 2025, when Indian air defences faced drone swarms along the western border, made it urgent for India. Zen's stock rose about 55% in the weeks after 7 May 2025 — from about ₹1,385 to ₹2,140 by 30 May — as investors bet on anti-drone orders.

LayerWhat it doesTypical technology
DetectFind the droneRadar, radio-frequency detectors, cameras, acoustic sensors

Demand is broad: every base, airfield, border post and critical installation needs protection, and the systems must be updated as drones change. That favours suppliers with software that can be upgraded and with product families spanning detection to hard kill. It also means competition: BEL has DRDO-designed systems, start-ups are entering through iDEX, and the forces buy through fast-track routes where price and speed matter. Zen's claim of at least ₹2,000 cr in anti-drone orders is a pipeline, not a signed book.

5c · Why simulation pays

The customer's side of the business

To understand why armies keep buying simulators even when budgets are tight, look at the customer's costs. A soldier learning to shoot fires hundreds of rounds; a tank crew learning gunnery fires expensive main-gun ammunition and burns fuel; every hour on a real vehicle adds wear that must later be repaired. A simulator converts much of that recurring cost into a one-time purchase plus a maintenance fee, and it allows training at night, in bad weather and in scenarios that would be too dangerous to rehearse live.

Training needLive costSimulator advantage
MarksmanshipAmmunition, range time, instructorsUnlimited repetitions; every shot scored
Vehicle driving and gunneryFuel, wear, main-gun roundsHours of practice without wear or ammunition
Tactics and commandMoving whole units to exercise areasRehearse missions in a classroom
Dangerous scenariosCannot be practised safelyAmbushes, casualties and failures can be simulated

This is why simulators feature on India's indigenisation lists and why training budgets tend to survive even when equipment purchases are delayed. The weakness, from a supplier's point of view, is that simulators are bought in batches as new equipment enters service or training centres are upgraded, so demand comes in waves. The maintenance contracts that follow each batch — ₹318 cr of Zen's order book — are the steadier part of the business, and their share is worth watching as a sign of how recurring the company's revenue is becoming.

Unlike most industries in this project, the most important input here is people, not materials. That cuts both ways: the business needs little capital and earns high gross margins, but its costs do not fall when orders are delayed. The second critical input is trial success — a product that fails or is delayed in Army trials can lose a year of revenue.

7 · The business calendar

Trials, fiscal year-ends and emergencies

  • January–March: deliveries and acceptances bunch before the fiscal year ends; Zen's fourth quarter is often its largest, and its results in May often move the stock sharply.
  • April–June: usually the smallest quarter for revenue; new orders are placed.
  • July–December: trials and deliveries; management expects FY27 revenue to be weighted to the second and third quarters.
  • Event-driven: border incidents and conflicts trigger emergency purchases, as after Galwan (2020) and Operation Sindoor (2025).

Quarterly results are the stock's biggest events. In the last two years Zen fell 11% on Q4 FY26 results (May 2026), fell sharply after results in February 2025, and fell about 5% on Q1 FY27. Investors should expect large moves around each results date.

For this company, the order book and conversion guidance matter far more than the month.

9 · What moves margins

Eight drivers

  1. Order conversion. The timing of deliveries and acceptances drives revenue.
  2. Operating leverage. Fixed engineering costs magnify revenue swings into profit swings.
  3. Anti-drone orders. The largest potential new source of revenue.
  4. Maintenance contracts. Steady, high-margin, a cushion in weak years.
  5. Product mix. Software-heavy simulators earn more than hardware-heavy systems.
  6. Competition. BEL and start-ups in anti-drone; foreign firms in high-end simulation.
  7. Exports. The AVT partnership and other markets.
  8. Other income. Interest on cash adds to reported EBITDA and profit.

The anatomy of the cycles

  1. A small simulator maker (2015–2019): lumpy orders and two small-cap crashes; the stock fell in four of five years.
  2. Anti-drone and indigenisation (2020–2022): Galwan and the indigenisation lists brought new demand; gains in 2020–21, a pause in 2022.
  3. The boom (2023–2024): record orders and profits; the stock rose about thirteen-fold in two years to a peak of ₹2,577 on 23 December 2024.
  4. The bust and rebound (2025–2026): a 62% fall in six weeks, a 55% jump after Operation Sindoor, then falling revenue and a partial recovery on anti-drone hopes.

Patterns that repeat

  • Revenue follows order conversion, not the order book. FY26 revenue fell while the order book grew.
  • Operating leverage cuts both ways. The same fixed costs that lifted EBITDA margin to 54.7% cut it to 40.9% (27.3% excluding other income) when revenue fell.
  • Conflicts lift the stock quickly; results decide whether the gains hold.

The global military simulation and training market is estimated at about $15 bn in 2026, growing around 7% a year, with Asia-Pacific — including India — growing faster. Counter-drone spending is growing much faster from a smaller base, driven by Ukraine and the Middle East. For Zen, exports are a second leg: its products are cheaper than Western equivalents, and the AVT partnership aims to open US defence training, but so far exports have been irregular.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Game engines and virtual reality. Cheaper, more realistic training content.
  2. AI opponents and scoring. Simulators that adapt to trainees and measure performance.
  3. Live–virtual–constructive training. Mixing real units, simulators and computer-generated forces in one exercise.
  4. AI-based drone detection. Telling drones from birds and classifying threats automatically.
  5. Hard-kill options. Lasers, interceptor drones and guns alongside jamming.
  6. Robotics. Unmanned ground vehicles and interceptor drones, which Zen launched in 2026.

Each shift raises the importance of software and speed of development. Zen's advantage — software-heavy products with high gross margins — depends on keeping pace; anti-drone in particular is a technology race, where last year's system can be defeated by this year's drone.

What is priced in

At about ₹15,009 cr, Zen trades at roughly 78 times FY26 adjusted profit — a multiple that assumes the FY27–FY28 revenue target is largely met. If revenue does reach ₹4,000 cr over two years at margins near historical levels, profit would be several times FY26's, and the multiple would look reasonable. If conversion keeps slipping, the stock has shown it can fall 40–60% quickly. The next two quarters, which management expects to carry most of FY27's revenue, are the test.

The honest read

Zen has a genuinely attractive business — software-like gross margins, protected domestic demand, recurring maintenance income and a product line in the most urgent area of modern defence. Its problem is predictability: revenue depends on a few large orders whose timing it does not control. That makes the stock a bet on conversion over the next few quarters, not on the long-term story, which is not in doubt.
