Steel Pipes & Tubes

A pipe is the cheapest way to move something — oil, gas, water, sewage — and a tube is one of the cheapest ways to hold a building up. India's listed pipe makers are usually discussed as one industry, but they are really four: line pipe for oil and gas projects, ductile iron pipe for municipal water, seamless tube for wells and boilers, and light structural tubes for construction and furniture. Each has its own customers, contract structure and cycle. What they share is an economic model: they buy steel, add shape and coating, and earn a conversion margin per tonne. This primer explains all four, why 2026 has split the sector so sharply, and what to watch in each.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Exports booming, domestic water starved

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Pipes are how everything invisible travels: the water to your tap, the gas to your kitchen, the oil from a well to a refinery. Some pipes are made by rolling a flat sheet of steel into a circle and welding the seam; others are pierced from a solid bar so there is no seam at all, which matters when the pressure is enormous. Factories that make pipes do not decide the price of steel — they buy it, bend it, coat it and sell it for a little more. So they earn money on the work they add, and they need someone building a pipeline or a water scheme to place an order.

Say it to a client in one minute

One sector, four businesses, and a wide gap between them this year.

  • Line pipe: Welspun Corp's order book is a record ₹42,100 cr after its largest-ever order — $1.8 bn (about ₹17,200 cr) of American pipe for FY28–FY29. Q1 EBITDA was a record ₹692 cr at a 17% margin.
  • Water pipe: the opposite. Central spending on rural water schemes was cut from ₹66,770 cr to ₹16,944 cr in FY26; Jindal Saw's profit fell 75% and Ratnamani's income 16%.
  • Structural tubes: APL Apollo's volumes fell 6% but EBITDA per tonne rose 18% to ₹5,522; it still guides to 15–20% volume growth and 8 mt of capacity by FY28.
  • Seamless: tied to oil and gas drilling and boiler demand.
  • Policy: American Section 232 tariffs of 50% keep Indian pipe exports out, which is why making pipe inside the US has become so valuable.

The distinction that matters for an investor is between project pipe and product pipe. Project pipe — line pipe, DI pipe, large seamless — is ordered in bulk for a specific pipeline or water scheme, priced by tender, and delivered over quarters; revenue is therefore lumpy and the order book is the key disclosure. Product pipe — structural and ERW tubes — is sold through distributors in small lots every day, like cement or paint; there is no order book, and the key disclosures are volumes and margin per tonne.

The chain in one breath

  • Steel mills supply coil, plate and billet.
  • Pipe mills form, weld or pierce it into pipe.
  • Coating lines protect it for decades underground.
  • Approvals decide who may bid for the biggest projects.
  • Projects and dealers take the pipe to its use.
Fund-manager lens

Because steel is most of the cost, a pipe maker's revenue swings with steel prices while its profit should not. The critical number is EBITDA per tonne: APL Apollo earned ₹5,522 a tonne in Q1 FY27, up 18% even as volumes fell 6%. When a company's revenue rises but EBITDA per tonne falls, it is being squeezed; when volumes fall but margin per tonne rises, as at APL Apollo, it is holding pricing. The second critical number, for project businesses, is the order book and its execution period — Welspun's ₹42,100 cr stretches into FY29.

5 · How money is made

Conversion margin, order books and inventory

BusinessHow it earnsKey metricCycle
Line pipeProject tenders, often in dollars, with steel usually passed throughOrder book and execution scheduleGlobal oil, gas and water capex
DI pipeGovernment water tendersVolumes and receivables from state bodiesBudget releases
Seamless tubeOilfield and boiler orders; import substitutionRealisation per tonneDrilling activity
Structural tubesDealer sales, priced daily off coilVolumes and EBITDA per tonneConstruction activity

Three things decide profitability across all four. First, the conversion margin — what the market pays above the cost of steel for shaping, welding, coating and certifying. Second, inventory: when hot-rolled coil moves sharply, a pipe maker holding two months of stock books a gain or a loss that has nothing to do with operations. Third, working capital: government water contracts pay slowly, which is why the DI pipe makers' balance sheets swell when state funding is tight.

Why Welspun's profit deserves a second look

Welspun Corp reported Q1 FY27 EBITDA of ₹692 cr and net profit of ₹1,048 cr. A profit larger than operating earnings can only come from outside operations — asset sales, revaluations, associate income or tax effects. That does not make the quarter bad: the EBITDA itself was a record, up 31.9% with a 17% margin. But the headline profit figure is not the run rate, and any valuation built on it will be wrong. This is the same discipline applied to Maithan Alloys in 065: separate what the business earns from what the balance sheet earns.

The American tariff is the subtlety worth understanding. Section 232 duties of 50% on steel — extended in April 2026 to apply to the full value of many products — effectively close the US market to Indian-made pipe. A company that owns pipe mills inside the United States, as Welspun does, sells into a protected market at protected prices; a company exporting from Gujarat does not. That is why the same policy that hurts Indian steel exports has been so good for one Indian pipe maker.

On the water side, the rural drinking-water programme was relaunched as Jal Jeevan Mission 2.0 in March 2026 with an outlay of ₹8.69 lakh cr and central assistance of ₹3.59 lakh cr, after a year in which actual spending collapsed to about a quarter of the budget. Reported coverage reached 15.80 crore rural households, about 81.6%, although survey evidence suggests far fewer taps run reliably. For pipe makers, the sequencing matters: money must reach states, states must award contracts, and contractors must be paid before orders convert into tonnes.

7 · The business calendar

Projects, monsoon and year-end billing

  • January–March: project billing is pushed to close the fiscal year; new tenders slow; the weakest window for the stocks.
  • April–July: new budgets release orders, sites reopen and dealer demand for structural tubes peaks before the rains. The strongest window, with 11 wins in 15 years.
  • July–September: the monsoon halts pipe-laying; deliveries slow.
  • October–December: post-monsoon execution resumes and construction demand returns; 10 wins in 14 years.
  • Throughout: steel price moves create inventory gains and losses; large export orders can be announced at any time.

How to use this

The April–July strength here matches the same window in steel (066) and mining (064) and comes from the same physical cause: the pre-monsoon construction and execution push. It is one of the more reliable calendars in this project — but 2026 shows that a single export order can matter more than any season.

9 · What moves margins

Eight drivers

  1. Conversion margin per tonne. The core economics of every pipe maker.
  2. Steel prices. Cost, and the source of inventory gains and losses.
  3. Order books. For project businesses, the only forward visibility.
  4. Government water funding. Decides the DI pipe cycle.
  5. Oil and gas capex. Drives line pipe and seamless tube.
  6. Construction activity. Drives structural tube volumes.
  7. Trade policy. American tariffs, Indian safeguards and anti-dumping cases.
  8. Working capital. Government receivables decide returns in the domestic business.

The anatomy of the cycles

  1. The capex freeze (2012–2013): stalled projects and a currency crisis; the basket fell 30% in 2013.
  2. Order-book recovery (2014–2017): pipeline and water projects returned; Ratnamani rose 434% in 2014 and the basket 109% in 2017.
  3. Cost squeeze and crash (2018–2019): steel prices rose faster than pipe prices.
  4. The water decade (2020–2023): Jal Jeevan Mission and a construction boom lifted every sub-sector; 2023 was the best year on record at +161.5%.
  5. The split (2024–2026): domestic water funding dried up while export order books hit records.

Patterns that repeat

  • Government money is the cycle. The water names rise and fall with budget releases, not with steel.
  • Order books lead earnings by a year or more. Welspun's American order will be executed in FY28–FY29.
  • Margin per tonne is the discipline. APL Apollo's 18% rise on falling volumes shows the difference between a brand and a converter.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Higher-grade line pipe. X70 and X80 steels allow thinner walls and higher pressures.
  2. Hydrogen-ready pipelines. Specifications are being written for hydrogen blends.
  3. Advanced coatings. Longer-life anti-corrosion systems for buried and subsea pipe.
  4. Large-diameter structural tubes. Replacing concrete and conventional steel sections in buildings.
  5. Plastic competition. HDPE and PVC pipes compete for water projects where pressure allows.
  6. Digital dealer networks. The structural tube business is becoming a branded consumer-like distribution model.

What is priced in

Welspun's tripling prices in a decade-long American order book; the question is execution and what replaces those orders in FY30. APL Apollo is priced as a branded compounder rather than a converter, which its margin discipline supports but its 6% volume decline tests. The domestic water names — Electrosteel 68% below its 2024 high, Jindal Saw 23% — are priced for continued funding delays, which makes them the leveraged recovery if Jal Jeevan Mission 2.0 money actually reaches contractors.

The honest read

Pipes are a better business than steel: less capital, faster payback, and in the branded and approval-heavy niches, genuine pricing power. But the sector's two halves must be judged separately. Export-facing pipe is enjoying a once-in-a-decade order cycle at prices set by American protection. Domestic water pipe is a government-funding story that has disappointed for two years and could turn quickly. Owning both in one basket, as the index does, hides more than it shows.
