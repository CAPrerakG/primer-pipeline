1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Feeding the plants that feed us

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Plants need three main foods: nitrogen (to grow green leaves), phosphorus (for strong roots and seeds) and potassium (to stay healthy). Soil runs out of them after many harvests, so farmers add fertiliser. Nitrogen fertiliser (urea) is made from natural gas and air. Phosphorus fertiliser (DAP) is made from a rock dug up in Morocco and Jordan, plus sulphur and ammonia. Potassium (potash) is a salt mined in Canada and Russia. India has little gas, rock or potash, so it imports a lot. To keep food cheap, the government tells companies what price to charge farmers and pays them the difference. So a fertiliser company's profit depends partly on the government's rulebook.

Say it to a client in one minute

Indian fertilisers work under two regimes.

  • Urea: the farm price is fixed (₹242 per 45 kg bag) and producers are paid cost-plus under the New Urea Policy, with gas pooled at a common price. Profits come from beating energy norms and producing extra, so they are steady but capped.
  • Phosphatic and potassic fertilisers (DAP, NPK, SSP, MOP): these get a fixed nutrient-based subsidy per kg, but retail prices are informally held down (DAP at ₹1,350 per bag). Margins get squeezed when global prices spike.

India imports most of its gas, rock phosphate, phosphoric acid, sulphur and ammonia, and all its potash, much of it through the Gulf. The 2026 Hormuz closure shut some Indian urea plants for lack of LNG, sent global urea above $850/t and DAP to ~$770/t, and forced a bigger subsidy. Q1 FY27 showed the split. Integrated makers and chemical-heavy companies earned more (Paradeep +24%, Deepak +101%, GNFC 4x), while import-heavy Coromandel's profit fell 25%. The winners are integration, manufacturing rather than trading, and unsubsidised businesses (chemicals, crop protection, specialty nutrients).

Analogy

Fertiliser is a multivitamin for soil. Nitrogen is the protein for growth, phosphorus is the calcium for roots and grain, and potassium is the immune booster. Indian farmers tend to overdose on the cheapest vitamin (urea, because its price is fixed and low) and underuse the others. That is why the government keeps pushing "balanced fertilisation".

Everyday examples

The white granules a Punjab wheat farmer broadcasts in December are urea. The grey-black granules drilled with soybean seed in Madhya Pradesh in June are DAP. The powder a small farmer in Rajasthan buys cheaply is SSP from a regional maker like Rama or Khaitan. The "Gromor" sign in an Andhra village is Coromandel's retail store.

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Raw materials: gas comes by ship as a super-cold liquid (LNG); phosphate rock is dug from mines in Morocco; sulphur comes from oil refineries in the Gulf; potash is mined from old dried-up seas in Canada.
  • Intermediates: gas plus air, squeezed and heated, makes ammonia. Sulphur burned makes sulphuric acid (see 004). That acid poured on crushed rock makes phosphoric acid.
  • Fertiliser plants: ammonia plus CO₂ makes urea; phosphoric acid plus ammonia makes DAP. The mix is rolled into granules and bagged.
  • Imports: when Indian plants can't make enough, companies buy ready-made DAP or urea abroad and sell it here.
  • Distribution: the farmer buys at a shop with an Aadhaar-linked machine; only then does the company earn its subsidy from the government.
Fund-manager lens

In this industry the "price" is set in Delhi, not in the market. Earnings quality depends on three things. How integrated are you (rock → acid → DAP)? How much of your profit comes from unsubsidised products (chemicals, crop protection, specialty nutrients)? And how fast does the government pay (subsidy receivables drive working capital and debt)?

5 · How money is made

Two rulebooks

Urea: cost-plus

  • The government reimburses a normative cost (energy per tonne at a set norm, plus fixed costs) minus the fixed farm price.
  • Gas is "pooled": all plants pay the same average gas price, so no one wins or loses on location.
  • Profit comes from using less energy than the norm, producing above reassessed capacity, and recovering fixed costs. ROE is steady but capped (roughly 12–18% for efficient plants).
  • Risk: gas supply cuts (2026), delayed payments, norm tightening.

DAP/NPK/SSP/MOP: nutrient-based subsidy

  • A fixed subsidy per kg of N, P, K and S, set each season (kharif, rabi). For rabi 2025–26, P was ₹47.96/kg and the DAP subsidy ₹29,805/t.
  • MRPs are "decontrolled" in theory but guided in practice (DAP ₹1,350 per bag), so companies absorb global price rises until the next revision or a special package.
  • Integrated manufacturers do better than traders importing finished DAP (Paradeep vs Coromandel's import business in Q1 FY27).
Analogy

Urea is like a government canteen contractor paid cost plus a fixed fee: safe, but you only earn more by cooking more efficiently. DAP is like a shop told to sell a product at a fixed price while its supplier's price changes every month, with a government top-up that's revised only twice a year.

Working capital: the hidden driver

Subsidy is paid after the fertiliser is sold to the farmer, through point-of-sale machines. When budgets run short, arrears pile up and companies borrow. In 2013 and 2018–19, arrears were a major drag on earnings and stocks. Budget allocations, supplementary grants and "special banking arrangements" matter as much to earnings as plant efficiency.

7 · The business calendar

Two crop seasons

  • Kharif (June–October sowing; monsoon crops such as rice, cotton, soybean): fertiliser offtake peaks June–August, with stocking in April–June.
  • Rabi (October–December sowing; wheat, mustard, pulses): the biggest DAP season is October–November, and urea peaks December–January.
  • NBS rates are announced before each season, around March–April and September–October.
  • The budget (February) sets the subsidy allocation; supplementary grants follow mid-year if prices rise.
  • Monsoon forecasts (April and June from IMD) set kharif expectations. Reservoir levels in October set rabi's.

8 · What the stocks do

Seasonality test on TradingView prices

The exceptions, and why

  • April misses: 2019 (−4.7) and 2013 (−0.8). Both were subsidy-arrears years. The best Aprils were 2016 (+16.6, a monsoon recovery forecast), 2026 (+14.8, subsidy increase after the Hormuz shock) and 2022 (+12.6, the Ukraine price spike).
  • April–July failures: 2012 (−9.5), 2013 (−22.2), 2018 (−21.4), 2019 (−17.0), 2022 (−0.1). Rupee crashes (2013, 2018), arrears (2019) and a weak 2012 monsoon start. The biggest runs were 2021 (+59.1) and 2025 (+45.3).
  • February–March beat the Nifty only in 2022 (+19.9, the war), 2021 (+8.3) and 2017. The worst was 2024 (−22.5).
  • October–December misses: 2012 (−7.7), 2019 (−5.6), 2025 (−8.8). The best were 2017 (+27.6) and 2015 (+20.8).
  • December beat the Nifty in 12 of 15 years, in line with rabi demand and subsidy releases.
How to use this

April and October–December are the favourable windows; February–March is poor. The annual result is set by policy (subsidy rates and payments), the rupee and gas, and by PSU sentiment for half the basket. In 2026 the median stock is down 16% even though most companies grew profit.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Eight drivers

  1. Monsoon: a good monsoon lifts volumes and fills reservoirs for rabi. 2026's forecast was cut to 90% of LPA (El Niño), and June sales dipped slightly (urea −2.6%, DAP −1.2%).
  2. Global prices vs subsidy rates: when import prices rise faster than NBS rates, phosphatic margins shrink.
  3. Gas and LNG supply: decides urea output; the 2026 shock forced shutdowns.
  4. Subsidy budget and payments: arrears drive debt and interest.
  5. The rupee: most inputs are dollar-priced; a weaker rupee (~₹96/$) raises costs until subsidies adjust.
  6. Policy changes: urea energy norms, NBS revisions, nano-urea promotion, the balanced-fertilisation push, 100% neem coating (2015), DBT (national from 2018).
  7. Non-fertiliser businesses: chemicals (GNFC, GSFC, Deepak, FACT), crop protection (Coromandel, Chambal), ammonium nitrate for mining (Deepak).
  8. PSU sentiment: about half the basket is state-owned, and PSU re-ratings (2017, 2021–23) and de-ratings (2024–26) swamp fundamentals.

The anatomy of a fertiliser cycle

  1. Global prices spike (2021–22, 2026): import costs jump, and margins get squeezed until the government raises subsidy.
  2. The government raises subsidy: a special package (DAP subsidy +140% in May 2021; kharif 2026 +11–12%). Margins recover, and the stocks re-rate on "policy support".
  3. Prices normalise and subsidy is trimmed; profits settle. Stocks drift unless there is another theme (PSUs, capacity growth).
  4. Payment delays (2013, 2018–19) are the real bear market: working capital balloons and interest eats profit.

Patterns that repeat

  • The monsoon matters less than policy. 2013 had a good monsoon (106%) but the basket fell 27% on arrears and the rupee; 2014 had a drought (88%) but rose 53% on reform hopes.
  • PSUs move as a group: FACT, NFL, RCF and Madras Fertilizers doubled or tripled in 2021–23 and fell 50%+ from 2024 peaks.
  • Chemical-heavy names diverge: Deepak and GNFC track ammonium nitrate and TDI more than fertiliser policy.
  • Capacity growth gets the premium: the Ostwal group (Madhya Bharat, Krishana) led 2025–26 on DAP/NPK expansions.

11 · The global picture

Resource owners set the price

Fertiliser power sits with whoever owns cheap gas (for nitrogen), phosphate rock (for phosphorus) or potash mines. India owns little of any, so it is the world's largest importer of urea and DAP in many years. Its policy aim is to lock in supply through joint ventures abroad (Paradeep with OCP; IFFCO and others in Oman, Jordan, Senegal) and new domestic urea plants.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • Europe → gas-rich regions (2000s–2022): European ammonia plants closed as gas became expensive, especially after 2022.
  • China became a swing exporter, then restricted exports (2021 onwards) to keep domestic food prices down.
  • India's self-sufficiency push: new urea plants (Gorakhpur, Sindri, Barauni, Ramagundam) cut urea imports; coal gasification (Talcher) is the next attempt.

12 · Where technology is going

Five shifts

  1. Nano urea and nano DAP: liquid sprays promoted as replacing part of granular fertiliser. Their efficacy is debated in field trials; if adoption is real, volumes of granular urea fall.
  2. Green ammonia: made with renewable hydrogen instead of gas. India's hydrogen mission tenders include green-ammonia supply to fertiliser plants.
  3. Coal gasification: Talcher Fertilizers (coal to urea) and GNFC's MoU with GMDC aim to cut LNG dependence.
  4. Specialty and water-soluble fertilisers, fortified and customised fertilisers, and drip fertigation: unsubsidised and higher margin (Aries, Coromandel).
  5. Soil health and precision: soil-health cards, drones spraying nutrients, and data-led dosing.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What share of volume is manufactured vs imported? What is the margin on each?
  2. How integrated are you (ammonia, sulphuric acid, phosphoric acid), and what is your cost vs import parity?
  3. What are subsidy receivables and days outstanding? Any special banking arrangement?
  4. What share of EBIT is unsubsidised (chemicals, crop protection, specialty nutrients)?
  5. (Urea) What is your energy consumption vs norm, and output vs reassessed capacity? How much gas was curtailed in 2026?
  6. (Ostwal group) What are the commissioning dates and utilisation targets for new DAP/NPK capacity?
  7. What would a DAP retail price increase, or a lower subsidy, do to your volumes?

Red flags

  • Rising receivables with flat sales.
  • Growth driven by trading imports at thin margins.
  • PSU valuations disconnected from losses (FACT).
  • Capacity plans dependent on imported acid in a tight market.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. How does a urea maker earn money under the New Urea Policy?

Cost-plus reimbursement at energy norms; profits come from beating the norms, extra output above reassessed capacity, and fixed-cost recovery.

2. Why can DAP margins shrink even with a subsidy?

The subsidy is fixed per season while import costs move monthly, and the retail price is informally capped at ₹1,350 per bag.

3. Why did Paradeep's profit rise 24% while Coromandel's fell 25% in Q1 FY27?

Paradeep is integrated with a Moroccan partner; Coromandel's import-heavy business absorbed the gap between spiking import costs and capped retail prices.

4. What did the Hormuz closure do to Indian fertilisers?

Cut LNG flows (some urea plants shut), sent urea from ~$400 to ~$850/t and forced a larger kharif subsidy.

5. Why are subsidy arrears so important?

Subsidy is paid after sale; delays force borrowing, raising interest and hurting profits (2013, 2018–19).

6. Which companies are really chemical businesses?

Deepak Fertilisers (ammonium nitrate, nitric acid, IPA) and GNFC (TDI, acids, aniline).

7. Why did the basket fall 27% in 2013 despite a 106% monsoon?

The rupee crash and ballooning subsidy arrears outweighed the monsoon.

8. What is the Ostwal group's growth story?

SSP makers (Madhya Bharat, Krishana) adding sulphuric acid, DAP and NPK capacity; they led the basket in 2025–26.

9. What is gas pooling?

All urea plants pay the same average gas price, removing location advantages.

10. Which months are strongest for these stocks?

April (13 of 15 years) and December (12 of 15); February–March is the weakest.
