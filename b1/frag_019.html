Commercial Vehicles

Trucks, buses and vans: the vehicles that move India's goods and people for a living. Nobody buys a truck for pleasure, so demand follows freight rates, infrastructure spending and the cost of credit. That makes this the most cyclical corner of the auto industry, with peaks and troughs of 40% or more.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Vehicles bought to earn money

Explain it to a 10-year-old

A car is bought to use. A truck is bought to earn. A transporter buys a truck the way a shopkeeper buys a shop: only if the goods coming in will pay for it. So when factories are busy and lorry rents (freight rates) are high, transporters buy lots of trucks. When business is slow, they buy none and simply run the old ones longer. That's why truck makers can sell 40% more one year and 40% less the next. Buses are similar, except the buyer is usually a state government, which is now buying electric ones.

Say it to a client in one minute

Commercial vehicles are a derived-demand, credit-driven cycle: freight rates and fleet profitability set the appetite, financing decides whether the purchase happens, and regulation shifts the timing. The current upcycle is real. Crisil expects FY27 volumes at a record 12.4 lakh units, and GST 2.0 cut the tax from 28% to 18% in September 2025.

  • Tata Motors (CV) is the market leader, now a separate listed company after the October 2025 demerger; its Q1 FY27 wholesales rose 26%, ahead of the industry's 18%.
  • Ashok Leyland (Hinduja) is the truck-and-bus number two, with 29% of medium and heavy trucks.
  • Force Motors makes vans and SUVs and engines for Mercedes and BMW in India.
  • SML Mahindra (formerly SML Isuzu) is Mahindra's route into bigger trucks and buses.
  • Olectra is the electric-bus play, with a huge order book and a history of slow execution.

The catch in 2026 is margins: steel and other costs cut Ashok Leyland's margin to 10.1% and Tata's to 11.7%, even as volumes grew.

Analogy

Think of trucks as farm equipment for the economy. The buyer is a business, so the purchase is an investment decision: what it earns per month against the EMI. When freight rates rise and diesel is steady, the maths works and orders flood in. When either moves the wrong way, orders stop almost overnight, which is why this cycle is so violent.

Everyday examples

The Tata Ace carrying vegetables to your local market. The 14-wheeler on the expressway with a cement company's name on it. The blue electric city bus in Hyderabad or Mumbai, probably an Olectra or a JBM. The Force Traveller taking a school group on a trip. The BharatBenz tipper at a construction site.

Freight rates and utilisation

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Materials: a 16-tonne truck is several tonnes of steel, so when steel prices rise, truck makers' costs rise fast.
  • Aggregates: the engine, gearbox and axles are the expensive parts; most makers build their own.
  • Chassis: the factory usually sells a "drivable skeleton": wheels, engine and a cab, with no cargo box.
  • Body building: a local workshop welds on the load body, tanker or bus shell the customer wants.
  • Finance: almost nobody pays cash for a truck. If lenders get nervous, sales stop even when freight is fine.
  • Operators: the truck has to earn more per month than its EMI, diesel, driver and tolls.
Fund-manager lens

This is an operating-leverage business on top of a credit cycle. Fixed costs are heavy, so a 20% volume swing moves margins by several points, in both directions. The three things to track in order are freight rates and fleet profitability (does the buyer earn?), financing availability (can the buyer borrow?), and regulation (does a rule change pull demand forward or push it away?). Everything else, including market share, matters less over a cycle.

Why margins swing so far

  1. Fixed costs: plants, engineering and dealer networks are sized for peak volumes.
  2. Discounting: in downturns, makers compete with discounts of ₹1–3 lakh a truck, which goes straight out of margin.
  3. Steel with a lag: steel is bought quarters before the truck is sold, so margins fall when steel rises (2026) and expand when it falls.
  4. Mix: tippers and tractor-trailers earn more than base haulage models; parts and defence earn more than both.
Analogy

A truck maker is like a wedding hall. The hall costs the same whether it is booked every day or twice a month. In a good season the owner raises rates and prints money; in a bad one, the bills keep coming and discounts appear. The catering business (spare parts and service) keeps the lights on between seasons.

7 · The business calendar

March is the month

  • March: the biggest sales month of the year. Fleet buyers register vehicles before the financial year ends to claim depreciation, and makers push to hit annual targets.
  • April–June: a quiet quarter after the March push; price increases are announced.
  • July–September (monsoon): mining and construction slow, so tipper demand falls; freight rates soften.
  • October–December: the festive and post-harvest freight peak; bus tenders and school-bus buying.
  • Regulatory deadlines (BS-IV in 2017, BS-VI in 2020, ELV or safety rules) cause pre-buying before the date and a hole after it.
  • Monthly dispatch data on the 1st; e-way bill volumes monthly as a freight proxy.

Seasonality test on TradingView prices

The exceptions, and why

  • April–July failures: 2012 (−7.1), 2013 (−16.8), 2017 (−9.0), 2018 (−21.4), 2019 (−23.7), 2020 (−6.2). Every one falls in a downcycle: the 2012–13 freight slump, the 2018 axle-load shock and NBFC crisis, the 2019 collapse and the 2020 lockdown. The biggest runs were 2023 (+56.6) and 2025 (+51.6), both upcycle years.
  • April misses: 2019 (−7.3), 2021 (−5.1), 2025 (−2.1). The best Aprils were 2022 (+17.4), 2020 (+13.7) and 2024 (+12.4).
  • February–March works (10 of 15, median +2.4), which fits the March registration rush. The exceptions were crisis months: March 2020 (COVID) and 2022 (the Ukraine commodity shock).
  • October–December is mixed (7 of 14); the worst was 2022 (−12.8).
How to use this

The calendar is secondary here: the cycle dominates. The honest rule is that April–July pays only when the cycle is up, and in a downturn it is the worst window of the year. Check freight rates, e-way bill volumes and financing before using the seasonal pattern at all.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Nine drivers

  1. Freight rates and fleet profitability: the buyer's return on a truck. Better rates and GST savings have lifted fleet profitability in this cycle.
  2. Infrastructure and mining activity: road building, cement and steel output drive tipper and tractor-trailer demand.
  3. Financing: NBFC and bank lending to transporters; the 2018–19 credit freeze caused the last collapse.
  4. Replacement cycle: trucks bought in the FY19 peak are now due for replacement, the backbone of this upcycle.
  5. Taxes and rules: GST 2.0 (28% to 18%), axle-load norms (2018), emission deadlines, scrappage rules.
  6. Steel and commodity costs: the margin swing factor, with a lag.
  7. Diesel prices and tolls: the operator's running costs; diesel rose ~8.6% in May 2026.
  8. Government bus programmes: PM e-Bus Sewa and PM E-DRIVE fund thousands of electric buses.
  9. E-commerce and last-mile delivery: the structural growth driver for small commercial vehicles.

The anatomy of a CV cycle

  1. Freight recovers: industrial output and infrastructure spending lift load availability and rates; operators start earning again.
  2. Replacement plus growth: deferred purchases return in a rush; makers raise prices and margins expand fast (2014–15, 2021–23).
  3. The shock: a rule change (axle load 2018), a credit freeze (2018–19), a cost spike or a recession stops orders cold. Volumes can halve.
  4. The trough: discounts, plant shutdowns and losses; the stocks fall 40–60%, then start pricing the next recovery before volumes turn.

Patterns that repeat

  • The stocks lead the volumes by two to three quarters in both directions (2014 was a stock boom before the volume boom).
  • Regulatory pre-buying steals from the following year: BS-IV in 2017 and BS-VI in 2020.
  • Margins peak late in the cycle, then get taken away by commodities (2022, 2026).
  • Order books are not revenue: Olectra has had a huge order book through years of weak execution.

11 · The global picture

India builds cheap trucks; the world buys them

Global truck making is concentrated among a few groups: Daimler Truck, Traton (MAN, Scania, Navistar), Volvo Group, Paccar and China's Sinotruk and FAW. India is one of the largest markets outside China and the US, and its trucks are among the cheapest in the world, which supports exports to Africa, South Asia and the Middle East. Global groups entered India through partnerships: Volvo with Eicher (VECV), Daimler through BharatBenz, Isuzu on its own and, until 2025, through SML.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • Two makers to many (2000s–2010s): Tata and Ashok Leyland's duopoly opened up as VECV, BharatBenz, Mahindra and Isuzu entered.
  • Buses go electric (2019 onwards): state tenders moved to electric on per-kilometre contracts, bringing in Olectra, JBM and Switch.
  • Indian makers buy global assets: Tata's agreed purchase of Iveco would make it a global truck group rather than an Indian one.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Electric buses: about 4.5% of bus sales in FY26 and rising, funded by PM e-Bus Sewa (10,000 buses, ₹57,613 cr) and PM E-DRIVE (₹4,391 cr for 14,028 buses).
  2. Electric small commercial vehicles: last-mile delivery is the first economic use case; Tata holds about 47% of that niche.
  3. CNG and LNG trucks: cheaper fuel for fixed routes, expanding with the gas pipeline network.
  4. Hydrogen and long-haul pilots: early trials by Tata, Ashok Leyland and Reliance on mining and port routes.
  5. Telematics and fleet software: tracking, fuel management and uptime guarantees; makers increasingly sell service contracts, not just trucks.
  6. Safety and comfort: air-conditioned cabins (now required), AIS safety norms, driver-assistance systems; factory-built bus bodies replacing roadside builders.

The honest read

Volumes are in the good part of the cycle, and the record FY27 forecast is credible given replacement demand and the tax cut. The market is right to worry about margins: the last two upcycles ended when commodity costs and discounting ate the gains. Ashok Leyland is the cleanest cyclical read; Tata CV adds the Iveco integration as both an opportunity and a risk; SML is a turnaround under a new owner; Olectra needs execution, not more orders.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What are discount levels per vehicle, and how have they moved this quarter?
  2. What is the order-to-delivery gap, and how much is replacement versus new fleet addition?
  3. How much margin was lost to commodities, and what pricing has been taken?
  4. What is the mix between MHCV, LCV, buses, parts and exports?
  5. (Tata CV) What are the Iveco closing timeline, funding and integration plan?
  6. (Ashok Leyland) Why did MHCV share slip, and what is the margin path back to the teens?
  7. (Olectra) How many buses were actually delivered, and what is the working-capital cycle on gross-cost contracts?
  8. (SML Mahindra) What does the Mahindra integration change in sourcing, network and product plans?

Red flags

  • Volumes held up by discounts rather than demand.
  • Receivables from state transport undertakings stretching out.
  • Order books that never convert to deliveries.
  • Capacity expansion announced late in the cycle.

18 · Myths and live debates

What you'll hear, and the evidence

ClaimEvidence
"The CV cycle has peaked."Volumes are forecast to hit a record 12.4 lakh units in FY27, but growth slows to 5–6% after a 13% rebound. Margins, not volumes, are rolling over first.
"GST 2.0 changed the industry."It cut upfront cost about 8% and unlocked deferred demand, but it is a one-time price effect, not a growth engine.
"Electric buses are a huge opportunity."The orders are real (Olectra's ~₹15,000 cr book) but they are long-dated, capital-hungry gross-cost contracts; execution and working capital decide who profits.
"Tata's Iveco deal de-risks the business."It diversifies away from the Indian cycle, but it adds European exposure, integration risk and leverage. Judge it after closing, expected by November 2026.
"Market share is what matters."Over a cycle, volume and cost control matter more. Ashok Leyland's 29% share meant little in a quarter when its margin fell to 10.1%.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. Why are commercial vehicles more cyclical than cars?

Demand is derived from freight and construction activity and depends on credit, so buyers can defer purchases entirely when their economics worsen.

2. What caused the 2018–19 collapse?

The July 2018 axle-load increase added about 20% to legal payloads (cutting the need for trucks), and the NBFC crisis froze fleet financing.

3. Why is March the biggest sales month?

Fleet buyers register before the financial year ends to claim depreciation, and makers push for annual targets.

4. What happened to Tata Motors in October 2025?

It demerged: the commercial-vehicle business became a separate listed company (renamed Tata Motors Ltd), while cars and JLR became Tata Motors Passenger Vehicles.

5. Why did Ashok Leyland's stock fall in 2026 despite record Q1 revenue?

Its EBITDA margin fell to a multi-quarter low of 10.1% on raw-material and staff costs, and its MHCV share slipped.

6. What did GST 2.0 do for this industry?

Cut the tax on commercial vehicles from 28% to 18% in September 2025, lowering upfront cost by about 8% and unlocking deferred demand.

7. Why has Olectra's order book not translated into profits?

Electric-bus contracts are long-dated, capital-hungry gross-cost deals; execution and working capital, not orders, decide returns.

8. What is Force Motors' non-cyclical earner?

Building engines and axles for Mercedes-Benz and BMW in India, alongside its Traveller vans.

9. What would Iveco do for Tata Motors CV?

Make it a global truck group with European exposure; it also adds integration risk and leverage, with closing expected by November 2026.

10. Which seasonal window is most dangerous?

April–July in a downcycle: it lost 21–24 points in 2018 and 2019, while gaining 50+ points in 2023 and 2025.
