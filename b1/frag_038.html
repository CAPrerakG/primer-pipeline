Renewable Power Generation

The companies building India's solar and wind capacity — four-fifths of every megawatt the country added last year. The technology works, the cost has collapsed, and the growth is extraordinary. What has not kept up is the ability to sell the output: about 45 gigawatts of granted grid connectivity is sitting idle because nobody has signed the contract to buy the power.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Building faster than the grid can absorb

Explain it to a 10-year-old

A solar farm is a field of panels that makes electricity whenever the sun shines, with no fuel and almost no running cost. Once it is built, it is nearly free to operate — which is why nearly every new power plant in India is now solar or wind. But there are two catches. The sun only shines in the daytime, and everyone needs electricity most in the evening. And before you build, somebody has to promise to buy your electricity for the next twenty-five years — and right now a great many builders have finished the paperwork to connect to the grid and still cannot find a buyer willing to sign.

Say it to a client in one minute

Operationally this is the best-growing industry in the market; commercially it has hit a bottleneck.

  • The growth is real: Adani Green at 20.1 GW (+27%) with a 94% EBITDA margin, NTPC Green revenue +63% and 10,671 MW operational, ACME Solar profit +80%.
  • The bottleneck is contracts. Around 31.8 GW of granted connectivity plus 13.5 GW applied for is awaiting power purchase agreements, with signing delays of up to twelve months; Rajasthan alone accounts for 18.75 GW.
  • Tariffs keep falling: 2026 bids at ₹2.86–2.97 a unit, and regulators are rejecting higher ones — Haryana's regulator turned down a ₹2.99 contract on tariff and delay grounds.
  • The answer everyone is building is storage. Adani Green's battery capacity reached 3,551 MWh and ACME's 3.62 GWh, roughly 40% of India's total, with ACME now guiding to more than 10 GWh by FY27.
  • The market has noticed the gap: basket flat, median −6%, Adani Green still 55% below its 2022 peak.

Analogy

A solar plant is an orchard that produces its entire harvest between ten in the morning and four in the afternoon, every day, free, for twenty-five years — provided you signed a buyer before planting. India now has a great many orchards planted, a falling price per crate, and a queue of growers waiting for someone to sign. Batteries are the cold store that lets you sell the fruit in the evening, when it is worth more.

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Auction: the government asks who will supply solar power most cheaply, and the lowest bidder wins.
  • Contract: a state electricity company then has to agree to buy it — and many are refusing or delaying because they think prices will fall further.
  • Land and wires: you need a lot of flat land and a permit to plug into the grid.
  • Equipment: panels, inverters and increasingly big batteries are bought and installed.
  • Building: a large solar farm can be built in under a year, far faster than a coal plant.
  • Generating: once running, costs are tiny — so nearly all the revenue becomes profit, provided the customer pays.
Fund-manager lens

The economics of a renewable developer are simple and brutal: a fixed tariff for twenty-five years against a cost base that is almost entirely capital and interest. That produces a 94% EBITDA margin and enormous sensitivity to two things — the bid tariff, which has fallen to ₹2.86–2.97 a unit, and the cost of capital, because the asset is effectively a bond. What has changed in 2026 is a third variable: whether a signed offtake contract exists at all. A developer with connectivity, land and no power sale agreement is carrying the cost of capital with no revenue, which is why capacity growth and share prices have decoupled so sharply.

Read this section in three layers

The scale developers — Adani Green, NTPC Green, ACME Solar, and now Juniper Green and Clean Max — are genuine growth businesses with 90%-plus EBITDA margins and access to capital. The mid-tier — KPI Green, Ravindra — grew explosively in 2022–24 and have given most of it back. The legacy wind tail is what the sector's first cycle left behind: Orient Green Power, Indowind, Karma Energy and others, all 60–86% below their peaks after two decades. The equal-weighted basket mixes all three, and in 2026 that produces a flat number that describes none of them: ACME +83%, KPI Green −43%.

Why a 94% margin business can still disappoint

Because almost all of the cost sits below the EBITDA line. A solar plant's fuel is free and its maintenance is trivial, so nearly all revenue becomes operating profit — and then interest and depreciation consume most of it. That is why Adani Green can report EBITDA of ₹4,122 cr and net profit of ₹983 cr in the same quarter. The consequence is that returns depend on financing terms and on the tariff, not on operations, and both have moved against developers: tariffs have fallen to under ₹3 a unit, and any delay between building an asset and signing a buyer is pure carrying cost. With about 45 GW of connectivity granted or applied for and unused, the industry is currently financing a large quantity of capacity that is not yet earning.

Analogy

Owning a renewable developer is like owning a toll bridge you built with a mortgage, where the toll is fixed for twenty-five years and the government decides when the road opens. The bridge is superb; the only questions are what you paid for the mortgage and when the traffic is allowed on.

7 · The business calendar

Sun, rain and the March deadline

  • February–May: the highest solar generation months — clear skies and long days — and the start of the construction season.
  • June–September: the monsoon cuts solar output sharply and halts site work, while wind output peaks; August–September is the weakest window for these stocks.
  • October–December: the post-monsoon commissioning rush, and historically the best window (11 of 14 years).
  • January–March: the financial-year commissioning deadline, and then a lull; the weakest calendar-quarter window.
  • Auction calendar: central and state tenders arrive irregularly, and the signing of power sale agreements afterwards is the event that matters most.

The exceptions, and why

  • October–December has worked in 11 of 14 years (median +4.3), with only 2025 (−12.3), 2024 (−11.4) and 2012 (−1.8) failing — and the two recent failures are precisely the de-rating years.
  • The 2021 reading of +94.0 in that window is the high-water mark of the energy-transition trade, and it flatters every average in this table.
  • April–July works 10 of 15 (median +5.5) but with one catastrophic exception, 2012 at −34.9, from the sector's earliest and most speculative phase.
  • August–September fails 9 times in 14, matching the monsoon, when solar generation and construction both stop.
How to use this

The October–December pattern is the most reliable in the Power family and has a real cause: the monsoon ends, output recovers and developers rush to commission before the financial year closes. But note how much of this table is history from a different industry — the 2020 and 2021 readings belong to a speculative phase that is unlikely to repeat, and the legacy wind names in the basket distort the early years. The more useful signal today is not seasonal at all: it is the monthly count of power purchase agreements actually signed against the 45 GW waiting.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Eight drivers

  1. PPA and PSA signing: the bottleneck — about 45 GW of connectivity is idle awaiting contracts.
  2. Bid tariffs: ₹2.86–2.97 a unit in 2026, and regulators pushing back on anything higher.
  3. Cost of capital: the dominant input for an asset with no fuel cost.
  4. Module and battery prices: the capital cost per megawatt, and increasingly per megawatt-hour (see 045, 046).
  5. Transmission availability: connectivity granted is not the same as evacuation delivered (see 039).
  6. Renewable purchase obligations: the regulatory demand floor.
  7. Storage attachment: what converts a daytime asset into firm, higher-tariff supply.
  8. Commercial and industrial open access: selling to corporates instead of to state utilities — better credit, as Clean Max's model shows.

The anatomy of two renewable cycles

  1. The first cycle (2010–2018) was wind, built on accelerated depreciation and feed-in tariffs. It ended in tariff renegotiation, discom non-payment and stranded assets — the −38.6% of 2016 and −37.7% of 2018, and a legacy tail still 60–86% below its peaks.
  2. The second cycle (2019–2024) was solar, built on competitive auctions and global energy-transition capital. It produced +151% in 2020 and +127% in 2021.
  3. The constraint moved from cost to offtake. Tariffs fell below ₹3, capacity was built fast, and state buyers stopped signing — which is where 2025 and 2026 sit.
  4. The next leg is storage, converting an intermittent daytime product into firm supply that a utility actually wants to contract.

Patterns that repeat

  • Tariff deflation is permanent and relentless. Every cycle has ended with bids below what incumbents assumed.
  • The buyer's credit decides the outcome. Both cycles' crises came from state distribution companies renegotiating or not paying.
  • Capacity growth and share prices decouple for years. Adani Green has added over 4 GW in a year and remains 55% below its 2022 peak.
  • The legacy names never recover. Orient Green's +692% in 2021 left it still 70% below its later peak.

11 · The global picture

The cheapest electricity ever built, and the hardest to sell

Solar is now the lowest-cost source of new electricity in most of the world, and India has some of the best irradiation and the lowest construction costs anywhere — which is how tariffs reached ₹2.86–2.97 a unit. The global pattern is that this abundance creates its own problem: when a large share of supply arrives in the same six daylight hours, the value of each additional daytime unit falls, and the system's need shifts to firm capacity that can serve the evening. Europe, California and now India are all working through the same sequence. India's version is complicated by the financial condition of its state distribution companies, which is why the binding constraint here is contractual rather than technical: about 45 GW of connectivity has been granted or applied for and cannot find a signed buyer.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • From feed-in tariffs to reverse auctions (mid-2010s): competitive bidding collapsed tariffs and destroyed the first generation of developers.
  • From wind to solar: solar's cost decline and faster construction made wind a niche, until hybrid contracts revived it.
  • From energy to firm power: the shift now under way, where storage-attached contracts replace plain solar — and where ACME's 40% share of India's commissioned batteries becomes strategic.

12 · Where the business is going

Six shifts

  1. Battery energy storage at scale: ACME at about 3.62 GWh and guiding above 10 GWh by FY27; Adani Green at 3,551 MWh and targeting over 10,000 MWh.
  2. Firm and dispatchable renewable contracts: tenders that require supply in specified hours, which price storage properly.
  3. Hybrid solar-wind projects: smoothing output and improving utilisation of the same grid connection.
  4. Commercial and industrial open access: selling directly to corporates, avoiding state-utility credit risk — Clean Max's entire model.
  5. Higher-efficiency modules and trackers: more output from the same land and the same connectivity.
  6. Green hydrogen as a future offtaker: the long-dated answer to surplus daytime power, still unproven commercially.

The honest read

This is the clearest example in the series of an industry whose operating performance and share prices are answering different questions. The operators are compounding capacity at 27% and revenue at 63%, with margins no other industry can match. The market is pricing the fact that roughly 45 GW of connectivity is sitting unused, that tariffs have fallen below ₹3 and are still being rejected as too high, and that the buyer at the other end of a twenty-five-year contract is a state distribution company. Both views are correct. The resolution is likely to come from two directions, and both are already visible: storage, which turns an unwanted daytime product into firm power a utility will contract for — ACME holds about 40% of India's commissioned batteries and Adani Green is building at Khavda — and commercial and industrial supply, where Clean Max sells to corporates with better credit than any discom. Judge developers from here on the quality of their offtake, not the size of their pipeline: a gigawatt without a signed buyer is a liability with a solar panel on it.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. How much of the pipeline has a signed power sale agreement, and how much has only connectivity?
  2. What is the average tariff of capacity commissioned this year against the portfolio average?
  3. What is the cost of debt on recent project financing, and the expected equity return?
  4. What is the capacity utilisation factor achieved, and how does it compare with the bid assumption?
  5. What is receivable days from each major offtaker?
  6. What is the storage attachment plan, in GWh and in capital cost per GWh?
  7. What proportion of revenue comes from commercial and industrial customers rather than state utilities?
  8. How much capacity is built but not yet earning, and what is the carrying cost?
  9. What happens to returns if tariffs fall another 10%?

Red flags

  • Pipeline quoted in gigawatts without stating how much is contracted.
  • Commissioned capacity growing faster than energy sold.
  • Equity raised repeatedly to fund a pipeline that is not yet contracted.
  • Receivables from a single state utility rising for consecutive quarters.

19 · Glossary

Every term you'll meet

Technology and operations

Utility-scale solar
A large ground-mounted solar farm selling to the grid rather than to a building.
Capacity utilisation factor (CUF)
Actual output as a share of theoretical maximum; for Indian solar, typically around a fifth to a quarter.
Irradiation
The solar energy falling on a site, which sets how much a panel can produce.
Hybrid project
Solar and wind on the same grid connection, smoothing output.
Firm and dispatchable renewable energy (FDRE)
A contract to supply guaranteed power in specified hours, usually requiring storage.
Round-the-clock contract
Renewable supply matched to demand at all hours (see 037).
BESS
Battery energy storage system, measured in megawatt-hours. ACME has about 3.62 GWh commissioned.
MWh and GWh
Units of energy storage capacity, as distinct from MW of power capacity.
Tracker
A mounting system that turns panels to follow the sun, raising output per megawatt.
Curtailment
Output the grid refuses to accept, usually when supply exceeds demand (see 037).
Evacuation and connectivity
The right and the physical ability to inject power into the transmission network.
Greenfield capacity
Newly built rather than acquired capacity; Adani Green added 4,327 MW of it in a year.
Commissioning
The point at which a project starts generating and earning.
Module
An assembled panel of solar cells — the basic building block of a solar farm (see 045).
Inverter
The equipment converting the panel's direct current into alternating current for the grid.
Degradation
The slow annual loss of a panel's output, typically under 1% a year, assumed in every bid.
Wind capacity factor
The wind equivalent of CUF; higher in the monsoon months, which offsets solar's seasonal dip.
Repowering
Replacing old, small wind turbines with fewer larger ones on the same site.
Pooling substation
Where several projects' output is collected before entering the transmission network (see 039).

Commercial and contractual

Reverse auction
A tender in which developers bid the tariff down; the standard route to capacity in India.
Bid tariff
The price per unit a developer commits to for twenty-five years — ₹2.86–2.97 in 2026.
Power purchase agreement (PPA)
The contract to buy the power (see 037).
Power sale agreement (PSA)
The back-to-back contract between the intermediary agency and the ultimate state buyer; the step that is stalling.
Letter of award
Confirmation of an auction win, which precedes the contracts and the financing.
Offtaker credit
The creditworthiness of the buyer, which determines the project's cost of capital.
Open access
Selling directly to a large consumer rather than to the local utility (see 037).
Commercial and industrial (C&I) supply
Power sold to corporates, typically at better credit and higher realisation.
Renewable purchase obligation
The minimum renewable share a buyer must procure.
Qualified institutional placement (QIP)
A share issue to institutions; ACME used one to fund expansion.
Domestic content requirement
Rules requiring locally made modules or cells in certain projects (see 045).
Levelised cost of energy
The lifetime cost of a project per unit generated; the number auction bids are built from.
Accelerated depreciation
The tax incentive that drove India's first wind cycle.
Feed-in tariff
A fixed administered price for renewable power, used before competitive auctions replaced it.
Tariff renegotiation
State attempts to reopen signed contracts — the event that broke the first renewable cycle in 2018.
Must-run status
The rule requiring grid operators to take renewable output ahead of other generation.
Deemed generation compensation
Payment for output a project could have produced but was curtailed (see 037).
Bank guarantee
Security a developer lodges when winning an auction, forfeited if the project is not built.
Project internal rate of return
The expected return on the project's cash flows, the number a bid is ultimately set by.

Battery storage. ACME has about 3.62 GWh commissioned — roughly 40% of India's total — and Adani Green 3,551 MWh.

5. Which state has the largest unsigned queue?

Rajasthan, at 18.75 GW, followed by Andhra Pradesh (7.11 GW), Karnataka (6.99 GW) and Madhya Pradesh (4.85 GW).

6. What did the first renewable cycle do to shareholders?

It ended in tariff renegotiation and non-payment. The legacy wind names — Orient Green, Indowind, Karma — remain 60–86% below their peaks.

7. What is NTPC Green's ambition?

7–8 GW of commissioning in FY27, 60 GW by FY32 and 136 GW by FY37, from 10,671 MW operational today.

8. Why is Clean Max's model different?

It sells directly to commercial and industrial customers under open access, avoiding the state distribution companies whose credit and signing behaviour constrain everyone else.

9. Which seasonal window is most reliable, and why?

October–December, 11 of 14 years: the monsoon ends, generation recovers and developers rush to commission before the financial year closes.

10. What should a developer be judged on from here?

The contracted share of its pipeline, not its size. A gigawatt without a signed buyer is a cost, not an asset.
