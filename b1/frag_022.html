1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Selling the petrol pump of the electric age

Explain it to a 10-year-old

An electric car needs a plug the way a petrol car needs a pump. Some plugs are slow and cheap, like the one at home that fills the battery overnight. Some are fast and expensive, like the ones on highways that fill it in half an hour. Somebody has to make those machines, and somebody else has to own them, plug them into the electricity grid, and charge drivers for the power. Making the machine is a factory business. Owning the plug is a property business: it only makes money if lots of cars use it every day.

Say it to a client in one minute

There are three different businesses in this bucket, and they should not be valued the same way.

  • Charger manufacturers (Exicom, Servotech, Ampvolts) sell hardware to carmakers, fleets, oil companies and charge-point operators. It is an equipment business with order books, tenders and price competition.
  • Charge-point operators (Tata Power, Statiq, ChargeZone, Jio-bp, none listed here) own the stations. Their economics are all about utilisation: a 50 kW charger idle 20 hours a day loses money; the same charger at 60% is profitable.
  • EV component and vehicle makers (Mercury EV-Tech) sit closer to 018 and 023.

Policy is generous: PM E-DRIVE funds 72,300 stations with ₹2,000 cr and subsidies of 70–100% on upstream infrastructure at qualifying sites. The trap is that generous subsidies attract crowded bidding, and the listed names have thin margins, negative group-level profits (Exicom) or tender dependence (Servotech). The stocks moved 406% in 2023 and −53% in 2025 on the same underlying reality.

Analogy

A charger maker is like a company that builds petrol pumps: it sells machines to whoever is opening a station. A charge-point operator is like the person who owns the petrol pump: the machine matters less than the corner it stands on. The best corner in a city earns many times what an identical machine earns on a quiet road.

Everyday examples

The 7.4 kW box in an apartment parking bay that came free with a Nexon EV. The four DC chargers behind a highway dhaba where three cars wait on a Sunday evening. The row of 120 kW units at a city bus depot charging overnight. The swapping kiosk where a delivery rider changes a battery in two minutes instead of waiting an hour.

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Components: a fast charger is mostly a big power converter, turning grid AC into the DC a battery wants, using expensive semiconductors.
  • Manufacturing: companies assemble those modules into a weatherproof box with a screen, a cable and software.
  • Site: the hardest part is not the box but getting enough electricity to the spot, which needs a transformer and permission.
  • Operating: whoever owns the charger sells electricity by the unit, plus a margin, through an app.
  • Customers: fleets are the good customers because they charge every day; private cars mostly charge at home, cheaply.
Fund-manager lens

The money in this chain is unevenly distributed. Hardware is competitive and increasingly commoditised, with Chinese and global suppliers setting prices. Operating is a real-estate and utilisation business that needs patient capital. The one durable position is being designed into a carmaker's or fleet's platform (Exicom's sole-supplier AC contract) or winning repeat public-sector tenders (Servotech). Order books are worth watching, but ask what margin sits inside them.

The honest label for this section is "EV-adjacent small caps". None of India's largest charging networks is listed here, which is the single most important thing to know before using this basket as an EV-infrastructure proxy.

5 · How money is made

The charging-station arithmetic

Take one 60 kW DC charger costing about ₹12 lakh installed. Suppose it delivers power for four hours a day at an average 40 kW, which is 160 units a day, and the operator earns a spread of ₹6 per unit after electricity and demand charges. That is roughly ₹960 a day, or ₹3.5 lakh a year, before rent, maintenance, payment fees and downtime. Payback lands in the range of four to five years. Double the usage and payback halves; halve it and the asset never pays for itself.

Analogy

A charging network is a chain of tiny petrol pumps where the fuel margin is thin and the rent is real. The winner is not whoever installs the most machines but whoever installs them where cars already stop: fleet depots, highways with food, malls with long dwell times.

7 · The business calendar

Tenders, budgets and the March rush

  • January–March: government and public-sector buyers push to spend allocated budgets, so order announcements cluster before the financial year ends.
  • April–June: new scheme allocations and fresh tender cycles begin.
  • Monsoon: civil work and installations slow, which pushes revenue recognition into later quarters.
  • Festive quarter: EV sales peak, which lifts home-charger volumes sold with vehicles.
  • Scheme deadlines: PM E-DRIVE runs to March 2028, so the pace of approvals and disbursement sets the order flow.

The exceptions, and why

How to use this

Don't trade this basket on the calendar. Trade it, if at all, on order flow and disbursement: tender awards, PM E-DRIVE approvals and the gap between order book and revenue. The 2023 to 2025 round trip shows what happens when a policy narrative outruns cash flows.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Eight drivers

  1. EV sales: about 6% of cars and 6% of two-wheelers in 2026 (see 017, 018). Charging demand follows the installed fleet, not the annual sales number.
  2. Fleet electrification: taxis, delivery fleets and buses charge daily and are the anchor customers for public charging.
  3. PM E-DRIVE and state schemes: ₹2,000 cr for 72,300 stations, with 70–100% subsidies on upstream infrastructure at qualifying sites.
  4. Grid access: transformer availability and load sanction decide how fast sites open.
  5. Tender pricing: aggressive bidding compresses hardware margins; Servotech's tender share is a double-edged achievement.
  6. Component costs: power semiconductors and imported modules.
  7. Export demand: Exicom's Tritium exposes it to global high-power charger demand and its price war.
  8. Standards: connector and communication standards, and any move to standardised swappable batteries.

The anatomy of a policy-narrative cycle

  1. A scheme is announced (FAME-II, state EV policies, PM E-DRIVE). Every company with a charger product re-rates, regardless of its share of the market.
  2. Order announcements arrive, often without disclosed margins or delivery schedules. Prices run far ahead of revenue (2022–23).
  3. Execution reality: grid connections are slow, tenders are priced aggressively, and revenue converts more slowly than announced. Margins disappoint.
  4. De-rating (2025), after which the genuine businesses keep growing revenue (Exicom +61%, Servotech +66%) while the market ignores them for a while.

Patterns that repeat

  • Order book announcements move prices more than profits do.
  • The largest operators are not listed here, so the basket rarely reflects actual charging growth.
  • Revenue growth is real but low-margin: 60%+ growth with single-digit margins and group-level losses.
  • Drawdowns are brutal: all four members are 64–71% below their peaks even after 2026's recovery.

11 · The global picture

China built it first, and cheapest

China has more public charging points than the rest of the world combined, built through utility and state-backed rollouts alongside its EV boom. Europe mandates charging coverage along major roads. The United States is consolidating around Tesla's connector after other makers adopted it. India is early: more than 27,000 public points for a fleet that is still small, with the market forecast to grow from about $0.71 bn in 2025 to $2.48 bn by 2030.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • Start-ups to incumbents: the earliest Indian networks were start-ups; the largest are now Tata Power and Jio-bp, which own sites and capital.
  • Hardware to service: as charger prices fall, value moves to uptime, software and site rights.
  • Distress creates bargains: Exicom bought Tritium out of administration in 2024, buying global high-power technology cheaply, and is still working through its losses.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Higher power: 120–400 kW units for cars and megawatt-class charging for trucks and buses.
  2. Battery swapping for two- and three-wheelers and delivery fleets, where downtime is the real cost.
  3. Charging plus storage: pairing chargers with batteries to avoid demand charges and grid upgrades; Servotech's storage line points this way.
  4. Smart charging and V2G: scheduling charging to cheap hours, and eventually feeding power back to the grid.
  5. Plug-and-charge: the car authenticates and pays automatically, removing app friction.
  6. Standardisation: connector and communication standards, and the possibility of standard swappable packs mandated for small vehicles.

The honest read

The industry is finally converting policy into orders and orders into revenue, which is genuine progress. What is missing is profit: group-level losses at Exicom and single-digit margins at Servotech. The question for the next four quarters is whether growth at these prices can carry fixed costs, and whether Tritium stops consuming cash. If you want exposure to India's charging build-out with less volatility, the site owners (Tata Power, and the oil marketing companies) capture more of the long-term economics than the box makers do.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What is the order book, and what gross margin is embedded in it?
  2. How long does an order take to convert into revenue, and what delays it?
  3. What share of revenue is EV charging versus other businesses (telecom power, solar, storage)?
  4. What are realisations per kW, and how have tender prices moved?
  5. (Exicom) When does Tritium break even, and what is the cash burn until then?
  6. (Servotech) How dependent is the order pipeline on a few public-sector buyers?
  7. How much of the subsidy is received directly versus by the customer?
  8. What is the working-capital cycle on government orders?

Red flags

  • Order announcements without value, margin or timeline.
  • Revenue growth with falling gross margins.
  • Receivables from government buyers stretching beyond a year.
  • Equity issuance to fund working capital at low margins.

18 · Myths and live debates

What you'll hear, and the evidence

ClaimEvidence
"Charging is the picks-and-shovels play on EVs."Only if you own the shovels people actually use. Hardware is commoditising, and the largest Indian networks are not in this basket.
"The 72,300-station plan guarantees demand."It funds infrastructure, but the tenders are competitive and disbursement is slow; margins, not volumes, are the constraint.
"These companies grew revenue 60%, so the stocks are cheap."Exicom's group EBITDA is still negative and Servotech's net margin is about 5%. Growth without margin is not earnings.
"Battery swapping will make charging obsolete."Swapping works for small vehicles and fleets with standard packs; cars, buses and trucks will still need high-power charging.
"Public charging is where EV owners charge."Most private cars charge at home overnight. Public charging is driven by fleets and long-distance travel, which is why depot and highway sites matter most.

19 · Glossary

Every term you'll meet

Hardware and technology

AC charger
A slow charger that feeds alternating current to the car's on-board charger; cheap, used at homes and offices.
DC fast charger
A charger that converts AC to DC itself and feeds the battery directly; far faster and costlier.
kW (kilowatt)
The power rating; roughly, how fast a charger fills a battery.
kWh (unit)
The energy delivered; what the driver actually pays for.
On-board charger
The converter inside the vehicle used for AC charging.
Power module
The building block of a DC charger; several combine to reach higher power.
IGBT / silicon carbide
Power semiconductors that switch high currents; the costliest components.
Connector standard
The plug type and communication protocol a charger uses.
Plug-and-charge
Automatic authentication and payment when the cable is plugged in.
Battery swapping
Exchanging a depleted pack for a charged one (see 018).
V2G (vehicle-to-grid)
Letting a parked EV feed power back to the grid.
BESS
Battery energy storage system; used at charging sites to avoid grid upgrades.
Megawatt charging
Very high-power charging being developed for trucks and buses.

Business and operations

CPO (charge point operator)
The company that owns and runs chargers.
EMSP
E-mobility service provider: the app or account through which a driver pays, sometimes separate from the CPO.
Utilisation
The share of the day a charger is actually delivering power; the key profit driver.
Dwell time
How long a driver stays at a location; long dwell suits slower chargers.
Demand charge
A fixed electricity charge based on peak load, which punishes low-utilisation sites.
Load sanction
Permission from the distribution company for the site's electrical load.
Revenue-share model
Paying a site owner a share of charging revenue instead of rent.
Uptime
The share of time chargers work; the main complaint of EV drivers.
Site host
The owner of the land or parking where a charger sits; usually paid rent or a revenue share.
Depot charging
Charging a fleet overnight at its own yard; the most profitable and predictable use case.
Opportunity charging
Short top-ups while a vehicle is parked during the day, common for buses and taxis.
kWh throughput
Units of electricity a charger delivers in a period; the revenue driver an operator tracks daily.
Tariff spread
The margin between what the operator pays the discom and charges the driver.
Roaming / interoperability
Using one app or card across different operators' networks.
Range anxiety
The fear of running out of charge; what public charging density is meant to cure.
Grid connection
The physical and contractual link to the distribution network; usually the longest step in opening a site.
Order book
Confirmed orders yet to be delivered (see 019).
Tender-led business
Revenue won through competitive public bidding; volume at thin margins.
Upstream infrastructure
The transformer, cabling and civil work behind the charger; what PM E-DRIVE mostly subsidises.

Policy

PM E-DRIVE
The current central EV scheme, including ₹2,000 cr for public charging (see 018).
FAME-II
The earlier scheme that funded the first charging stations (see 018).
De-licensed activity
A business anyone may enter without a licence; public charging is one.
EV tariff
A special, usually lower, electricity tariff for charging stations.
AIS / BIS standards
Indian automotive and product standards for chargers (see 018, 021).

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. What are the three different businesses inside this section?

Charger manufacturing, charge-point operation, and EV components or vehicles. Only the first and third are listed here.

2. What decides whether a charging station makes money?

Utilisation: a 60 kW charger used four hours a day pays back in four to five years; idle, it never does.

3. What does PM E-DRIVE fund?

72,300 public charging stations with ₹2,000 cr, subsidising 70–100% of upstream infrastructure at qualifying sites, to March 2028.

4. Why did the basket rise 406% in 2023 and fall 53% in 2025?

A policy narrative and order announcements re-rated every EV-adjacent small cap; execution, margins and disbursement then disappointed.

5. What is Exicom's non-EV business, and why does it matter?

Critical power systems for telecom and data centres, its older, steadier base; the EV business alone would not support the company.

6. What is the risk in Servotech's tender success?

Tenders are won on price, so a high share of public-sector awards can mean thin margins and concentrated receivables.

7. Why is the median more useful than the mean in this basket's seasonality?

Because a couple of explosive years (April–July 2023 at +179.5 points) drag the average far above the typical year.

8. Where do most private EV owners charge?

At home, overnight. Public charging demand comes mainly from fleets and long-distance travel.

9. What did Exicom buy in 2024, and why?

Tritium, an Australian high-power charger maker, out of administration, acquiring global technology cheaply; it is still loss-making.

10. Who captures the long-term economics of charging?

Site owners and operators with high-utilisation locations, not the makers of the hardware, which is commoditising.
