Material Handling
Equipment

Every tonne of coal, ore, cement or grain that leaves a mine or plant rides on something: a belt, a feeder, a wagon tippler or a hook. The equipment is sold once; the belt wears out, the splice fails and the liner abrades every year. This primer separates the companies that sell that recurring wear and service from those that build the one-off project, and from the names that only share the label.

The industry in 60 seconds

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Think of a school canteen with a long sliding tray rail. The rail itself is built once. But the trays get scratched, the wheels squeak and somebody has to fix the rail when it jams at lunchtime. A mine has the same thing, only the “rail” is a rubber belt kilometres long carrying rocks. The belt wears out and has to be joined and replaced. The company that builds the whole rail gets paid once. The company that sells new belts, joins them and fixes them gets paid again and again, as long as the mine keeps running. Some companies also rent out giant cranes, like renting a bicycle instead of selling it.

Say it to a client in one minute

The watchlist calls ten companies “material handling”, but only four have a usable price history in the activity. Thejo Engineering splices and services conveyor belts and makes wear-protection products for mines and plants; 57% of its nine-month FY26 revenue came from services. Somi Conveyor Beltings makes rubber conveyor belts in Jodhpur. International Conveyors makes PVC solid-woven belts for underground mines, mostly for export, but its profit is now driven by a large investment book. TRF, a Tata Steel associate, designs and supplies bulk-handling equipment such as wagon tipplers and stacker-reclaimers, and is recovering from a decade of legacy project losses.

The investment distinction is between replacement and service income, which follows the tonnes moved through an installed system, and project income, which follows the customer’s capital budget. Thejo’s June quarter shows the first: revenue up 31%, operating margin up two points, order book nearly doubled. TRF shows the second: revenue up 11% but operating margin down to about 5% while finance costs on Tata Steel-held preference shares absorb the rest. The basket fell 9.9% this year, in line with the Nifty, but that average hides Thejo’s 26.1% gain against falls of 13–28% at the other three.

Conveyors are the cheapest way to move bulk material over a fixed route. Once a mine, cement plant, steel works or port has installed a conveyor network, the belts, idlers, pulleys and liners on it are consumed in proportion to the tonnes carried and the abrasiveness of the material. That makes belt replacement and maintenance a volume-linked, recurring business. The equipment that sits around the belt — feeders, crushers, stackers, reclaimers, wagon tipplers, ship loaders — is a project business, bought when the customer adds capacity.

Carry forward the Engineering family tests through 078 — Castings (see 078: who writes the specification and does the part wear out), 080 — Forgings (see 080: how much value sits in processing rather than material weight) and 081 — Fabrication (see 081: who carries input cost between quote and delivery). The project-acceptance and order-to-cash tests in 090 — Industrial Machinery apply directly to TRF. The replacement-channel question from 084 — Bearings applies to belts: being qualified on a conveyor is not the same as owning its replacement demand.

Two warnings frame everything below. First, reported profit is not operating profit in this group: International Conveyors’ June profit is almost entirely mark-to-market investment gains, and TRF’s other income exceeded its pre-tax profit. Second, the source watchlist mixes in a freight company, a real-estate company, an aerospace engineering firm, an elevator maker and a crane-hire fleet. Every one of them is placed below, and every exclusion is explained.

Why a belt is a consumable

A conveyor belt is a composite. The carcass — fabric plies, a solid-woven textile or steel cords — carries the tension. The covers protect the carcass from the material, the weather and the pulleys. Every tonne of sharp ore or hot clinker wears the top cover; every pass over the drive pulley flexes the carcass; every misaligned load gouges the edge. Belt life therefore depends on tonnes carried, lump size, drop height at transfer points, temperature and how well the system is maintained. A belt is replaced when the cover wears through, the carcass is damaged or a splice fails.

The splice is the weak link. A belt arrives on a roll of finite length and must be joined at site, either mechanically with fasteners or by hot or cold vulcanising. A badly made splice can fail in weeks and stop the whole line. That is why splicing, inspection and quick repair are sold as a service separate from the belt: the customer is paying for uptime. Thejo built its business on this service and then added products that the same crews install. The economics are close to the consumable model described in 082 — Fasteners, Tools & Abrasives: a small-ticket item whose failure is expensive.

Why a stacker-reclaimer is not

A stacker-reclaimer or wagon tippler is designed for a specific yard, material and throughput, built from fabricated steel and bought-out drives, erected at site and accepted after trials. It is bought when the customer builds or debottlenecks capacity. Its supplier earns engineering and fabrication margin once, then spares and service if it keeps the relationship. TRF’s history shows the risk: fixed-price EPC contracts taken in the last cycle turned onerous, retention money stayed uncollected after work was finished, and bank guarantees remained outstanding. That is the project-risk pattern in 090 applied to bulk handling.

The money in this chain sits in two places. One is specification and qualification: a mine that has approved a belt grade, or a steel plant that relies on a contractor’s splicing crew, does not change suppliers lightly, because a failed belt stops production worth far more than the belt. The other is the recurring tonne: once a conveyor runs, belts and liners are bought every year whether or not the customer expands. The weakest position is the fabricated equipment sold on a fixed price into one project, where the supplier carries steel prices, site risk and the customer’s acceptance schedule.

That ranking is not automatic. A belt maker without approvals sells on price into tenders where the lowest compliant bid wins. A service provider is only as profitable as its crews’ utilisation, and labour-heavy service can carry lower margins than manufacturing. Thejo’s June-quarter segment results show both sides: manufacturing units earned ₹16.73 crore on ₹73.36 crore of segment revenue, service units ₹10.15 crore on ₹122.51 crore. Services bring the customer relationship and the volume; manufactured wear products bring more of the margin.

Fund-manager lens

Ask every company in this space what share of revenue repeats without a new customer capex decision. Thejo can answer with its service and wear-product mix; Somi with its replacement versus first-fill sales; TRF with its spares and Tata Steel rate-contract share. The higher and more measurable the repeating share, the more the business behaves like a consumable compounder and the less like a project contractor.

The audit

Six exclusions, three history repairs, one sensitivity. ABC India, Skyline Millars and Sika are outside the activity. Crown is a rental fleet with a sparse, gappy price history; its −34.3% close-to-close move on 8 February 2022 follows a 277-day gap and is not evidence of a one-day crash. Indef and L.T. Elevator are too recently listed. All ten source series end on 18 September 2026; none is filled forward.

International Conveyors’ NSE series begins only in September 2021. Its BSE history on the same ISIN (INE575C01027) runs from May 2005, overlaps at a scale factor of 0.999 and was joined at the first common quote. Somi’s available NSE history begins in July 2016 and its BSE history in TradingView only in January 2024; no earlier price is invented. Thejo’s NSE history begins in May 2014 on the SME platform with gaps of up to 82 days to 2017 and frequent ten-to-33-day gaps until 15 November 2019; it enters the basket only from December 2019. No basket member has a daily move of −30% or worse, or +50% or more, inside its used window.

International Conveyors is kept but flagged. Its belt business is genuine and it is the only listed PVC solid-woven belt maker, but in the June quarter its investment segment earned ₹143.39 crore against ₹9.29 crore from belts. Since 2023 its share price partly tracks a treasury portfolio. Removing it leaves the April record at 9 of 10 and the 2026 return at −9.5%; the page reports both.

Two inclusions deserve a defence. TRF is kept even though its operations and cash are weak, because excluding the poor performers would make the history look better by construction. International Conveyors is kept because the alternative is a three-name basket that starts in 2016, and the investment distortion is a recent feature that the sensitivity isolates. Thejo’s exclusion before December 2019 is the opposite case: the company was active and relevant, but a share that did not trade for weeks at a time cannot produce reliable monthly returns.

Thejo: the service-and-wear model working

Thejo’s group revenue rose 31.0% and the operating proxy 54.6%, lifting the margin from 12.1% to 14.2%. The company’s own commentary says all subsidiaries except the one in Saudi Arabia were profitable, and that Middle East and Gulf disruption deferred some subsidiary execution. Group profit of ₹15.23 crore is below owner profit of ₹16.17 crore because a minority-held subsidiary lost ₹0.93 crore; EPS follows the owner figure. Standalone revenue rose about 30% and standalone EBITDA margin (including other income) was 16.30%. This is the clearest example in the roster of volume-linked service and wear-product income compounding without a new customer capex cycle.

The segment table matters more than the headline. In June, manufacturing units contributed ₹16.73 crore of segment result on ₹73.36 crore of gross segment revenue, service units ₹10.15 crore on ₹122.51 crore, and others ₹0.97 crore. Gross segment revenue totals ₹211.77 crore before ₹34.16 crore of inter-segment elimination: the manufacturing units sell products that the service units install. Services carry the customer relationship; manufactured products carry more of the margin. Capital employed is ₹151.92 crore in manufacturing and ₹188.13 crore in services, which tells you services are not asset-light either: they carry receivables, inventory at sites and equipment.

Somi: a belt maker with a volume problem

Somi’s revenue fell 40.5% in the June quarter. Material cost fell with it, and an inventory build of ₹3.28 crore cushioned reported costs. The operating margin actually rose from 8.4% to 9.6%, but on a much smaller base, so the operating proxy fell from ₹2.22 crore to ₹1.50 crore and profit halved to ₹0.60 crore. A one-quarter revenue fall of this size in a replacement business usually reflects tender timing or a lost large order rather than a collapse in belt wear; the filing does not say which. The new mixing line, commissioned and in final trials in FY26, adds capacity before the volume to use it is visible.

TRF: a project company still digesting its past

TRF’s revenue rose 11.2%, but the operating proxy fell from ₹3.20 crore to ₹1.33 crore and margin from 13.7% to 5.1%. Employee cost is half of revenue. Finance cost of ₹4.55 crore in a quarter, against revenue of ₹26.03 crore, reflects redeemable preference shares issued to Tata Steel and accounted as compound instruments, plus other borrowings. Reported profit of ₹1.88 crore exists only because other income of ₹5.89 crore — including a ₹1.27 crore gain on an asset held for sale and ₹0.14 crore of liabilities written back — exceeded the operating proxy fourfold. The FY26 annual consolidated figures show the same pattern: operating proxy ₹8.72 crore on revenue ₹85.03 crore, other income ₹15.92 crore, finance cost ₹14.82 crore, and an exceptional ₹11.31 crore voluntary-separation charge for 52 employees, giving a loss of ₹4.47 crore.

International Conveyors: a belt business inside an investment company

International Conveyors’ belt operations are healthy on their own terms: a 20.2% operating proxy margin in June. But the ₹7.12 crore of operating earnings is dwarfed by ₹147.05 crore of other income, mostly mark-to-market gains. The segment table shows investment assets of ₹632.77 crore against conveyor-belt assets of ₹79.58 crore. In the March quarter, other income was negative at −₹10.45 crore. The share is therefore partly a geared play on the company’s portfolio. The note to the June result prints the investment profit as “₹142.80 Lakhs”, which conflicts with the ₹143.39 crore segment result in the same document by a factor of one hundred; the crore reading fits every other line, but it is an inference and the source contradiction is recorded rather than silently corrected.

The honest read

Only Thejo shows rising operating earnings from the core activity in the latest quarter. Somi’s margin held but its volume fell; TRF’s reported profit depends on other income; International Conveyors’ profit is an investment result. A thesis on “material handling” has to be built company by company from the operating proxy, not from reported PAT.

The consumable model is supposed to produce steady cash. Thejo’s FY26 shows the qualification: a service business growing 30% a year needs working capital, because customers, often PSUs and large miners, pay on contract terms while crews and materials are paid monthly. Receivables absorbed almost as much cash as the year’s operating cash flow. That is a growth cost, not a flaw, but it means Thejo’s free cash flow after capex was close to zero in FY26. Watch whether receivable days stabilise as growth slows.

Somi’s FY26 cash looks excellent and is not repeatable on its own terms: most of it came from collecting old receivables. With June-quarter revenue down 40%, another release is possible as the book shrinks, which would again flatter cash. The test is cash after the new mixing line is running at volume.

TRF’s cash problem is structural and historical. Its annual report lists “legacy project closure and working capital stress” as a key risk: certain legacy projects remain financially open despite completed obligations, retention money is slow to recover, contracts have become onerous, and bank guarantees remain outstanding. Its board has proposed liquidating the Singapore holding companies, citing the absence of viable business prospects. The company has also re-entered the products and spares business and says it has secured supply orders for idlers, pulleys and spares from Tata Steel. The path to cash is closing old projects and replacing them with smaller, lower-risk product orders.

The test to apply

For a service or consumable company, compare operating cash with operating profit over three years, not one, and check receivable days. For a project company, follow retention, contract assets and bank guarantees. For an investment-heavy company, remove the portfolio before judging cash. Each test is different because the business models are different.

The critical constraint differs by model. For a belt maker it is the compound and the mixer: a belt that loses its cover early ruins a supplier’s qualification, and mixing capacity determines how many grades can be made at once. Somi’s decision to add a mixing line before volume returned is a bet that the constraint was capacity, not demand. For a service company it is people. Thejo’s growth is bounded by how many trained splicing and maintenance technicians it can deploy safely at remote mine sites, in India and abroad. For a project company it is balance sheet: TRF can only bid for what its guarantee limits and finance costs allow.

Rubber and fabric prices pass through with a lag in a tender-driven market. A belt priced in a PSU tender may be delivered months later; if natural rubber rises in the meantime, the supplier absorbs it unless a price-variation clause applies. Replacement orders from private customers reprice faster. Ask each belt maker what share of its order book carries price variation, rather than inferring margin direction from a commodity chart.

Exchange rates cut both ways. International Conveyors sells mainly to Canada, the US and Europe and buys some yarn and chemicals; Thejo earns about 30% of revenue from international markets through subsidiaries that also spend locally. A weaker rupee helps the export belt maker more cleanly than it helps the overseas service business, whose costs are in the same currency as its revenue.

India’s coal production calendar is the single most useful operating clock for belt and service demand. Coal India produced 768.19 million tonnes in FY26, down from 781.06 million tonnes in FY25, with off-take of 744.83 million tonnes. Its April 2026 production was 9.7% lower than April 2025. A year of flat-to-lower coal volumes is consistent with weaker replacement demand and with Somi’s revenue fall, but it is not proof of it: belts also serve cement, steel, ports and power, and exports matter. Coal India production data

The monsoon cuts both ways. Mining output falls in July to September, and so do new-belt dispatches to mines. Plants use the slow season for maintenance, which can lift service work. A service company with a large shutdown book can therefore look less seasonal than a belt maker. Neither effect shows up reliably in the share-price seasonality below, which is driven more by market-wide small-cap flows than by the operating calendar.

Project timing follows a different clock. TRF’s revenue depends on when customer sites are ready and when acceptance trials are complete; its annual report describes delays in closing legacy projects even after obligations were fulfilled. A slipped acceptance moves revenue and cash between quarters without telling you anything about demand.

April, and what it does not prove

April beat the Nifty in 14 of 15 years, by 13.9 points on average. The only failure was April 2020, by 1.8 points, when the whole market rebounded from the March crash. The largest April gains were 2015 at 37.4 points, 2021 at 24.2 and 2012 at 23.6. The pattern survives every removal tested: without International Conveyors it is 9 of 10 Aprils; without Thejo 15 of 15. That robustness is the most interesting statistical feature of this basket.

It still is not a trading rule. In 2012–2016 the basket had only two names, so an April observation was one or two stock moves. April relative strength did not carry into the April–July window in 2012, 2013, 2016, 2017, 2018, 2019 and 2022; the 2018 and 2019 windows lost 21.7 and 22.0 points. July itself is the weakest month, beating the Nifty in only 5 of 16 years with an average shortfall of 5.9 points. A plausible but untested explanation is that fiscal-year results and the start of the coal year draw attention to small industrial names in April, and the monsoon slowdown deflates it by July. The data are consistent with that story; they do not establish it.

February–March is the mirror image: positive in only 2 of 15 years. In thinly traded small caps, year-end tax selling and quarter-end liquidity are common explanations for weak February–March windows across many baskets in this series, so the pattern should not be read as specific to material handling.

Membership changes matter. Somi joins in August 2016 and Thejo in December 2019; before that the basket was International Conveyors and TRF only. Each month equally weights available returns, caps each at ±50%, and requires at least two names. The study uses closing prices without dividends, costs, taxes, impact or circuit limits, and these are small, illiquid stocks where equal weights may not have been executable.

How to use this

Use April as a calendar for company work, not as a position. The current-year sensitivity is the bigger lesson: Thejo alone moves the 2026 result from −20.6% to −9.9%. In a four-name basket, one company’s operating story can be the whole result.

The cleanest earnings driver in this group is installed-base utilisation: conveyors that run more hours wear out belts and liners faster. That is why a consumable supplier can grow even in a year without much new mine capex, and why a mine closure or a long strike hurts it directly. Capacity additions matter with a lag: a new FMC conveyor needs a first fill of belts at commissioning and then replacement belts several years later.

For TRF the driver is the balance sheet rather than demand. Its operating earnings before other income are smaller than its finance costs, so incremental revenue mainly changes how much of that gap is covered. A change to the preference-share structure held by its largest shareholder would move value more than any order. For International Conveyors, the portfolio is the dominant driver of reported profit; the belt business is the floor.

Two cycles in the price history

The first cycle runs from 2013 to 2019. A two-name basket fell in 2013, more than doubled in 2014, fell sharply in 2016, rose in 2017 and then fell hard in 2018 and 2019. TRF’s own history explains part of it: its AGM presentation charts revenue of about ₹547 crore in FY15 falling in the following years as legacy EPC contracts turned loss-making and Tata Steel stepped in with preference capital from 2019. The share-price cycle was steeper than the operating cycle because these are small, illiquid stocks.

The second cycle runs from 2020 to 2025. Every member rose in 2021; three of four rose strongly in 2023 and 2024, and all four basket members set their 2020–2025 highs between February and July 2024. That was the period of the coal production push and the market’s rerating of Indian small-cap industrials. 2025 reversed most of it for Somi and TRF. In 2026 only Thejo, with rising earnings and a growing order book, has held up.

The global industry and India’s place

Globally, conveyor belting is dominated by a few rubber-industry groups. Continental’s ContiTech division, which includes conveyor belts among its industrial products, reported 2025 sales of €6,005 million, down 6.0% (3.3% organically), with adjusted EBIT down 19.3% to €316 million, a 5.3% margin; Continental blamed weak industrial markets. Michelin owns Fenner, another of the largest conveyor-belt makers, which it acquired in 2018; Fenner operates a large Indian belting business from Madurai. Powder & Bulk Solids, Michelin moves to buy Fenner The Indian listed belt makers are therefore small domestic challengers to global incumbents that also manufacture in India. Their route to margin is approvals, niche grades and service, not scale.

The lifting-equipment industry offers a useful measure of what service is worth. Konecranes reported 2025 order intake of €4,389.3 million, sales of €4,187.8 million and a record comparable EBITA margin of 14.0%. Its Industrial Service business earned a 21.9% comparable EBITA margin, against 11.7% for Industrial Equipment and 9.2% for Port Solutions. Konecranes financial statement release 2025 The same structure — equipment sold once at a lower margin, service earning more — is what Thejo’s segment split and TRF’s re-entry into spares are reaching for in Indian conveyors.

India’s place is distinctive in one way: much of its mining is open-cast, so underground-grade PVC belts have a small home market. International Conveyors states that its products are mainly used in underground mines and sold predominantly to Canada, the US and Europe, and that it produced 3,83,520 metres in FY26 against installed capacity of about 4,25,000 metres at Falta and 7,00,000 metres at Aurangabad — roughly a third of capacity. International Conveyors FY26 annual report It says a new thicker-cover, low-abrasion product could compete with PVG and rubber belts, with trials under way and market entry hoped for from the second half of FY27. That is a plan, not a result.

Thejo exports services rather than belts. About 30% of its revenue comes from international markets, through subsidiaries in Australia, Brazil, Chile, Saudi Arabia and the UAE. In July 2026 it signed a three-year master supply agreement with a leading Latin American miner and received initial and follow-on orders for THOR mill liners worth USD 0.89 million and USD 0.44 million. Thejo June 2026 update Those are small orders; their significance is entry into a qualified product supply relationship with a large miner.

Somi’s mixing line is the most concrete technology investment in the roster. The company says installation, dry runs and calibration were completed in FY26 with commissioning and final trials under way, and that the line will enhance compounding capacity across all grades including steel-cord belts. Somi FY26 annual report The investment question is whether better compound consistency raises realisations or qualifies Somi for higher-grade tenders. Capacity alone is not the answer when the latest quarter’s revenue fell 40%.

Thejo’s technology is in materials and methods: patented wear-protection products, mill liners and splicing practice, backed by a DSIR-approved R&D centre and more than 25 patents according to its February 2026 presentation. Thejo investor presentation, February 2026 The test is whether products developed for its own service crews can be sold to customers who do not use Thejo’s services, as the Latin American mill-liner agreement suggests.

Monitoring and digital inspection could shift belt replacement from emergencies to planning. That helps the customer and can help the supplier who owns the data, but it can also reduce emergency-repair revenue. A service company that sells monitoring must show it as a paid contract with renewal, not as a marketing feature.

The most consequential long-run technology shift is not in the belt but in the customer: mechanised coal transport under the FMC programme replaces trucks with conveyors, rapid loading systems and silos. Each kilometre of new conveyor adds belts, idlers and pulleys that will need replacement for decades. Its arrival is gradual and its timing set by mine projects.

Policy and standards

First Mile Connectivity. On 18 August 2025 the Ministry of Coal told the Rajya Sabha it had planned 102 FMC projects with 1,092 million tonnes a year of capacity by FY30, that 44 projects of 429.5 MTY were operating, and that the envisaged capital expenditure was about ₹31,367.66 crore. It scheduled 12 projects of 102 MTY for FY27 and 15 of 201 MTY for FY28, with Coal India expected to dispatch 95% of its projected FY30 output via FMC. PIB, First Mile Connectivity, 18 August 2025 The ministry’s year-end review on 12 January 2026 instead described 139 FMC projects costing about ₹45,000 crore, with 65 projects of 552 MTY commissioned. Ministry of Coal year-end review 2025, 12 January 2026 The two official statements use different project counts and costs; this page reports both and does not reconcile them.

For belt makers, FMC means first-fill belts when each conveyor is commissioned and replacement demand afterwards. For equipment suppliers it means tenders for conveyors, crushers and silos, usually through large EPC contractors. It is a real, dated programme, but its revenue reaches a listed belt maker only through specific orders, and those are not disclosed in the latest filings of the four basket members.

Belt standards. Indian textile conveyor belting is specified under IS 1891 in several parts, including general-purpose, heat-resistant and surface fire-resistant belting. BIS published a product manual for IS 1891 (Part 2) heat-resistant belting, fourth revision, dated January 2025. BIS product manual, IS 1891 (Part 2) Certification under a product manual matters in PSU and steel-plant tenders that require the Standard Mark.

Underground mining approvals. India’s mine-safety regulator lists conveyor belting among the equipment requiring its approval for use underground, with fire-resistance and electrical-resistance tests specified. DGMS approval policy, Appendix I and II International Conveyors’ export markets impose their own approvals; the company cites certifications across several countries. Each approval is a barrier to entry and a renewal risk.

Trade remedies on lifting equipment. The DGTR issued final findings on 19 September 2025 in its anti-dumping investigation into certain cranes from China, initiated on 30 September 2024. DGTR case page, certain cranes from China The case concerns mobile crawler and truck cranes, which belong to construction equipment rather than the hoists and overhead cranes Indef makes; whether any duty was notified by the data date should be checked before treating it as a catalyst for any listed name.

Consumables and projects in different phases

Consumable and service businesses do not need a capex boom; they need conveyors running. The current evidence suggests the installed base is growing slowly through FMC and industrial capacity, while coal volumes have paused. That is a better environment for a service company winning share and adding overseas customers, like Thejo, than for a belt maker dependent on domestic coal tenders, like Somi.

For project suppliers, the cycle is earlier and more uncertain. Steel, cement and port capacity additions create demand for bulk-handling systems, but TRF is deliberately bidding selectively after its losses, and its value depends as much on the resolution of legacy contracts and its preference-capital structure as on new orders.

Where conviction should come from

From Thejo: the next two quarters of order-book conversion and receivable days. From Somi: revenue recovery and mixing-line utilisation. From TRF: operating earnings before other income exceeding finance costs. From International Conveyors: the belt business’s utilisation and new-product approvals, judged separately from the portfolio.

The closest proxy for consumable demand is tonnes moved, and Coal India’s monthly production is published and timely. It covers only one end market, and belts on cement, steel and power plants follow other volumes. Questions to ask management

Each question asks for a figure or bridge that can be checked against the next filing. An evasive answer is a reason to limit precision, not to fill the gap with an assumption.

What share of revenue is replacement and service, and what is first fill or project?

Split revenue by new installation, replacement belts or liners, splicing and maintenance, and O&M contracts. Give the share that renews without a new customer capex decision, and how it has moved over three years.

How much of the order book carries price variation?

For belt makers: what share of the book is fixed-price PSU tender work, what share has rubber or fabric price-variation clauses, and what is the average time from order to dispatch?

Why did revenue fall 40% in June? (Somi)

Was it a lost tender, a delayed dispatch, customer destocking, or a decision not to bid at low prices? Give the current order book and the dispatch schedule, which has not been disclosed since an August 2024 presentation.

What is the mixing line producing? (Somi)

State commissioning date, current output, which grades it enables, whether steel-cord belts have been made and tested, and what revenue the added capacity needs to earn its cost.

How fast are receivables growing relative to revenue? (Thejo)

FY26 receivables absorbed ₹38.88 crore. Give receivable days by customer type and geography, overdue ageing, and the expected trend as the ₹380 crore book executes.

What happened in the Gulf subsidiaries? (Thejo)

Quantify revenue and orders deferred by the Middle East disruption, the Saudi subsidiary’s loss, and the minority partner’s share of it.

When will operating earnings cover finance cost? (TRF)

Bridge the June-quarter operating proxy of ₹1.33 crore to finance cost of ₹4.55 crore. Explain the preference-share terms with Tata Steel, redemption dates and any plan to restructure them.

Which legacy projects remain open, and what is owed? (TRF)

List legacy contracts not financially closed, retention receivable, guarantees outstanding and provisions held, with expected closure dates.

How should investors value the belt business separately? (International Conveyors)

Give belt-segment capital employed, utilisation, export order book and the approval status of the new low-abrasion product. Explain the investment policy, the portfolio’s composition and the unit discrepancy in the June note.

Who owns the installed base?

For service companies: on how many conveyors are you the maintenance contractor, how long are contracts, and how often are they rebid? For equipment suppliers: do you supply spares to systems you built, and at what share?

What would make you slow capex or hiring?

Describe the utilisation, collection and order-intake conditions under which expansion pauses.

The most important live debate is whether consumable economics are owned or merely accessed. A service company that maintains the conveyor and supplies the wear parts it installs, as Thejo does, sits closest to owning the replacement cycle. A belt maker selling into lowest-price tenders sits furthest. Both sell consumables; only one controls the reorder.

The decision rule

Prefer the company that can show recurring revenue, rising operating earnings and cash after working capital in the same year. In this roster that is currently one company. The others each have a specific unresolved test — volume for Somi, financing for TRF, portfolio separation for International Conveyors — that should be passed before they are treated as material-handling exposures.

Glossary: find a term

Belts and conveyors

Conveyor belt
A continuous band that carries material between pulleys; its carcass carries tension and its covers resist wear.
Carcass
The load-bearing core of a belt: fabric plies, a solid-woven textile or steel cords.
Ply
One layer of fabric in a multi-ply textile belt; the number and rating of plies set belt strength.
Cover
The rubber or PVC layer above and below the carcass that protects it from the material and the pulleys.
Steel-cord belt
A belt whose carcass is steel cables, used for long, high-tension conveyors.
Solid-woven belt
A belt with a single woven textile carcass, impregnated with PVC; widely used underground for fire safety.
PVG belt
A solid-woven belt with PVC impregnation and nitrile rubber covers, offering better wear than pure PVC.
Fire-resistant belt
A belt that resists ignition and flame propagation; required in underground coal mines and approved by the mine-safety regulator.
Heat-resistant belt
A belt with covers designed to carry hot material such as clinker or sinter.
Splice
The joint that connects two belt ends; made mechanically or by vulcanising and often the point of failure.
Vulcanising
Curing rubber under heat and pressure to bond a splice or repair; cold vulcanising uses chemical bonding instead.
Idler
A roller that supports the belt between pulleys; consumed and replaced over the conveyor’s life.
Pulley
A drum that drives, turns or tensions the belt at the conveyor ends.
Transfer point
Where material drops from one conveyor to another; a major source of belt wear and spillage.
Belt cleaner
A scraper that removes carry-back material from the belt; a recurring accessory.
Compounding
Mixing rubber, fillers and chemicals into the compound used for belt covers; the recipe sets performance.
Mixing line
The plant equipment for compounding; often the bottleneck in belt manufacture.
IS 1891
The Indian Standard for conveyor and elevator textile belting, in parts for general-purpose, heat-resistant and fire-resistant grades.

Bulk handling, lifting and service

Bulk material handling
Moving loose materials such as coal, ore, limestone or grain in large quantities.
Wagon tippler
Equipment that tips railway wagons to unload bulk material onto a conveyor or hopper.
Side-arm charger
A machine that positions rail wagons at a tippler.
Stacker-reclaimer
A yard machine that builds stockpiles and recovers material from them.
Apron feeder
A heavy feeder made of overlapping steel plates that meters material from a hopper.
Ring granulator
A crusher that sizes coal for boilers using rotating rings.
First Mile Connectivity (FMC)
India’s programme to move coal from mine pits to dispatch points by conveyors, silos and rapid loading rather than trucks.
Rapid loading system
A silo-fed system that loads trains quickly and accurately.
Hoist
A device that lifts loads vertically, often mounted on an overhead crane.
EOT crane
Electric overhead travelling crane, running on rails across a factory bay.
Crane hire
Renting out cranes with or without operators; returns depend on fleet utilisation and financing.
O&M contract
An operations-and-maintenance agreement under which a contractor runs and maintains customer equipment.
Wear liner
A replaceable lining that protects chutes, hoppers and pipes from abrasion.
Mill liner
A wear lining inside a grinding mill; replaced as it wears with tonnes processed.
Shutdown
A planned stop of a plant or line for maintenance, when much belt and liner work is done.
Retention
Part of a contract payment held back until acceptance or warranty conditions are met.
Onerous contract
A contract whose unavoidable costs exceed the benefits expected; a provision is recognised for the loss.
Mine-safety approval
Regulatory approval required before equipment such as conveyor belting can be used underground.

Financial statements and shareholder claims

Other income
Income outside revenue from operations, such as interest, dividends, investment gains or asset-sale gains.
Operating proxy
This page’s bridge: pre-tax profit plus finance cost and depreciation, less other income and exceptional items.
Mark-to-market gain
A gain from revaluing investments to market price; unrealised and reversible.
Segment result
Profit reported for an operating segment before unallocated items; useful to separate businesses.
Capital employed
Segment assets less segment liabilities; the capital tied up in a business line.
Owner PAT
Profit attributable to the parent’s shareholders after minority interests.
Non-controlling interest
The share of a subsidiary’s profit or loss belonging to outside shareholders.
Basic EPS
Owner profit divided by weighted ordinary shares outstanding in the period.
Diluted EPS
EPS adjusted for options and other potentially dilutive instruments.
Weighted shares
Shares averaged by time outstanding; differs from closing shares after allotments.
ESOP allotment
Shares issued to employees under an option plan; changes capital slightly between periods.
Redeemable preference share
A share with a fixed dividend that must be repaid; often accounted partly as debt, with its cost in finance costs.
Compound instrument
A financial instrument split into liability and equity components for accounting.
Exceptional item
A separately disclosed item of unusual size or nature, such as a voluntary-separation cost.
Comprehensive income
Profit plus other comprehensive income such as actuarial and currency items; not the same as profit.
Deferred-tax credit
A tax benefit recognised for future deductions; non-cash in the period.
Operating cash flow
Cash from operations after working-capital changes; can differ sharply from profit.
Receivable days
Trade receivables relative to daily revenue; rising days mean customers are paying more slowly.
Bank guarantee
A bank’s undertaking to pay a customer if the supplier fails an obligation; uses credit limits.

Price-study vocabulary

Equal weighting
Giving each available member the same weight each month rather than weighting by market value.
Relative return
Basket return minus Nifty return over the same period, in percentage points.
Hit rate
The share of periods with positive relative return; not a forecast probability.
Median
The middle observation; less affected by extreme years than the mean.
Monthly clipping
Capping each member’s monthly return at ±50% before averaging.
Price splice
Joining a BSE history to an NSE history of the same ISIN at an overlapping quote.
SME platform
An exchange segment for small companies with lot-based, often thin trading; Thejo traded on NSE Emerge until 2023.
Trading gap
A run of days without a recorded trade; long gaps make month-end prices stale.
Survivorship bias
Distortion from studying only companies present today.
Window drawdown
Distance from the highest close within a stated window, not the all-time high.
Sensitivity test
Recomputing the basket without a member to see how much one name drives the result.

Why are there ten roster names but four basket members?

Three names are outside the activity (freight, real estate, aerospace), one is a crane-rental fleet with a gappy history, and two lifting and elevator makers listed too recently. Poor results alone excluded no one.

Why does Thejo enter the basket only in December 2019?

Before then it traded thinly on the SME platform with gaps of up to 82 days, which would make month-end prices stale. The company existed; its usable price history did not.

Is International Conveyors’ June profit a sign of strong belt demand?

No. ₹143.39 crore of the segment result came from investments and ₹9.29 crore from belts. Its belt operating proxy was ₹7.12 crore.

Why can TRF report a profit while its operations struggle?

Other income of ₹5.89 crore, including an asset-sale gain, exceeded the ₹1.33 crore operating proxy; finance cost of ₹4.55 crore exceeds operating earnings.

Why was Somi’s FY26 cash flow so much higher than profit?

It collected ₹14.83 crore of old receivables. That release is not a recurring source of cash.

Do the two official FMC statements agree?

No. August 2025 described 102 projects and ₹31,367.66 crore; January 2026 described 139 projects and about ₹45,000 crore. Both are reported; neither is assumed.

Sources and method

Primary result filings govern revenue, other income, profit, EPS and capital. Annual reports govern business descriptions, cash flows, capacities and share counts. Presentations supply management claims and plans, identified as such and dated. Rounded web locators were used only to find filings. All financial and policy evidence is dated no later than 18 September 2026.

Figures in lakh are divided by 100 to give crore; International Conveyors reports in whole lakh. Where text extraction was ambiguous, the PDF page was rendered and read visually (Somi’s June table, International Conveyors’ June tables). EPS reconciliations use published weighted share counts where available and disclose where only closing capital exists.

The four price series were checked for the fixed endpoint before calculation. The International Conveyors BSE/NSE splice, Thejo’s entry date, the all-roster gap and move scan, sensitivities and member calendar returns are reproduced by audit_091.py after compute_stats.py 091. A small generic option was added to the shared statistics script so a documented per-member entry date can be applied; it changes no earlier primer. The earlier research package for this industry is preserved in the repository’s pilot folder with its source manifest.
