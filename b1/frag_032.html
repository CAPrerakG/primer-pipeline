Petroleum Products & Trading

What happens to the parts of the barrel that do not become fuel. Transformer oil that insulates the grid, white oil in your moisturiser, rubber process oil in tyres, bitumen under every road you drive on, and calcined petroleum coke that becomes the anode in an aluminium smelter. Three genuinely different industries share this watchlist bucket, and the only thing they have in common is that their raw material comes out of a refinery.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

One bucket, three businesses, four shells

Explain it to a 10-year-old

When a refinery splits crude oil, not everything becomes fuel. Some of the light, clean oil is turned into the liquid inside electrical transformers, or the oil in baby lotion, or the soft oil mixed into rubber. The thick black sludge at the very bottom becomes the tar that roads are made of. And the solid black lumps left over can be baked again until they are almost pure carbon, which aluminium factories need to make metal. So these companies buy leftovers from refineries and turn them into something specific that somebody must have. They are small, they are not famous, and their fortunes depend on completely different things: one on the electricity grid, one on how many roads the government is building, one on aluminium.

Say it to a client in one minute

Treat this as three separate positions, never as a sector.

  • Panama Petrochem makes specialty oils — transformer, white, rubber process and ink oils. Q1 FY27 revenue rose 150% and EBITDA margin went from 7.9% to 22.35%, though the company itself attributes part of that to inventory timing. The stock is up 65%.
  • Agarwal Industrial is bitumen: importing, blending and delivering road tar, plus logistics. Revenue fell 27% and profit 20% in the June quarter; the stock is down 42%.
  • Goa Carbon makes calcined petroleum coke for aluminium smelters, and lost ₹48.23 cr in FY26 on rising revenue.
  • Four of the eight names carry no market capitalisation in the data and trade in single digits. They should be removed from the section before anyone screens it.

The interesting structural fact is bitumen: India now imports about 40% of its requirement, up from 30% five years ago, and more than 95% of that comes from the Gulf — which makes a road-building programme hostage to West Asian shipping.

Analogy

A refinery is a butcher and these companies are the specialists who buy the offcuts. One turns them into a delicacy that only one restaurant orders (transformer oil for the grid), one sells them by the tonne to the roadworks down the street (bitumen), and one bakes them into something an aluminium smelter cannot operate without (calcined coke). The butcher's price sets everyone's cost, but each has a completely different customer and a completely different cycle.

Where small converters fail

Each business in one line

  • Specialty oils: buy base oil, purify and blend it to a customer's written specification, and stay on their approved-supplier list for years.
  • Bitumen: buy tar cheaply abroad, keep it hot all the way to the road, and get paid by a contractor who is himself waiting to be paid by the government.
  • Calcined coke: buy green coke, bake it, and sell the output to a handful of very large aluminium smelters who know exactly what it is worth.
Fund-manager lens

All three are spread businesses on a dollar-priced input, which means two things. First, reported profit in any single quarter can be dominated by inventory effects rather than operations — Panama's margin move from 7.9% to 22.35% is explicitly attributed in part to inventory timing, and should not be annualised. Second, the critical question for each is who holds the pricing power: a transformer-oil supplier on an approved list has some, a bitumen trader competing on delivered cost has very little, and a calciner selling to three aluminium smelters has almost none. That hierarchy explains the returns better than the commodity cycle does.

Audit recommendation

This is the weakest-constructed section encountered so far in the series, and it should be rebuilt. Four of the eight members — Asian Petroproducts, M Lakhamsi, Omnipotent and Samyak — carry no market capitalisation in the data, trade in single or low double digits and have no visible operating disclosure; they are not investable and they contaminate any screen. Suraj Industries is classified as a distillery. Of the three real businesses, Goa Carbon belongs with carbon and graphite materials rather than petroleum trading, and Agarwal Industrial is as much a logistics company as a products company. The defensible core of this section is one company: Panama Petrochem. Everything in this primer that refers to "the basket" refers to Panama, Agarwal and Goa Carbon only.

Reading Panama's quarter honestly

A company of this size reporting quarterly revenue of ₹1,735 cr against ₹823 cr in the immediately preceding quarter, with margins tripling, is reporting something other than ordinary business growth. The company's own explanation names two causes: strong operational demand, and timing differences in inventory adjustments. In a quarter when crude rose sharply, a converter holding base-oil stock bought earlier will show exactly this pattern — the inventory is revalued upward while the cost of goods sold reflects the older, cheaper purchase. That is a real cash profit, but it is not repeatable, and it reverses if feedstock prices fall. The correct treatment is to look at the annual figures and at the underlying margin in a stable-price quarter before extrapolating anything.

Analogy

A shopkeeper who bought a warehouse of rice at ₹40 and sells it after the price moves to ₹70 has genuinely made money, and will report a spectacular margin. But the next warehouse costs ₹70. Inventory gains are real, non-recurring, and symmetrical — which is why the same effect produced Goa Carbon's losses when coke prices fell.

7 · The business calendar

Roads, monsoons and smelter contracts

  • October–June: the road construction season. Bitumen demand is concentrated in the dry months, which is why the October–December window has beaten the Nifty in 9 of 14 years for this basket.
  • The monsoon (June–September): road laying stops almost entirely; bitumen volumes collapse for a quarter.
  • Year-end government spending (January–March): contractors push to complete work before the financial year closes, but the stocks have historically fallen in this window regardless.
  • Aluminium smelter contracting: CPC is sold on annual or half-yearly contracts, so pricing resets are lumpy and negotiated.
  • Transformer and grid ordering cycles: utility capital expenditure determines specialty electrical oil demand.

The exceptions, and why

  • April–July failed six times in fifteen years, and twice catastrophically: 2018 (−38.4) and 2019 (−31.6), the two years the 2017 squeeze unwound.
  • The best readings were 2021 (+76.6) and 2026 (+48.1) — one an infrastructure boom, the other a single company's inventory-driven quarter.
  • January–March is the most consistently negative window in this entire primer series: 4 of 15 years, median −12.2, negative in eleven separate years.
  • October–December works (9 of 14, median +5.5), which is the only seasonal pattern here with a plausible physical cause — the start of the road-building season.
How to use this

With great caution. A three-stock basket of micro caps in which one year (2017, +314%) is a liquidity-driven squeeze will produce impressive-looking averages that mean very little. The only pattern worth acting on is the road season: bitumen demand genuinely concentrates between October and June, and the October–December record is consistent with it. Everything else in this table is small-sample noise, and the section's fundamental problem — that it bundles three unrelated cycles — makes any aggregate statistic suspect by construction.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Eight drivers

  1. Crude and base oil prices: the input cost for every business here, and the source of inventory gains and losses.
  2. Road construction spending: Bharatmala and state programmes set bitumen volume.
  3. Gulf supply security: more than 95% of imported bitumen comes from the Gulf, so shipping disruption is a physical risk, not a price one.
  4. Aluminium production: the sole meaningful driver of calcined coke demand.
  5. Import quotas and duties: the CPC quota for smelters was raised to 0.8 million tonnes a year for FY2026, and petcoke carries an 11% duty against 2.75% on competing thermal coal.
  6. Grid and transformer capital expenditure: the demand behind electrical-grade oils.
  7. The rupee: dollar inputs against rupee revenue.
  8. Receivables from contractors: bitumen is sold to road builders who are paid by government agencies, with all the delay that implies.

The anatomy of a converter's cycle

  1. Feedstock falls or a product shortage appears. Spreads widen sharply, because selling prices are stickier than input costs.
  2. Illiquid micro caps re-rate violently — 2017's +314% is the extreme case, with Goa Carbon up 729%.
  3. Capacity and imports respond, or the shortage passes, and the spread normalises.
  4. The de-rating is as fast as the re-rating: −47% in 2018 and −55% in 2019 gave back the whole of 2017.

Patterns that repeat

  • The averages lie. Excluding 2017, this basket's long-run record is unremarkable; including it, the section looks like a compounder. It is not.
  • Inventory drives the quarters. Panama's 22.35% margin and Goa Carbon's losses are two faces of the same mechanism.
  • The three businesses rarely move together. In 2026 they are +65%, −42% and −11%.
  • Liquidity is the hidden risk. These are micro caps whose price moves can be as much about the order book as about the business.

11 · The global picture

India as an importer of both ends of the barrel

India's position in these products is unusual: it exports refined fuels in bulk (see 031) while importing a growing share of the heavy residue it needs for roads. Bitumen imports have risen to roughly 40% of consumption from about 30% five years ago, and more than 95% of them come from Gulf refiners — the UAE, Oman, Iraq and Iran. That concentration means a shipping disruption in West Asia is a road-building problem, not merely a price problem. In calcined coke the story is a managed shortage: domestic calcining capacity is insufficient for the aluminium industry's growth, so smelters import under a quota that was raised to 0.8 million tonnes a year for FY2026, while the duty structure — 11% on petcoke against 2.75% on competing thermal coal — leaves domestic calciners squeezed from both directions.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • From domestic bitumen to imports: refinery configurations optimised for fuels produce less bitumen, so a road programme growing faster than refining has to import.
  • From unrestricted petcoke to quotas: environmental restrictions on petcoke burning reshaped the market and separated fuel-grade from anode-grade demand.
  • From commodity oils to specification products: the specialty blenders that secured electrical and pharmaceutical approvals escaped the commodity trap; those that did not remain price-takers.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Polymer modified bitumen: the fastest-growing bitumen grade as NHAI tightens expressway specifications; higher value per tonne than plain bitumen.
  2. Bitumen emulsions and warm-mix technology: lower-temperature laying, which cuts energy use and extends the working season.
  3. Ester and bio-based transformer fluids: alternatives to mineral transformer oil in some applications, a long-term substitution risk for specialty blenders.
  4. Higher-purity white oils: pharmaceutical and food-grade specifications that command a premium and require capital.
  5. Recycled asphalt pavement: reusing milled road material, which reduces virgin bitumen demand per kilometre.
  6. Anode technology in aluminium: any change in smelter anode consumption directly changes CPC demand.

The honest read

There is one thing here worth serious attention and it is not the returns. India's road programme now depends on Gulf bitumen for about 40% of its requirement, in the same year that crude is above $100 and West Asian shipping is disrupted — that is a genuine, under-discussed supply risk that shows up in Agarwal's 27% revenue decline before it shows up anywhere else. As investments, the three real companies are small, illiquid and driven by inventory as much as by operations: Panama's spectacular quarter should be read with the company's own inventory caveat attached, and Goa Carbon's losses are the same mechanism running the other way. The section as constructed cannot be screened, compared or indexed, and the recommendation in section 4 stands: strip out the five names that do not belong and treat what remains as three separate single-stock cases.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. How much of this quarter's margin came from inventory revaluation rather than operating spread?
  2. What is the normalised margin at current feedstock prices?
  3. What proportion of sales is on approved-supplier specifications versus spot commodity sales?
  4. (Bitumen) What share of volume is imported, from where, and what is the freight and heating cost per tonne?
  5. (Bitumen) What are receivable days from road contractors, and what is overdue beyond 180 days?
  6. (Calcining) What is the realised CPC price against green coke cost, and what is kiln utilisation?
  7. (Calcining) How does the smelter import quota at 0.8 million tonnes affect your pricing?
  8. What is the export share, and what currency exposure is hedged?
  9. What capital expenditure is planned, and what return is expected at mid-cycle spreads rather than current ones?

Red flags

  • An exceptional margin quarter presented without an inventory explanation.
  • Selling more than is produced for several quarters — an inventory drawdown dressed as demand.
  • Revenue growth with falling gross margin, meaning pass-through without pricing power.
  • Rising short-term borrowing against imported inventory in a volatile currency.

19 · Glossary

Every term you'll meet

Specialty oils

Base oil
The refined mineral oil that is the starting point for lubricants and specialty oils; classified by purity into groups.
Transformer oil
Highly refined oil that both insulates and cools an electrical transformer; sold against strict specifications.
White oil
Ultra-pure, colourless mineral oil used in cosmetics, pharmaceuticals and food-contact applications.
Rubber process oil
Oil blended into rubber compounds to soften them and aid processing (see 012, 025).
Ink oil
Carrier oil used in printing inks.
Hydro-treating
Treating oil with hydrogen to remove sulphur and aromatics, raising purity.
Approved-supplier specification
A customer's technical qualification of a supplier's product; the main barrier to entry in specialty oils.
Ester fluid
A synthetic or bio-based alternative to mineral transformer oil.
Group I, II and III base oils
The purity grades of base oil. Higher groups cost more, are more refined and are required for demanding specifications.
Viscosity index
How little an oil's thickness changes with temperature; a headline specification for electrical and process oils.
Flash point
The temperature at which an oil's vapour ignites; a safety specification that governs transformer and food-grade approvals.
Aromatic content
The share of aromatic hydrocarbons in an oil. Low-aromatic grades command a premium in pharmaceutical and food-contact uses.
Blending plant
The facility where base oils and additives are mixed to a recipe; the core asset of a specialty oil business.
Drum and bulk despatch
The two delivery formats; bulk is cheaper per litre but requires the customer to hold tankage.

Bitumen

Bitumen
The heavy residue from distillation used to surface roads; also called asphalt binder.
Polymer modified bitumen (PMB)
Bitumen blended with polymer for greater durability; increasingly specified for expressways.
Bitumen emulsion
Bitumen dispersed in water for lower-temperature application.
Warm-mix asphalt
Asphalt laid at lower temperature, reducing energy use and emissions.
Recycled asphalt pavement (RAP)
Milled road material reused in new surfacing, reducing virgin bitumen demand.
Heated tankage
Insulated, heated storage required to keep bitumen liquid; the core fixed asset of a bitumen business.
Bulk bitumen tender
A supply contract with a refiner or agency; Agarwal has held one from HPCL for 130,000 tonnes worth ₹477.5 cr.
Bharatmala
The national highway development programme that underpins bitumen demand.

Petroleum coke

Green petroleum coke
The raw solid carbon residue from a refinery coker (see 031).
Calcined petroleum coke (CPC)
Green coke baked at high temperature to remove volatiles, producing near-pure carbon.
Calcining
The roasting process, usually in a rotary kiln; energy-intensive.
Anode
The carbon block consumed in aluminium smelting; CPC's principal end use.
Graphite electrode
Another CPC application, used in electric arc steelmaking.
Import quota
The cap on CPC that aluminium smelters may import, raised to 0.8 million tonnes a year for FY2026.
Fuel-grade versus anode-grade
Petcoke burned for energy versus petcoke calcined for anodes; environmental rules treat them differently.

Commercial terms

Converter
A business that buys a commodity input and processes it into a specified product, earning the spread.
Inventory gain or loss
Profit or loss from holding stock as prices move; a dominant driver of reported margins here (see 031).
Normalised margin
The operating margin excluding inventory and one-off effects — the number worth modelling.
Spread
Output price minus input cost, the fundamental unit of profit in all three businesses.
Pass-through
The ability to raise selling prices when input costs rise (see 025).
Receivable days
How long customers take to pay; critical where the end payer is a government agency (see 030).
Liquidity risk
The risk that a share cannot be bought or sold in size without moving the price — acute in this section.
Shell company
A listed entity with negligible operations; four members of this section have no recorded market capitalisation.
Kiln utilisation
How much of a calciner's installed capacity is actually running; the operating leverage in a coke business.
Toll processing
Processing someone else's material for a fee, taking no price risk on the commodity itself.
Landed cost
The delivered cost of an imported input including freight, duty and handling — the number a bitumen importer competes on.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. What three unrelated businesses share this section?

Specialty petroleum oils (Panama Petrochem), bitumen and its logistics (Agarwal Industrial), and calcined petroleum coke (Goa Carbon).

2. How should Panama's 22.35% margin be read?

With the company's own caveat: strong demand and inventory timing. A margin tripling on doubled revenue in a rising-crude quarter is substantially an inventory effect, and not a run-rate.

3. What is India's bitumen supply risk?

About 40% of requirement is imported, up from 30% five years ago, and more than 95% of imports come from Gulf suppliers — so West Asian disruption is a road-building risk.

4. Why has Goa Carbon been losing money?

Calcining is a spread business squeezed by green coke costs, energy, an 11% duty and smelters who can import up to 0.8 million tonnes a year. FY26's loss was ₹48.23 cr.

5. What does "sold more than it produced" tell you?

Goa Carbon sold 156,117 tonnes against 134,585 produced — an inventory drawdown, which flatters volumes but cannot continue.

6. Why is the 2017 return of +314% misleading?

It was an illiquid squeeze — Goa Carbon +729% — that reversed over the next two years, −47% and −55%. It still distorts every long-run average for the section.

7. Which seasonal window has a genuine physical cause?

October–December, which has beaten the Nifty in 9 of 14 years and coincides with the start of the dry-season road-building programme.

8. What is wrong with this section as constructed?

Four of eight members have no market capitalisation or usable disclosure, one is classified as a distillery, and the remaining three belong to three different industries.

9. What is the only real moat in the section?

Customer specification approval in electrical and pharmaceutical-grade oils, which takes years to earn and protects the supplier that holds it.

10. Why does polymer modified bitumen matter commercially?

It is the fastest-growing grade as NHAI tightens expressway specifications, and it carries a higher value per tonne than plain bitumen.
