Oil Refining & Marketing

The middle of the barrel's journey: buying crude, cracking it into petrol, diesel, LPG and jet fuel, and selling it through 90,000-odd retail outlets at a price the government effectively sets. It is two businesses bolted together — a cyclical processing business that is currently earning extraordinary margins, and a retail business that is currently losing ₹25 a litre on diesel. 1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Two businesses, one share price

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Crude oil is useless as it comes out of the ground — it is a thick black soup of hundreds of different liquids mixed together. A refinery heats it in a tall tower so the lightest parts rise to the top and the heaviest sink, and takes each one off at a different height: cooking gas near the top, then petrol, then kerosene and jet fuel, then diesel, and tar at the bottom. The refinery earns the difference between what the soup cost and what the separated parts sell for. Then the same company sells the petrol and diesel at pumps. And here is the catch: the government does not like the pump price going up, so when crude becomes expensive the company must keep selling at the old price and lose money on every litre — even while its refinery is making more than ever.

Say it to a client in one minute

Never look at this sector as one industry. It is a spread business and a regulated retail business, and in 2026 they are pointing in opposite directions.

  • Refining is booming. BPCL earned $41.4 a barrel, HPCL $23.8, and Chennai Petroleum's margin rose from $3.22 to $8.78 — the best refining environment in years.
  • Marketing is bleeding. Diesel was sold at a loss of ₹25.2 a litre and petrol at ₹7.7, so HPCL lost ₹12,264 cr and BPCL ₹1,873 cr in a single quarter, against profits a year earlier.
  • LPG is the biggest hole: about ₹510 of loss per cylinder in the June quarter against ₹80 in March, and an industry under-recovery of ₹62,000 cr by August — against ₹30,000 cr of compensation approved by the Cabinet, payable in twelve tranches.
  • The structural change is the Russian discount disappearing. It had underwritten Indian refining economics for two years and, by August 2026, Urals was trading at or near parity with dated Brent.
  • The stocks have split accordingly: Chennai Petroleum +69%, HPCL −28%.

The pure refiners are the clean expression of a strong crack environment. The marketers are a bet on when the government lets prices rise, and that has always been a political question rather than a financial one.

Analogy

A refinery is a mutton shop. You buy the whole animal at one price and sell it in cuts, each worth something different, and your profit is the difference between the carcass and the cuts — the "crack". Skill lies in buying a cheaper carcass than your competitor and getting more of the expensive cuts out of it. The Indian twist is that the government has decided two of the cuts are essential to the poor, and fixes their price at the counter while the carcass price floats.

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Sourcing: huge ships bring crude from other countries; the buyer tries to find the cheapest one that the refinery can handle.
  • Refining: the crude is heated and separated into different fuels; clever equipment turns the cheap heavy leftovers into expensive light fuels.
  • Evacuation: the fuels travel through underground pipes and trains to storage depots near cities.
  • Retail: tankers take them to the pumps you see on the road.
  • Compensation: when the government keeps the price low, it later pays the companies back — but less than the loss, and much later.
Fund-manager lens

Model the two halves separately or you will get this sector wrong every time. Refining earns gross refining margin per barrel times throughput; it is a global spread business, and right now an exceptional one. Marketing earns a rupee margin per litre times volume; it is a regulated business whose margin is whatever is left between an international product price and an administered pump price. In a crude spike the first expands and the second goes negative — which is exactly why BPCL's record $41.4 margin produced a loss. The marketer's real asset is not the refinery but the retail network, and its real liability is political.

The distinction that matters

Three of these companies are oil marketing companies — they refine and retail, and the state expects them to absorb price shocks. Two are pure refiners — MRPL and CPCL — which sell their output to the marketers and keep the refining margin without the retail obligation. In a normal year the difference is modest. In a crude spike it is the whole story: in 2026 the pure refiners are up 69% and 9%, and the marketers are down 18% to 28%. The same split happened in 2022, when Chennai Petroleum rose 101% while HPCL fell 20%. If you have a view on crude, this distinction is the position.

Beyond the listed five

Roughly half of India's refining capacity sits outside this section: Reliance's Jamnagar complex, the largest refining site in the world, and Nayara Energy, part-owned by Rosneft. Both are export-oriented and unconstrained by domestic retail pricing obligations, and they set the competitive standard for complexity and crude flexibility. Nayara's ownership has also made it a direct target of sanctions, which is one of the channels through which Western measures reach the Indian market.

Why a $41 refining margin produced a loss

The arithmetic is worth doing once, because it explains the whole section. A refiner-marketer buys crude, converts it, and then sells a large share of the output through its own pumps. On the refining leg it books the international crack, which in the June quarter was exceptional. On the marketing leg it sells at a pump price that has not moved, and books the difference between that price and the international product value as its margin — which, with diesel at −₹25.2 a litre, was deeply negative. Because the marketers sell billions of litres, a negative rupee-per-litre margin overwhelms even an $41 barrel margin. HPCL, with the largest retail exposure relative to its refining capacity, lost the most: ₹12,264 cr in three months. MRPL and CPCL, which have no retail obligation, simply kept the crack.

Analogy

Imagine a bakery that mills its own flour. The mill is having a wonderful year, because flour prices have doubled. But the bakery must sell bread at last year's price by government order. The better the mill does, the worse the bakery does, and the owner reports one combined number that satisfies nobody. The way out is either to let bread prices rise, or for the state to write a cheque — which is exactly the ₹30,000 cr LPG compensation, and it covers less than half the hole.

7 · The business calendar

Elections, maintenance and the monsoon

  • The election calendar: the most reliable predictor of pump-price behaviour in India. Prices are rarely raised in the run-up to national or major state elections; 2018 and 2021 are the clearest examples.
  • Refinery turnarounds: planned maintenance shutdowns, usually scheduled in low-demand months, which cut throughput for a quarter.
  • The monsoon (June–September): diesel demand softens with construction and transport activity.
  • The winter and harvest quarters: diesel demand peaks with agricultural pumping and freight; LPG demand peaks in winter.
  • Fortnightly ATF revisions and daily retail revisions: the formal mechanism, which functions normally until crude spikes and then quietly stops.

The exceptions, and why

  • April–July failed five times, and two of them were disasters: 2013 (−29.8), when the rupee collapsed and under-recoveries exploded, and 2018 (−24.5), when crude ran to $86 before a general election.
  • The best April–July runs were 2015 (+45.0) and 2016 (+25.2) — the two years of cheap crude and freshly deregulated diesel.
  • January is the most consistent month (11 of 15), helped by December-quarter results and by budget expectations.
  • September–November is the weakest window (6 of 14, median −2.3), with 2024 (−17.6), 2020 (−15.3) and 2022 (−13.5) the worst.
How to use this

Barely. The seasonal signal in this section is weak because the dominant variable is a pricing decision, not a season. The more useful conditional rule is the one the record keeps repeating: when crude is rising fast, own the pure refiners and avoid the marketers; when crude is falling while pump prices stay put, own the marketers, because that is when the payback comes. 2022 and 2026 are the first case; 2023, when the basket rose 104%, is the second.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Eight drivers

  1. Product cracks: diesel, petrol and jet fuel spreads over crude, set in Singapore and the Gulf.
  2. Crude differentials: the discount on the grades India can buy — the Russian advantage, now gone.
  3. The retail pricing decision: whether pump prices are allowed to follow international prices. The single largest swing factor in the marketers' profit.
  4. LPG under-recovery and compensation: ₹62,000 cr outstanding against ₹30,000 cr approved.
  5. The rupee: a dollar cost base against rupee revenue.
  6. Inventory effects: large stock positions revalued with every move in crude.
  7. Demand growth: India's fuel consumption is still rising, unlike most large markets.
  8. Levies: excise duty, and the special additional excise duty that reduced IOC's reported margin from roughly $36 to $15.59.
The windfall levy is back — on product exports

The special additional excise duty, introduced in July 2022, was withdrawn in December 2024. It was re-imposed on 27 March 2026, after the West Asia conflict began, at ₹21.5 a litre on diesel exports and ₹29.5 on ATF exports, and has been reset every fortnight since — raised in early August (diesel to ₹25.5) and cut in mid-September to ₹20 on diesel, ₹15 on ATF and ₹0.5 on petrol. This time it applies to exports of petrol, diesel and ATF only, not to domestically produced crude, so 028's statement that the crude levy ended in December 2024 still holds for ONGC and Oil India. For refiners it is a direct cut to the margin on every exported litre: Indian Oil reported a GRM of $15.59 a barrel "net of SAED" against about $36 before it. Because the rate changes every two weeks, check the latest notification before modelling a quarter.

The anatomy of the Indian fuel cycle

  1. Crude rises. Refining margins expand; pump prices are held; marketing margins turn negative. The marketers report losses and de-rate — 2018, 2022, 2026.
  2. The state part-compensates, late and partially, through budgetary support: ₹30,000 cr against ₹62,000 cr today.
  3. Crude falls, and pump prices stay where they are. Marketing margins become extraordinary and the losses are recovered — 2015, 2023.
  4. Normality returns until the next spike, and the cycle repeats with different numbers.

Patterns that repeat

  • The pure refiners are the hedge. In both crude spikes in this record — 2022 and 2026 — Chennai Petroleum rose (+101%, +69%) while HPCL fell (−20%, −28%).
  • Deregulation was the single biggest structural event. Diesel was freed in October 2014; the basket rose 54% that year and 70% the next.
  • Election years suppress pricing freedom. 2018 and 2021 both show the marketers underperforming despite reasonable underlying conditions.
  • The payback is real but delayed. The 104% year of 2023 followed directly from the frozen prices of 2022.

11 · The global picture

India refines for the world, and buys from whoever is cheapest

India is one of the world's largest refining hubs and a substantial net exporter of refined products, particularly diesel and jet fuel to Europe. That position was hugely strengthened after 2022, when Russian crude became available at a discount that western buyers would not take: Indian refiners bought the crude, processed it, and sold the products into markets that had sanctioned the crude but not the products. Volumes reached roughly 1.9 million barrels a day by May 2026, having peaked near 2.3 million as refiners front-loaded purchases before a waiver expired. Then the arbitrage closed. By August 2026 the discount had been eliminated — Urals trading at or near parity with dated Brent — after Black Sea disruption, stronger Chinese buying and a sharp fall in Indian purchases. February 2026's imports of 1.06 million barrels a day were the lowest since September 2022.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • From subsidised distribution to deregulated retail (2010–2014): petrol was freed in 2010 and diesel in October 2014, turning the marketers into real businesses — until crude spikes revealed the freedom was conditional.
  • From simple to complex refining: investment in secondary processing let Indian refiners buy heavier, cheaper crude — the foundation of the export position.
  • From diversified sourcing to Russian dependence and back (2022–2026): a four-year round trip that gave Indian refining an extraordinary cost advantage and then took it away.

12 · Where the business is going

Six shifts

  1. Refinery-to-petrochemical integration: converting more of the barrel into polymers and aromatics rather than fuels, the main defence against peak fuel demand (see 002).
  2. Higher complexity: more cracking and coking capacity to handle heavier, cheaper crude slates.
  3. Ethanol blending: a material and growing share of the petrol pool, which displaces refinery volume and changes the economics of the pump.
  4. Retail diversification: convenience formats, electric-vehicle charging (see 022) and non-fuel income at the forecourt.
  5. Compressed and liquefied natural gas retail: the marketers are also city gas licensees and CNG retailers (see 034).
  6. Green hydrogen and biofuels at refineries: early, capital-intensive, and driven by mandates rather than returns.

The honest read

Everything in this section turns on one question: when do pump prices move? The refining half is performing as well as it ever has, and if the marketers were allowed to pass through international product prices, the group's earnings would be extraordinary rather than negative. They are not being allowed to, and the history in this primer says that changes only after crude falls — at which point prices stay put and the payback arrives, as it did in 2015 and 2023. That is a genuine, repeatable pattern, but it requires being early into a losing business and waiting for a political decision, which is a different discipline from owning a compounder. The cleaner expressions today are the two pure refiners, which capture the crack with no retail liability — though note that CPCL's 69% rise has already done much of that work, and that the vanished Russian discount removes a structural support the whole industry had relied on. Anyone quoting this sector's flat basket return as evidence it is holding up is quoting an equal weighting that gives 40% of the basket to the two smallest companies.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What was the marketing margin per litre on petrol and diesel through the quarter, and where is it today?
  2. What is the cumulative LPG under-recovery, and how much compensation has actually been received rather than approved?
  3. What is the gross refining margin before and after levies, and what is the core margin excluding inventory effects?
  4. What was the inventory gain or loss, and how many days of crude and product are held?
  5. What share of the crude slate was Russian this quarter, and what discount was realised?
  6. What is the current crude slate flexibility if discounted barrels are unavailable?
  7. What is the capital expenditure plan, and how is it funded given the quarter's losses?
  8. What is the petrochemical share of the barrel today and in three years?
  9. (Pure refiners) What is the pricing formula with the parent marketer, and does it move with international cracks?

Red flags

  • Borrowing rising to fund working capital while losses continue.
  • Compensation announced but not received, counted as good news twice.
  • Capital expenditure deferred to protect dividends, which stores up competitive weakness.
  • A refining margin flattered by inventory gains rather than cracks.

18 · Myths and live debates

What you'll hear, and the evidence

ClaimEvidence
"High crude is good for refiners."For pure refiners, often. For refiner-marketers, no: BPCL earned $41.4 a barrel and lost ₹1,873 cr, because the retail leg was selling diesel at −₹25.2 a litre.
"Fuel pricing is deregulated in India."Formally since 2010 and 2014. In practice pump prices stop moving whenever crude spikes, which is precisely when the mechanism is needed.
"The government compensates the losses."Partially and late: ₹30,000 cr approved in twelve tranches against ₹62,000 cr of LPG under-recovery alone, with nothing for negative auto-fuel margins.
"The Russian discount is a permanent edge."It lasted two years and was gone by August 2026, with Urals at or near parity to dated Brent. Structural advantages built on sanctions arbitrage are rented, not owned.
"The sector held up this year — it is flat against a falling market."The equal-weighted basket is flat; the median stock is −18%. Two small pure refiners are carrying a basket that gives them 40% of the weight.
"Electric vehicles make refining a value trap."Indian fuel demand is still growing, and the sector's answer is petrochemical integration. The nearer risk is regulatory pricing, not demand destruction.

19 · Glossary

Every term you'll meet

Refining

Refinery
A plant that separates and converts crude oil into usable fuels and feedstocks.
Atmospheric distillation
The first separation of crude by boiling point in a tall column.
Cracking
Breaking heavy molecules into lighter, more valuable ones; the heart of a complex refinery.
Fluid catalytic cracker (FCC)
The unit that converts heavy gas oil mainly into petrol.
Hydrocracker
A unit that uses hydrogen to convert heavy feed into diesel and jet fuel.
Coker
A unit that converts the heaviest residue into lighter products and petroleum coke.
Complexity (Nelson index)
A measure of a refinery's secondary processing capability; higher complexity means cheaper crude can be used.
Throughput
Crude processed in a period. Indian Oil ran 19.2 million tonnes in Q1 FY27.
Capacity utilisation
Throughput against nameplate capacity; IOC ran at 109.4%.
Distillate yield
The share of output that is middle distillates such as diesel and jet fuel (see 028).
Turnaround
A planned maintenance shutdown, which removes a refinery's output for weeks.
Crude slate
The mix of crude grades a refinery processes.
Sour and sweet crude
High-sulphur and low-sulphur crude; sour grades are cheaper and need more processing.
Heavy and light crude
Denser or lighter crude; heavier grades yield less light product without conversion units.

Margins and pricing

Gross refining margin (GRM)
Value of products minus cost of crude, per barrel. BPCL reported $41.4 in Q1 FY27.
Crack spread
The margin on one product over crude, such as the diesel crack.
Benchmark GRM
The regional margin (typically Singapore) against which a refiner's own margin is compared.
Marketing margin
Pump price minus the delivered cost of the product, per litre. It was −₹25.2 on diesel in Q1 FY27.
Under-recovery
The shortfall when a regulated product is sold below cost; ₹62,000 cr on LPG by August 2026.
Compensation / budgetary support
Government payments to cover under-recoveries; ₹30,000 cr approved, in twelve tranches.
Inventory gain or loss
Revaluation of crude and product stock as prices move within a quarter.
Trade parity pricing
The formula that values domestic products against import and export parity.
Special additional excise duty (SAED)
The windfall levy. From July 2022 to December 2024 it applied to domestic crude and to fuel exports; since 27 March 2026 it has applied again to exports of petrol, diesel and ATF only, reset every fortnight. It reduced IOC's reported GRM by roughly $20 a barrel this quarter (see the note in section 9).
Excise duty and VAT
Central and state taxes that make up a large share of the retail price.
Dated Brent and Urals
The North Sea benchmark and the Russian export grade; their differential was the Indian refiners' advantage until August 2026.
Differential / discount
The price of a crude grade relative to the benchmark — pure margin for the buyer.

LPG
Liquefied petroleum gas, sold in subsidised domestic cylinders and at market prices commercially.
HSD
High-speed diesel, India's largest fuel by volume.
MS

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. How did BPCL earn $41.4 a barrel and still lose ₹1,873 cr?

The marketing leg. It sold diesel at a loss of ₹25.2 a litre and petrol at ₹7.7, and retail volumes are large enough to overwhelm an exceptional refining margin.

2. What is the difference between an OMC and a pure refiner?

An OMC refines and retails and is expected to absorb price shocks; a pure refiner like MRPL or CPCL sells product to the marketers and keeps the crack without the retail obligation.

3. What happened to the LPG loss per cylinder this year?

It rose from about ₹80 in the March quarter to about ₹510 in the June quarter, taking industry under-recovery to ₹62,000 cr by August.

4. How much compensation has been approved, and on what terms?

₹30,000 cr to IOCL, BPCL and HPCL, payable in twelve tranches — less than half the LPG hole, and nothing for negative auto-fuel margins.

5. Why was 2023 such a good year for this sector?

Crude fell while pump prices stayed where they were, so marketing margins expanded enormously. The basket rose 104% against the Nifty's 20%.

6. What was the Russian discount, and what happened to it?

A structural cost advantage from buying sanctioned crude cheaply for two years. By August 2026 it was eliminated, with Urals at or near parity to dated Brent.

7. Why is the 2026 basket return misleading?

The basket is flat but the median stock is −18%: equal weighting gives the two small pure refiners 40% of the weight, and they are the only risers.

8. What was the single most important structural reform for this sector?

Diesel deregulation in October 2014, which converted the marketers from subsidy conduits into businesses with a margin. The basket rose 54% that year and 70% the next.

9. Why did IOC report a GRM of $15.59 when peers reported far more?

It was struck net of a special additional excise duty; management put the normalised figure at roughly $36 a barrel.

10. What is the best single predictor of a marketer's profit?

Whether pump prices are being allowed to move — which historically tracks the election calendar more closely than it tracks crude.
