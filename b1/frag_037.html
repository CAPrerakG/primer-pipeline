Power Generation & Integrated Utilities

The companies that turn coal, water, gas and sunlight into electricity and sell it to distribution utilities under contracts that can run for twenty-five years. Most of the sector earns a regulated return on capital, which makes it a bond with a construction programme attached; a minority sells into the open market, which makes it a commodity trade. Telling those two apart is the whole job.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Record demand, record building, falling shares

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Electricity cannot be stored in useful quantities, so it has to be made at the exact moment somebody switches something on. These companies own the machines that make it: huge boilers burning coal, dams with turbines, fields of solar panels. They usually sign a contract promising to supply a state electricity board for twenty-five years at an agreed price that covers their costs plus a fixed profit. That makes their earnings very predictable — but it also means that when everyone suddenly needs much more electricity, they do not earn much more. They only earn more by building more machines.

Say it to a client in one minute

This is a regulated-return industry with a growth programme, and the market has stopped paying for the programme.

  • Demand has never been higher: peak demand of 270.8 GW in May 2026, up about 12% for the April–June quarter, with consumption up 8.9% in April alone.
  • Supply is being built at record pace: 64,695 MW added in FY26. But 50,905 MW of that was renewable, which is a different industry with different economics (see 038).
  • Regulated generators earn on assets, not on prices. NTPC's profit rose 13% to ₹6,896 cr with 1,796 MW added and 35.7 GW under construction; its coal plants ran at 76.71% against a national average of 70.32%.
  • Merchant exposure is where the leverage is: Adani Power's profit rose 47% to ₹4,867 cr on record demand, and its stock is up 44% in a market where the median generator is down 4%.
  • The tail is still dangerous. Reliance Power −39%, Gujarat Energy −41%, GVK −85% from its peak: this section has never fully cleaned up after the 2010s capacity boom.

Analogy

A regulated generator is a landlord on a twenty-five-year lease with the rent set by a tribunal: dependable, unexciting, and it grows only when the landlord builds another building. A merchant generator is the same building let out nightly at whatever the market pays — empty for years, then extraordinary during a festival. India's power sector is mostly landlords with a few hoteliers, and 2026 has been a very good year to be a hotelier.

The reason this is an asset-growth business

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Fuel: coal arrives by train, or water sits behind a dam, or the sun shines.
  • Generation: heat makes steam, steam spins a turbine, the turbine makes electricity.
  • Transmission: very high voltage lines carry it hundreds of kilometres with as little loss as possible.
  • Offtaker: a state electricity company has promised to buy it for twenty-five years — and is often slow to pay.
  • Distribution: smaller wires take it to houses, where a lot of it is lost or never billed.
  • Return: a regulator decides how much profit the generator is allowed to make on the money it invested.
Fund-manager lens

For a regulated generator, value is a function of capitalised assets and the allowed return, not of power prices — which is why NTPC's 13% profit growth came from capacity additions and efficiency, not from the record demand. The three things worth tracking are therefore: capacity commissioned against plan (NTPC added 1,796 MW and has 35.7 GW under construction), plant availability, since the return is conditional on it, and receivables from distribution companies, which is where the sector's periodic crises begin. For merchant generators, invert all of it: what matters is short-term tariffs and utilisation, which is why Adani Power's earnings rose 47% in a hot summer.

How to use this section

Three groups, and they do not belong in one basket. Regulated compounders — NTPC, NHPC, SJVN, NLC — whose earnings grow with capitalised assets and whose share prices have de-rated 27–58% from 2024 peaks. Integrated and transitioning utilities — Tata Power, Torrent, JSW Energy — part regulated, part renewable growth platform. Merchant and distressed — Adani Power at one end earning record profits from a hot summer, and Reliance Power, GVK, RattanIndia and India Power at the other, still 65–85% below their peaks a decade after the capacity boom that created them. The equal-weighted basket blends all three, which is why its −5% tells you less than the individual numbers do.

Why record demand did not lift most of these companies

Because a regulated generator does not capture a demand spike. If a plant is contracted at a tariff that recovers costs plus a fixed return, then a 12% jump in peak demand raises the amount of electricity it sends out but not the profit per unit — and since fixed-cost recovery depends on availability rather than dispatch, even the volume effect is muted. The benefit shows up instead in the merchant market, where the uncontracted unit is sold at whatever a shortage will bear. That is the entire explanation for the 2026 scoreboard: Adani Power, with the largest merchant and short-term book, earned 47% more and rose 44%, while NTPC, Tata Power and NHPC — each with more of their capacity locked into contracts — grew profits modestly and their shares went nowhere. The corollary matters for the next few years: 50,905 MW of the 64,695 MW added in FY26 was renewable, and renewable supply arrives precisely in the daytime hours when merchant prices used to spike.

Analogy

A regulated generator is a taxi on a fixed monthly retainer: a surge in demand means more trips, not more money. A merchant generator is the same taxi on surge pricing. Both are useful; only one of them has a good monsoon.

7 · The business calendar

A weather business with a regulatory clock

  • April–June: peak demand season. Cooling load drives consumption to records — 270.8 GW on 21 May 2026 — and merchant tariffs with it.
  • July–September: the monsoon cuts demand, fills reservoirs and lifts hydro output, displacing thermal generation.
  • October–December: hydro fades, thermal plants run hardest, and the industry commissions capacity before the year-end. Historically the best window for these stocks, 9 of 14 years.
  • January–March: year-end capitalisation and tariff filings; the weakest window for share prices.
  • Tariff orders and regulatory cycles: multi-year tariff periods reset the allowed return, which is a step change rather than a seasonal one.

The exceptions, and why

  • October–December is the window that works — 9 of 14 years, median +3.9 — and its failures cluster in de-rating years: 2025 (−14.8), 2020 (−9.7), 2022 (−7.4), 2024 (−7.3).
  • April–July is unreliable (8 of 15, median +2.2) despite containing the demand peak, because the monsoon's arrival ends the surge inside the window. Its worst readings were 2018 (−24.8), 2013 (−21.0) and 2015 (−15.0).
  • February–March has failed in ten of fifteen years, the most consistent negative in the section.
  • The best single readings — 2021 and 2023 — were re-rating years, not weather years, which is the recurring lesson of this sector's price history.
How to use this

Treat October–December as the seasonal tilt and ignore the rest. But note what the record actually shows: the two enormous years, 2021 (+84%) and 2023 (+95%), were driven by a change in how the market valued these assets rather than by anything the companies did that year, and the two bad ones, 2018 (−36%) and 2025 (−12%), were the reverse. In a sector where earnings are regulated and slow-moving, the multiple does nearly all the work — so the honest question is not what demand will do but what the market is willing to pay for a regulated asset base, which is currently 20–35% less than it was in 2024.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Eight drivers

  1. Capacity commissioned: the only growth lever for a regulated generator — NTPC targets 9,557 MW in FY27.
  2. Allowed return and tariff orders: the regulated profit rate on that capital.
  3. Plant availability and load factor: availability earns the fixed cost; load factor earns the variable margin.
  4. Merchant tariffs: the swing factor for the uncontracted units, and the reason Adani Power outperformed.
  5. Peak demand and weather: 270.8 GW in May 2026, up about 12% year on year.
  6. Fuel availability: coal stocks at about 13 days, and gas affordability (see 033, 036).
  7. Discom receivables and payment security: the credit quality of the offtaker.
  8. Renewable displacement: 50,905 MW of the FY26 addition was renewable, which erodes daytime thermal utilisation.

The anatomy of India's power cycle

  1. A shortage appears and everybody builds. The 2008–2012 boom produced plants without coal linkages, without signed contracts and with imported-coal economics that never worked.
  2. The bill arrives: stressed assets, restructuring, and a decade of underperformance — 2013 to 2020 contains five negative years.
  3. Demand catches up with the overbuild, merchant tariffs spike, and the survivors re-rate violently: +84% in 2021, +95% in 2023.
  4. Capital floods back in, now into renewables, and the multiple deflates while the assets keep growing — 2025 and 2026.

Patterns that repeat

  • The multiple, not the earnings, drives the sector. Regulated earnings grew steadily through 2018's −36% and 2023's +95% alike.
  • Merchant exposure is the only way to monetise a demand spike — 2022 and 2026 both belonged to Adani Power.
  • The distressed names never fully recover. Reliance Power, GVK, RattanIndia and India Power all remain 65–85% below their peaks, and each bounce has been a trade, not a turnaround.
  • Peaks cluster: most of this section topped between June and October 2024.

11 · The global picture

The world's fastest-growing large electricity market

India is adding electricity demand faster than any other large economy, and unlike most of them it is still adding coal capacity at the same time as it builds renewables at record pace. The FY26 addition of 64,695 MW — of which 50,905 MW was renewable excluding large hydro — is among the largest single-year build-outs anywhere, yet coal and lignite still supplied 69.5% of electricity in the April–June quarter. That combination is the defining feature of the Indian market: renewables take the daytime energy share while thermal is retained for the evening peak and for reliability, which is why 230.8 GW of coal and lignite capacity is being run harder rather than retired.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • From state monopoly to independent producers (1990s–2000s): private generation was allowed, and a wave of projects followed.
  • From boom to stressed assets (2012–2020): plants built without fuel or contracts became the banking system's problem.
  • From thermal to renewables-led addition (2020s): renewables are now four-fifths of new capacity, which changes who builds, who finances, and what a thermal plant is worth.

12 · Where the business is going

Six shifts

  1. Firm and dispatchable renewables: contracts requiring round-the-clock supply, which pair solar with storage and hydro (see 038, 046).
  2. Thermal flexibilisation: running coal plants at lower minimum loads so they can ramp around solar — a technical change with real cost.
  3. Pumped hydro and battery storage: the answer to the evening peak; JSW Energy and NTPC are both building it.
  4. Nuclear entry by private capital: Adani Atomic Energy targeting 10 GW by 2035, alongside policy moves to open the sector.
  5. Cross-border hydro: Adani Power's 5 GW partnership with Bhutan's Druk Green.
  6. Integrated utilities with consumer businesses: Tata Power's rooftop, EV charging and distribution franchises, which earn retail rather than wholesale margins.

The honest read

This is a sector where the fundamentals and the share prices have genuinely diverged, and the divergence is rational rather than an anomaly. Demand is at records and capacity is being built at a record pace — but four-fifths of that build is renewable, which earns a lower and falling tariff, and the regulated generators capture none of the demand spike because their returns are fixed. What the 2023–24 re-rating priced was a decade of asset growth at a premium multiple; what 2025 and 2026 have delivered is the asset growth without the premium. The practical division is the one in section 4: if you want the demand cycle, you need merchant exposure, and Adani Power is the only large way to own it — with the corresponding risk that a normal summer removes the earnings. If you want compounding, the regulated names now offer their asset growth 20–35% cheaper than in 2024, with the caveat that their growth is increasingly renewable and therefore lower-return. And the distressed tail, which has bounced three times in fifteen years and recovered none of its losses, remains a trade rather than an investment.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. How much capacity was commissioned and capitalised this quarter, and what is the plan for the year?
  2. What share of capacity is regulated, long-term contracted and merchant?
  3. What was the realised merchant tariff, and what volume was sold that way?
  4. What is plant availability, and was the full fixed-cost recovery achieved?
  5. What are receivable days from distribution companies, and what is overdue?
  6. What is the return on equity on new renewable projects compared with the regulated thermal base?
  7. What is the capital expenditure plan and its funding mix?
  8. What are coal stock days and the current fuel cost per unit?
  9. (Transitioning utilities) What happens to group returns as the mix shifts from regulated thermal to bid-tariff renewables?

Red flags

  • Capacity targets repeatedly pushed out by a year.
  • Profit growth from other income or one-off regulatory recoveries.
  • Merchant exposure being described as upside in a shortage without stating the downside in a normal year.
  • New projects signed at tariffs below the group's existing return on equity.

19 · Glossary

Every term you'll meet

Technical

Megawatt (MW) and gigawatt (GW)
Units of power capacity; a gigawatt is a thousand megawatts. India's peak demand reached 270.8 GW.
Billion units (BU)
Billions of kilowatt-hours of electricity generated. NTPC produced 93.6 BU in a quarter.
Plant load factor (PLF)
Actual generation as a share of maximum possible; NTPC's coal plants ran at 76.71% against a 70.32% national average.
Availability factor
The share of time a plant is ready to run. Regulated plants are paid fixed costs on availability, not generation.
Baseload
Capacity designed to run continuously, typically coal or nuclear.
Peaking capacity
Plant used only in the hours of highest demand, usually gas, hydro or storage.
Flexibilisation
Modifying a coal plant to run at low minimum load so it can ramp around solar output.
Pumped hydro storage
Pumping water uphill when power is cheap and releasing it at peak; the largest form of grid storage.
Round-the-clock (RTC) power
A contract to supply firm power at all hours, usually by combining renewables with storage or thermal.
Evacuation
Getting generated power onto the transmission network (see 039).
Grid frequency
The measure of supply and demand balance; deviations are penalised.
Heat rate
The energy of fuel needed per unit of electricity produced; the core efficiency measure of a thermal plant.
Auxiliary consumption
Electricity a power station uses to run itself, which never reaches the grid.
Supercritical and ultra-supercritical
Higher-temperature, higher-efficiency coal plant designs that burn less coal per unit.
Ramp rate
How fast a plant can change output — the property that decides whether it can follow solar.
Curtailment
Generation the grid refuses to take, usually renewable, when supply exceeds demand or lines are full.
Merit order
The ranking by which the cheapest generation is dispatched first; renewables sit at the top, costly thermal at the bottom.
Hydro storage and run-of-river
Dammed hydro that can be dispatched, versus hydro that generates only as the river flows.
Captive power plant
A generator built to supply its owner's own factory rather than the grid.

Commercial and regulatory

Power purchase agreement (PPA)
A long-term contract, usually twenty-five years, to sell power at an agreed tariff.
Offtaker
The buyer under a PPA, usually a state distribution company.
Discom
A distribution company: the retailer of electricity, and the weakest credit in the chain.
Merchant power
Electricity sold without a long-term contract, at market prices (see 040).
Two-part tariff
A tariff split into a fixed capacity charge and a variable energy charge — the structure that protects regulated generators.
Regulated return on equity
The profit rate a regulator allows on equity invested in an approved project.
Regulated asset base
The capital on which that return is earned; it grows only when capacity is capitalised.
Capitalisation
Moving a commissioned project into the regulated asset base so it starts earning.
Fuel cost pass-through
Recovering fuel cost from the buyer, insulating the generator from coal or gas prices.
Late payment surcharge
The penalty regime that enforces discom payment discipline.
Renewable purchase obligation (RPO)
The minimum renewable share a buyer must procure.
Stressed asset
A project unable to service its debt — the legacy of the 2008–2012 capacity boom.
Coal linkage
An assured long-term coal supply entitlement (see 036).
Independent power producer (IPP)
A private generator selling to utilities rather than to its own consumers.
Integrated utility
A company combining generation with transmission or distribution.
Distribution franchise
A private operator running a state utility's distribution area under contract.
Aggregate technical and commercial (AT&C) losses
Electricity lost in the wires plus electricity delivered but never paid for; the central problem of Indian distribution.
Cross-subsidy

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. Why didn't record demand lift most power stocks?

Regulated generators earn a fixed return on capital regardless of demand. Only merchant exposure monetises a spike — which is why Adani Power rose 44% and NTPC fell 2%.

2. What was India's peak demand in 2026?

270.8 GW on 21 May 2026, after 256.1 GW in April; peak demand rose about 12% in the April–June quarter.

3. How much capacity did India add in FY26, and of what kind?

64,695 MW, of which 50,905 MW was renewable excluding large hydro.

4. What is a two-part tariff, and why does it matter?

A fixed capacity charge plus a variable energy charge. The fixed part is earned on availability, so a regulated generator is paid even when it is not dispatched.

5. What is NTPC building?

35.7 GW under construction — 15.7 GW coal, 16.4 GW renewable, 3.6 GW hydro — within a 126.6 GW portfolio, targeting 9,557 MW of additions in FY27.

6. Why did JSW Energy's profit fall 36% while it grew?

Growth spending and interest costs. It added about 1.1 GW of renewables and reached 14,535 MW, with renewables at 61% of capacity.

7. Which seasonal window works here, and why?

October–December, 9 of 14 years: the monsoon ends, hydro fades, thermal plants run hardest and capacity is commissioned before year-end.

8. What still supplies most of India's electricity?

Coal and lignite — 69.5% of supply in April–June 2026, from 230.8 GW of installed capacity.

9. What happened to the stressed generators of the 2010s?

They bounced in 2017, 2021 and 2023–24 but remain 65–85% below their peaks. Each rally has been liquidity rather than recovery.

10. What drives this sector's share prices more than earnings?

The multiple. Regulated earnings grew steadily through both 2018's −36% and 2023's +95%.

  • NTPC: the company's Q1 FY27 results statement; its operational and financial snapshot, with capacity, load factors and targets; the consolidated profit increase; generation growth and the expansion pipeline.
  • Adani Power: the company's Q1 FY27 release; revenue, profit and the 45 GW expansion plan; load factors, the phased build-out and the Bhutan and atomic initiatives.
  • Tata Power and JSW Energy: Tata Power's Q1 FY27 profit and 12 GW renewable portfolio; the segment drivers; JSW Energy's 36% profit decline; its capacity additions and 30 GW target.
