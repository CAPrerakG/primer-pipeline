Transformers

Every unit of electricity passes through several transformers between the power station and your socket — stepped up to travel, stepped down to be used. The world does not have enough of them. Grid expansion, renewables and data centres have created a global shortage with lead times measured in years, and Indian makers are filling order books at home and abroad. The limit on their profits is a single imported material: the special steel at the heart of every transformer.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

A global shortage, built from imported steel

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Electricity travels long distances at very high voltage because that loses less energy — but high voltage is dangerous, so before it reaches your house it has to be turned down, several times. A transformer is the box that does that: two coils of copper wire wrapped around a core of special steel, sitting in a tank of oil. There is one on the pole outside your street and huge ones, as big as a bus, at every substation. Right now the whole world is building new power lines and solar farms and data centres, and every one needs transformers, so there are not enough to go round. Indian factories have more orders than ever. Their problem is that the special steel for the core mostly comes from abroad, and it has become expensive and scarce.

Say it to a client in one minute

Demand is secular and global; margins are hostage to one imported input.

  • Order books are at records: Transformers & Rectifiers ₹6,630 cr (+26%) with a ₹1,000 cr-plus Power Grid order and ₹23,000 cr of enquiries; Atlanta Electricals ₹3,117 cr after record inflows of ₹972 cr in a quarter.
  • Revenue is growing fast: Atlanta +48%, Indo Tech +39%, Voltamp +28%.
  • Margins are being squeezed by steel: Voltamp's EBITDA margin fell to 14.77% from 17.15% on input costs. India imports about 90% of its CRGO steel, a 30% shortfall, and an anti-dumping investigation into CRGO imports opened on 22 June 2026 — protection for domestic steel that raises transformer makers' costs.
  • Capacity is the other constraint: Voltamp's new 6,000 MVA plant slipped to October 2026 on equipment delays; Transformers & Rectifiers' output was held back by its own expansion.
  • The stocks have been violent: +235% and +218% in 2023–24, −35% in 2025, +16% so far in 2026 with a median of +24%.

Analogy

A transformer is a gearbox for electricity. High voltage is a high gear — efficient for the motorway — and low voltage is a low gear for the last few streets. Just as every road trip needs gear changes, every unit of electricity needs several transformers, which is why grid expansion multiplies transformer demand rather than merely adding to it.

A long service tail

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Steel core: thin sheets of special steel that make magnetism work efficiently, stacked into a frame. Most of it comes from abroad.
  • Copper windings: thick copper wire wound in coils around the core.
  • Factory: the pieces are assembled, dried and sealed in a tank of oil.
  • Testing: every unit is tested with very high voltage before it leaves.
  • Customers: power companies, solar farms, factories, and now buyers in America who cannot get enough.
  • Service: a transformer works for thirty to forty years, with checks along the way.
Fund-manager lens

Two numbers decide a transformer maker: capacity utilisation and the spread between contract price and CRGO cost. In a shortage, capacity is sold out, so growth depends on how fast new MVA comes on line — and both Voltamp and Transformers & Rectifiers have seen expansions delay output. Meanwhile CRGO is 30–35% of cost and 90% imported, so a steel-price move lands straight on the margin unless contracts carry price-variation clauses. Order books and enquiry pipelines are impressive across the section, but the margin trajectory in 2026 — Voltamp down 238 basis points — says the steel side is winning for now. Watch price-variation clause coverage and the timing of new capacity more closely than order intake.

Why margins fell while orders boomed

A transformer is ordered, priced and then built over months — often more than a year for a large power unit. If CRGO steel and copper rise between the bid and the build, and the contract lacks a full price-variation clause, the maker absorbs the difference. That is the story of 2026: demand is extraordinary, but the core steel is 90% imported, India's supply is about 30% short of consumption, and the government has opened an anti-dumping investigation into imports from China, Japan, South Korea and Russia, which protects future domestic producers at the cost of today's buyers. Voltamp's 238 basis-point margin decline with 28% revenue growth is the result in miniature. Relief is expected when JSW-JFE's 350,000-tonne CRGO capacity is fully commissioned, which is targeted for FY2028; until then India depends on imports for more than 85% of its needs.

Analogy

A transformer maker in 2026 is a bakery with a queue round the block and a flour shortage. It can sell every loaf it bakes at a good price — but the flour comes from abroad, costs more each month, and a new tariff may be put on it. The queue does not guarantee a profit; the flour contract does.

7 · The business calendar

Order in spring, dispatch in winter

  • April–July: utilities release budgets and tenders; order announcements cluster — April is among the strongest months in the series.
  • August–September: the monsoon slows site installation and dispatch; the weakest window (3 of 14 years).
  • October–December: the dispatch and commissioning season before the financial year-end — the best window in the Power family, 12 of 14 years.
  • January–March: year-end billing, followed by weak share prices in most years.
  • Steel contract cycles: CRGO is bought on contracts that reset periodically, which is when margin pressure appears in results.

The exceptions, and why

  • October–December failed only twice in fourteen years: 2025 (−20.3), the de-rating year, and 2022 (−11.4). The best were 2015 (+45.3) and 2024 (+43.6).
  • April–July works 10 of 15 (median +11.1), with failures in 2018 (−26.4), 2019 (−16.6), 2017 (−13.1), 2013 (−11.4) and 2012 (−4.1) — the years before the upcycle.
  • August–September is reliably poor: 3 of 14 years, median −7.5, with 2021 at −30.6 and 2018 at −22.0.
  • January–March has failed 11 times in 15, the worst being 2013 (−29.9) and 2025 (−28.3).
How to use this

This is the strongest and most consistent calendar pattern found in the Power family: own into October–December, avoid August–September and the first quarter. It has a plausible cause in the utility ordering and dispatch cycle. The caveats are the usual ones for a small, volatile basket — three stocks for most of the record, all small caps, with magnitudes that reflect liquidity as much as fundamentals. Use it as a timing tilt on a position justified by the order book and the CRGO outlook, not as a reason to own the sector.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Eight drivers

  1. Transmission and distribution capex: India's grid plan to 2032 more than doubles transformation capacity (see 039).
  2. Renewable additions: every solar and wind farm needs step-up transformers (see 038).
  3. Export demand: the United States and other markets face severe transformer shortages.
  4. CRGO steel prices and availability: 30–35% of cost, 90% imported, under anti-dumping review.
  5. Copper prices: the second commodity exposure.
  6. Capacity commissioning: sold-out factories grow only as fast as new MVA comes on line.
  7. Price-variation clauses: decide who bears the commodity risk.
  8. Data-centre and industrial demand: dry-type and specialty units at premium prices.

The anatomy of this cycle

  1. A decade of thin margins (2012–2019): utilities were the only customers, price was everything, and the listed makers traded as distressed small caps.
  2. The global shortage (2021–2024): grid expansion, renewables and data centres met capacity that had not been built for years. Lead times stretched, prices rose, and the stocks returned +235% and +218% in consecutive years.
  3. New supply and new costs (2025): capacity announcements, new listings and rising CRGO prices triggered a 35% fall.
  4. Demand outlasts the de-rating (2026): record order books and export demand, with margins now set by steel.

Patterns that repeat

  • Small caps in a shortage re-rate spectacularly — and give back a third when supply and costs catch up.
  • New listings cluster at the top: five makers listed between December 2023 and March 2026.
  • The commodity input decides the margin, not the order book: the same lesson as tyres (025) and helmets (026).
  • The calendar is unusually consistent, with October–December working in 12 of 14 years.

11 · The global picture

A worldwide shortage India can supply

The transformer shortage is a global phenomenon. The United States faces severe supply constraints, with lead times for large power transformers running to years, as grid replacement, renewables and data centres all compete for the same limited factory capacity; European utilities report similar queues. Transformer manufacturing is capital-intensive and slow to expand, and the grain-oriented steel it depends on comes from a handful of producers in China, Japan, South Korea and Russia. India's makers are well placed to export — they are cost-competitive and many already have international type-test approvals — but they share the world's dependence on imported CRGO, and India's own plan to more than double transformation capacity by 2032 competes for the same factories.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • From surplus to shortage (2021 onwards): a decade of underinvestment in capacity met a synchronised global demand surge.
  • From domestic to export-led growth: Indian makers winning overseas orders as foreign utilities look beyond their own sold-out suppliers.
  • From imported to domestic CRGO (in progress): JSW-JFE's capacity, targeted for FY2028, is the first serious attempt to end the import dependence.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Higher voltage ratings: 765 kV and HVDC converter transformers for long-distance transmission (see 039).
  2. Amorphous-core distribution transformers: lower no-load losses, mandated in some efficiency programmes.
  3. Dry-type and ester-filled units: safer and more environmentally friendly, for cities and data centres (see 035).
  4. Smart transformers and monitoring: sensors for temperature, dissolved gas and load, enabling predictive maintenance.
  5. Renewable-specific designs: inverter-duty transformers built for the harmonics and cycling of solar farms.
  6. Domestic CRGO production: the supply-side technology shift that decides the industry's margins after FY2028.

The honest read

The demand case for transformers is as strong as for anything in the market: a domestic grid plan that more than doubles transformation capacity, renewables that each need step-up units, and an export market where American buyers wait years for supply. The weakness is that the profit is set by a material India mostly cannot make, and the policy response — an anti-dumping probe — will raise its cost before domestic supply arrives in FY2028. That is why 2026 shows record orders alongside falling margins. The makers best placed are those with price-variation protection, export access and new capacity arriving on time; the order book alone tells you little. After two years of +235% and +218%, a 35% fall, and a partial recovery, valuations already assume the shortage lasts — so the question worth underwriting is whether margins recover when CRGO supply does.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What share of the order book carries price-variation clauses for CRGO and copper?
  2. How much CRGO is contracted, at what price, and for how long?
  3. What would the anti-dumping outcome do to input costs?
  4. What is capacity utilisation, and when does new MVA come on line?
  5. What is the export share of orders, and at what margin relative to domestic?
  6. What is the enquiry-to-order conversion rate?
  7. What is the high-voltage versus distribution mix, and where is it moving?
  8. What test-bay capacity constrains high-voltage output?

Red flags

  • Orders booked at fixed prices while CRGO is rising.
  • Margins falling for several consecutive quarters despite price increases.
  • Capacity announcements timed to the top of the cycle.
  • Heavy dependence on a single domestic utility customer.

19 · Glossary

Every term you'll meet

The product

Transformer
A device that changes voltage using electromagnetic induction between two windings on a steel core.
Power transformer
A large unit at a transmission substation, typically 66 kV and above.
Distribution transformer
A smaller unit that steps voltage down to the level used by consumers.
Extra-high voltage (EHV)
220 kV and above; the most demanding and highest-value transformers.
Generator step-up transformer
Raises a power station's output to transmission voltage (see 041).
Inverter-duty transformer
Designed for solar inverters' output and cycling (see 038).
Dry-type transformer
An oil-free design used indoors, in buildings, metros and data centres.
Converter transformer
Used in HVDC stations to convert between AC and DC (see 039).
MVA
Megavolt-amperes: the unit of a transformer's rating, and of factory capacity. Voltamp's backlog was 10,270 MVA.
GVA
Gigavolt-amperes; India's transformation capacity is planned to reach 2,342 GVA by 2032.
Core
The stacked electrical-steel frame that carries the magnetic field.
Winding
The copper coils; high-voltage and low-voltage windings on the same core.
Transformer oil
Insulating and cooling oil in the tank (see 035).
Bushing
The insulated terminal carrying a conductor through the tank wall.
Tap changer
A mechanism adjusting the turns ratio to regulate voltage.
No-load and load losses
Energy lost in the core at all times, and in the windings under load; efficiency standards limit both.
Amorphous core
A non-crystalline steel core with lower no-load losses.

Materials and manufacturing

CRGO
Cold-rolled grain-oriented electrical steel — the core material, 30–35% of a power transformer's cost and about 90% imported.
CRNGO
Cold-rolled non-grain-oriented steel, used in motors rather than transformers.
Continuously transposed conductor (CTC)
A specialised copper conductor for large windings; another bottleneck input.
Core building
Cutting and stacking CRGO laminations; precision reduces losses.
Vapour-phase drying
Removing moisture from insulation before filling with oil.
Type test
A one-time test proving a design meets its specification; a qualification barrier for new makers.
Routine test
Tests performed on every unit before dispatch.
Test bay
The facility for high-voltage testing; limited bays cap large-unit output.

Commercial and policy

Price-variation clause (PVC)
A contract term adjusting price for changes in steel and copper costs.
Anti-dumping duty
A duty on imports sold below fair value; a CRGO investigation opened on 22 June 2026 (see 025).
DGTR
The Directorate General of Trade Remedies, which conducts anti-dumping investigations.
BIS certification
Mandatory quality certification, required for CRGO imports (see 026).
Enquiry pipeline
Tenders and requests under negotiation — ₹23,000 cr at Transformers & Rectifiers.
Lead time
The time from order to delivery; measured in years for large units in the United States.
Energy efficiency star rating
The loss-based rating for distribution transformers.
Capitalised loss evaluation
Utilities' practice of adding the present value of a transformer's lifetime losses to its bid price, which rewards more efficient designs.
Liquidated damages for delay
Penalties for late delivery, material when lead times stretch (see 039).
Dissolved gas analysis
Testing a transformer's oil for gases that signal internal faults; the basis of condition monitoring.
Refurbishment and retrofit
Rewinding or upgrading an ageing unit instead of replacing it — a service market as the installed base ages.

Cold-rolled grain-oriented electrical steel for the transformer core — 30–35% of a power transformer's cost, about 90% imported, with a 30% supply shortfall in India.

2. Why did Voltamp's margin fall while revenue rose 28%?

Input costs, chiefly steel, rose faster than prices; EBITDA margin fell 238 basis points to 14.77%.

3. How large is Transformers & Rectifiers' order book?
