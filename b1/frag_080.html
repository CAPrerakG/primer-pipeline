Forgings

A forging is metal that has been heated until it glows and then squeezed or hammered into shape — a crankshaft, a front axle beam, a steering knuckle, a pipeline flange, an artillery shell body, a turbine disc. Squeezing the metal lines up its grain along the shape of the part, which is why anything that must not break under repeated load is forged rather than cast. India is now the world's second-largest producer, and its listed forgers are one of the market's best-performing groups in 2026. This primer explains why the same process earns a 31% EBITDA margin at Happy Forgings and 12% at Kalyani Forge, what US tariffs and the American truck cycle mean for the exporters, and how defence and aerospace are changing what the biggest names are.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

The parts that must not break

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Think of modelling clay. If you pour liquid clay into a mould and let it dry, you get a shape, but it can crack along any line. If instead you take a warm lump and squash it hard into shape, the clay packs tightly and bends before it breaks. A forging company does the squashing, but with steel heated to over 1,000 °C and presses as heavy as a building. The parts it makes are the ones in a truck, a tractor or a gun that take the biggest knocks — the crankshaft that turns the engine, the beam that holds the front wheels. Because a broken part could cause a crash, customers test a forger for a long time before buying, and then tend to stay with it for years.

Three facts about the product decide almost everything else in this primer. Weight class sets the press a forger needs and the customers it can serve: a 16,000-tonne press shop making 500 kg crankshafts competes with a handful of companies worldwide, while a 1,000-tonne hammer shop making 2 kg gears competes with hundreds. Machining depth — whether the part leaves as a rough forging or fully finished and ready to bolt in — can double the revenue per kilogram. And safety criticality sets how long a customer takes to approve a new supplier, which is the main barrier to entry in the business.

The chain in one breath

  • Buy a bar of special steel with the right chemistry.
  • Heat it until it glows orange.
  • Squeeze it into shape in a die under thousands of tonnes.
  • Toughen it by heating and cooling it in a controlled way.
  • Machine it to a finish measured in microns.
  • Prove it to the customer, then deliver it every week for the life of the model.
Fund-manager lens

Castings (078) set the Engineering family's first test: who writes the specification, and does the part wear out? Forgings add a second. Almost every forging is made to the customer's drawing, so the forger rarely owns the design — but it does own the press and the approval. The margin therefore follows two things: the weight class (fewer competitors can make a 1,000 kg crankshaft than a 1 kg gear) and the depth of machining (a finished, balanced part is worth far more per kilogram than a rough forging). Happy Forgings earns ₹79 of EBITDA per kilogram on a ₹253 realisation because it has moved up both ladders. Tonnage growth without either is a much lower-quality kind of growth.

Seven of 31 names form the basket: Bharat Forge, CIE Automotive India, Ramkrishna Forgings, MM Forgings, Pradeep Metals, Kalyani Forge and Him Teknoforge, equal-weighted monthly from July 2011. Him Teknoforge is just below the ₹300 cr market-value threshold but is kept for its twenty-year record as a working gear forger.

Three of the six largest names are excluded for being too new. Happy Forgings and Azad Engineering listed in December 2023, Kross in September 2024 and Amic Forging on the SME platform in December 2023. Their strong records since listing are real but cover only one part of one cycle. Balu Forge's usable record starts in October 2020.

Five are misclassified. The levers

  1. Weight class and press tonnage. A heavier part needs a bigger press, and fewer companies own one. Happy Forgings' 8,000-tonne upsetter and its 3,000 kg parts put it in a smaller competitive set than a 5 kg gear maker.
  2. Machining depth. A fully machined, balanced crankshaft sells for a multiple of a rough forging of the same weight. More than half of Kalyani Forge's FY25 revenue came from value-added machining, which is why its margin tripled on flat revenue.
  3. Utilisation. Presses, furnaces and machining lines are fixed costs. Happy's forging lines run at 59%, leaving room to grow without capex; Ramkrishna's heavy capex years left it paying ₹333 cr of depreciation a year.
  4. Steel pass-through. Most contracts pass steel price changes to customers with a lag of one to three months. When steel falls, margins widen for a quarter; when it rises, they narrow. Ramkrishna's Q1 FY27 margin jump came partly from material costs falling to 46% of revenue.
  5. Currency. Exports are priced in dollars and euros; a weaker rupee lifts rupee revenue and margins.
  6. Customer concentration. A few truck makers can be a third or more of revenue; losing a programme at model change is the main operating risk.
The test to apply

Ask of any forger: how many other companies in the world could make this part to this approval, at this weight? If the answer is five, as for a 1,000 kg locomotive crankshaft or an aero-engine disc, the margin is protected. If it is five hundred, as for a small car gear, it is not, and growth will come with thin margins however fast the tonnes rise. The answer explains most of the spread in the table above.

5b · The export engine

American trucks, American tariffs

India's forging industry produces about 2.9 million tonnes a year from 4.8 million tonnes of capacity, with investment of about $3.8 bn and more than 300,000 workers, according to the Association of Indian Forging Industry. Nearly 30% is exported. For the listed leaders the share is higher: Bharat Forge's standalone exports are more than half its revenue, Happy Forgings' 28%, and Ramkrishna and Pradeep are built around export customers. The largest single export market is North America, and within it one product line: Class 8 trucks, the heavy tractors that pull US freight.

The Class 8 cycle

US heavy-truck demand runs in pronounced cycles of three to four years, and in 2026 it is being lifted by an emissions deadline. The EPA's 2027 nitrogen-oxide standard makes new trucks more expensive, so fleets bought early. June 2026 orders jumped 231% to 31,400 units; July was 22,562 (+71%); and August slipped to 16,800 (+31%) as the pre-buy reached the limit of OEM production capacity. Orders for the year to August were 111% ahead of 2025. The pattern after a pre-buy is well known: a strong build year, followed by a gap while the market digests it. For forgers exporting truck axles and crankshafts, this is the swing factor of FY27 and FY28.

The striking fact is how little the tariffs have dented volumes so far. India's auto-component exports to the US were roughly flat at about $6.2 bn in FY26, and the US still takes about 27% of the industry's exports. Bharat Forge's Americas exports grew 15% in Q1 FY27. Three things explain it. Many forgings have no quick alternative supplier, because re-approving a safety-critical part takes one to three years. Competing suppliers in Europe and China face tariffs too. And customers have often absorbed part of the duty. Where the tariffs bite is in margins at US plants: Bharat Forge's US operations ran at a −0.8% EBITDA margin in Q1 FY27, hurt by a press breakdown and by aluminium tariffs on Canadian metal.

How to use this

Separate three things when an exporter reports. Volume follows the Class 8 cycle, which is at a pre-buy peak and has begun to cool. Price follows steel pass-through and currency. Tariffs so far change who absorbs the cost more than how many parts are shipped — but the open overcapacity probe is a live risk, and a tariff aimed squarely at Indian components would change that. The export forgers were up sharply in 2026 on the pre-buy; the question for 2027 is how deep the post-pre-buy gap is.

Two cautions keep this in proportion. First, defence revenue is lumpy: shells and guns are bought in large tranches, and a year without a big export order can halve a segment's revenue. KSSL's margin jump from 6.9% to 17.2% shows how much mix and execution matter. Second, aerospace qualification is slow and small at first. Bharat Forge's aerospace revenue of about ₹400 cr is under 3% of consolidated revenue, and PTC's Airbus agreement starts with development and qualification. These businesses deserve a higher multiple than truck forgings because the approvals are harder to win and harder to lose — but not before they are a meaningful share of profit.

The honest read

The defence and aerospace story is real, and it is best evidenced at Bharat Forge, where it is already profitable at scale. For the smaller names it is mainly an option. The market in 2026 has paid for both — PTC trades at about 260 times earnings and Azad at about 127 — which leaves little room for delays in qualification or orders.

7 · The business calendar

Truck schedules, tractor seasons and the fiscal year

  • April: Indian truck, tractor and component makers set annual schedules and price resets; steel contracts are renegotiated. April has beaten the Nifty in 13 of 15 years.
  • April–June: the tractor pre-monsoon season and the start of the truck year; forgers run at their highest utilisation.
  • July–September: the monsoon slows construction trucks; tractor demand depends on the rains. In the US, the order season for next year's Class 8 trucks opens in the autumn.
  • October–December: festive truck and tractor buying in India; US Class 8 orders for the next year are placed.
  • January–March: fiscal year-end pushes in India, but European plants shut for Christmas and US build schedules reset. March has beaten the Nifty in only 6 of 15 years.

8 · What the stocks do

Seasonality test on TradingView prices

The exceptions, and why they matter

  • April–July failed in 2019 (−26.8), 2018 (−12.6), 2016 (−9.7) and 2012 (−2.3). 2019 was the Indian truck collapse; 2016 and 2018 were US truck downturns and a small-cap unwind. Its best were 2014 (+61.9) and 2021 (+48.3), both years of a truck recovery meeting a small-cap rally.
  • April itself failed only in 2019 (−5.7) and 2025 (−2.7). Its best were 2022 (+14.6) and 2024 (+12.9).
  • January–March was worst in 2013 (−20.4), 2025 (−18.3) and 2020 (−17.2), and best in 2014 (+28.2), 2017 (+17.0) and 2026 (+15.5). 2026 is the exception that matters now: the first quarter worked because US Class 8 pre-buy orders and Indian truck demand arrived together.
How to use this

This is the Engineering family's April effect again, a little weaker than castings (078, 14 of 15) and with the same mechanism: annual schedules and price resets in the Indian fiscal year. Two cautions. Four of the seven are small caps, so part of the April edge is the market's own small-cap April. And the basket is dominated by truck exposure, so a truck downturn overrides the calendar entirely — as in 2019. Use the calendar to time decisions already made on the truck cycle, not the other way round.

9 · What moves margins

Nine drivers

  1. US Class 8 truck build rates. The largest export driver; in a pre-buy peak in 2026.
  2. Indian medium and heavy truck sales. Retail truck sales rose 15% in April 2026 and Ashok Leyland's domestic heavy trucks 44% in June.
  3. Tractors and farm equipment. A third of Happy Forgings' revenue; driven by the monsoon.
  4. Passenger vehicles. India's car sales rose 25.9% in Q1 FY27 on lower GST; a growing share for Happy, Ramkrishna and CIE.
  5. Steel prices. Passed through with a lag; a falling-steel quarter widens margins.
  6. Energy costs. Decisive for European plants.
  7. Currency. A weaker rupee lifts export revenue and margins.
  8. Defence orders. Lumpy, high-value and increasingly large for Bharat Forge and Balu.
  9. Interest and depreciation. For Ramkrishna, the difference between ₹627 cr of operating profit and ₹72 cr of net profit.

The anatomy of the cycles

  1. The 2014 re-rating (2012–2015): a truck recovery and the election rally re-priced Indian engineering; the basket rose 232% in 2014, with a median member up 188%.
  2. The US truck dip and the small-cap boom (2016–2017): −15% in 2016, then +96% in 2017.
  3. The double downturn (2018–2019): the small-cap unwind, then India's truck collapse and the US Class 8 peak. The basket fell 39% in 2019 while the Nifty rose 12%.
  4. Recovery and exports (2020–2023): four winning years, led by Ramkrishna's export growth.
  5. Defence and the pre-buy (2024–2026): a pause in 2025, when Ramkrishna's accounting problem and weak Europe dragged, then 2026's broad rally on Indian trucks, the EPA 2027 pre-buy and defence orders.

Patterns that repeat

  • The basket is a truck-cycle basket. Its worst years — 2013, 2016, 2019 — are the years Indian or US truck demand fell.
  • Bharat Forge is the low-beta member. It fell only 5% in 2019 and rose in 2013 and 2025 when the rest fell, because defence and industrial parts offset trucks.
  • Ramkrishna is the high-beta one. +285% (2014), +204% (2017), +176% (2023), but −46% (2016), −38% (2018) and −41% (2025). Leverage amplifies both directions.
  • Small forgers fall hardest in downturns. Him Teknoforge fell 72% in 2019 and Kalyani Forge 46%; utilisation drops, and fixed costs do not.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • From captive to outsourced forging (1990s–2000s): vehicle makers closed in-house forge shops and bought from specialists.
  • From Europe and the US to India and China (2000s–2020s): cost, and engineering talent that could machine to tight tolerances.
  • From China to India for Western customers (2018–2026): tariffs and "China plus one" sourcing.
  • From automotive to defence and aerospace (2020s): Indian forgers moving into higher-barrier work.

Bharat Forge's history is the clearest case study of the second shift. It bought forging plants in Germany and Sweden in the 2000s to get close to European customers and technology. Two decades later the German plants are a drag — Q1 FY27 European EBITDA was 2.8% of revenue with a pre-tax loss — and the company is restructuring the largest of them, while its Indian plants earn 26%. The capability moved to India; the European plants kept the energy costs.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Electric vehicles. An electric car has no crankshaft, connecting rods or most gearbox parts. It still needs knuckles, control arms, axle shafts and motor shafts. Forgers exposed to engine parts in passenger cars face the largest loss; heavy trucks and tractors are much further from electrification.
  2. Lightweighting. Aluminium forgings replacing steel in suspension parts — the product of Bharat Forge's US aluminium plant.
  3. Near-net-shape and warm forging. Parts forged closer to final shape need less machining and less steel.
  4. Titanium and superalloy forging. For aero engines, airframes and turbines; the high-barrier end of the business.
  5. Automation and simulation. Robotic press lines and die-design software reduce scrap and labour.
  6. Larger presses. 8,000–16,000-tonne presses and upsetters for heavy industrial and defence parts.

What is priced in

For Bharat Forge, the defence story more than the truck cycle. At ₹1,962 the stock is about 97 times trailing consolidated earnings on Screener — a figure distorted by the one-off German charge, but high on any measure. The market is paying for the ₹11,196 cr defence order book, KSSL's margin reaching its 22–23% target and aerospace doubling. The truck business is at a pre-buy peak in the US and strong in India. The risk is the order of events: if US truck volumes fall in 2027 before defence revenue grows enough to offset them, reported earnings could disappoint for a year even if the strategy is right.

For Happy Forgings, a clean compounder at a full price. Volume +23%, margin above 30% for four quarters, 59% utilisation and a ₹950 cr order book. At about 60 times earnings, the price assumes the high-teens volume guidance is exceeded for years.

For Ramkrishna and CIE, a discount with reasons. Ramkrishna's margin recovery to 18% is real, but net profit is still thin after interest and depreciation, and the 2025 accounting failure will weigh on its multiple until several clean years pass. CIE, at about 16 times earnings, is the cheapest large name because its European half is growing only in rupee terms.

The honest read

The forgings basket has had its best relative year since 2014, and the reasons are good ones: Indian trucks and cars strong at once, a US pre-buy, and defence orders turning into revenue. But two of those three are cyclical peaks. Treat the section as a truck-cycle group with a growing defence and aerospace floor, not the reverse. The names whose margins rest on weight class and machining depth — Happy Forgings, Bharat Forge's Indian plants — should hold up best when the US cycle turns. The names whose 2026 gains rest on volume alone, or on one-off gains such as MM Forgings' land sale, have the most to give back.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What tonnage did you ship, at what realisation and EBITDA per kilogram?
  2. What is utilisation by press line, and how much volume can you add without capex?
  3. What share of revenue is fully machined, and at what margin?
  4. What share of exports goes to US Class 8 trucks, and what do customers' schedules show after the EPA 2027 pre-buy?
  5. Who pays the US tariffs on your parts — you or the customer — and what happens if the overcapacity probe ends in a new tariff?
  6. What is the lag in steel pass-through, and how much of this quarter's margin came from steel timing?
  7. How many new part approvals did you win this year, and how long does a typical approval take?

  9. What are interest and depreciation as a share of EBITDA, and when does new capacity reach full utilisation?

19 · Glossary

Every term you'll meet

Process

Forging
Shaping hot or cold metal by pressing or hammering it, which aligns its grain and makes it stronger than a casting of the same shape.
Closed-die forging
Pressing a billet between two shaped dies that enclose the part; used for most automotive forgings.
Open-die forging
Shaping metal between flat or simple dies without enclosing it; used for large shafts and blocks.
Ring rolling
Forming seamless rings by rolling a pierced blank; used for bearings, flanges and wind-tower parts.
Hot, warm and cold forging
Forging above, around or below the metal's recrystallisation temperature; colder forging gives closer tolerances on smaller parts.
Upsetting
Increasing a bar's diameter by compressing its length; an upsetter is the machine.
Press tonnage
The force a forging press can apply; it sets the largest part a plant can make.
Hammer
A forging machine that shapes metal by repeated blows rather than steady pressure.
Billet
A cut length of steel bar ready for heating and forging.
Die
The hardened steel tool that gives a forging its shape; it wears out and must be replaced.
Flash
Excess metal squeezed out between the dies, trimmed off after forging.
Heat treatment
Controlled heating and cooling — quenching and tempering — to give a forging its specified strength.
Near-net shape
Forging a part close to its final dimensions so less machining is needed.
Machining
Turning, milling and grinding a forging to final size.
Grain flow
The direction of the metal's internal structure, which forging aligns with the part's shape.

Products and materials

Crankshaft
The engine shaft that turns the pistons' reciprocating motion into rotation.
Front axle beam
The forged beam that carries the front of a truck.
Steering knuckle
The part that holds the wheel hub and pivots for steering.
Connecting rod
Links a piston to the crankshaft.
Flange
A ring used to bolt pipes and valves together.
SBQ steel
Special bar quality steel — alloy bars with tight chemistry for forging.
Micro-alloy steel
Steel with small additions of vanadium or niobium that reach strength without separate heat treatment.
Superalloy
A nickel- or cobalt-based alloy that keeps its strength at very high temperatures; used in turbines.
Titanium alloy
A light, strong, corrosion-resistant metal used in aircraft structures and engines.
Airfoil
A blade or vane in a turbine or compressor.
Shell body
The forged steel casing of an artillery round.

Business and markets

Class 8 truck
The heaviest US truck class, above 33,000 lb gross weight; the main export market for Indian truck forgings.
Pre-buy
Customers buying early to avoid a coming price rise, such as new emission rules; followed by a demand gap.
EPA 2027
The US Environmental Protection Agency's 2027 nitrogen-oxide standard for heavy trucks.
M&HCV
Medium and heavy commercial vehicles — India's truck and bus market.
OEM
Original equipment manufacturer — the vehicle or engine maker.
Tier-one supplier
A supplier selling directly to an OEM, often a systems maker that buys forgings.
Build to print
Making a part to the customer's drawing, without owning its design.
PPAP
Production part approval process — the automotive procedure for approving a new part and supplier.
IATF 16949
The automotive quality-management standard.
AS9100
The aerospace quality-management standard.
Realisation per kilogram
Revenue per kilogram shipped; rises with machining depth and mix.
EBITDA per kilogram
Operating profit per kilogram shipped; Happy Forgings' ₹79 is the benchmark here.
Utilisation
Output as a share of capacity; the driver of operating leverage.
Steel pass-through
Contract terms that move steel price changes to the customer, usually with a lag.
Section 232
The US law used to impose tariffs on grounds of national security, including on autos and parts.
Exceptional item
A one-off gain or charge reported separately, such as Bharat Forge's ₹358 cr restructuring provision.
FCCB
Foreign-currency convertible bond — the instrument Balu Forge plans for its $60 m raise.
QIP
Qualified institutional placement — a share sale to institutions, one route for Bharat Forge's raise.
SME platform
The exchanges' small and medium enterprise board, with lighter listing rules.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. Why are safety-critical parts forged rather than cast?

Forging aligns the metal's grain with the part's shape, making it tougher and more resistant to fatigue.

2. Why does Happy Forgings earn a 31% margin while Kalyani Forge earns 16%?

Weight class and machining depth: heavy, fully machined crankshafts and industrial parts face far fewer competitors than small automotive forgings.

3. Why did Bharat Forge report a loss in Q1 FY27?

A ₹358 cr exceptional restructuring provision for its German subsidiary; the underlying business grew 19%.

4. What is a pre-buy, and why does it matter now?

Fleets buying early to avoid costlier EPA 2027 trucks; it lifted 2026 orders but is followed by a gap.

5. How have US tariffs affected Indian forging exports so far?

Volumes have held up — exports to the US were flat at about $6.2 bn in FY26 — but margins at US plants have suffered.

6. What went wrong at Ramkrishna Forgings in 2025?

Production was overstated and rejections under-reported; the ₹220.52 cr discrepancy forced a restatement and cut net worth by ₹202.06 cr.

7. Why is MM Forgings' Q1 FY27 profit not a guide to its earnings?

It includes a ₹56.25 cr post-tax gain on selling land.

8. What is the strongest calendar signal for this basket?

April, which has beaten the Nifty in 13 of 15 years.

9. Which forgings does an electric car still need?

Steering knuckles, control arms, axle shafts and motor shafts — but not crankshafts, connecting rods or most gearbox parts.

10. Why is PTC Industries outside the forgings basket?

It is a castings and titanium-materials company; it belongs with castings (078).
