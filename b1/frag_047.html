Renewable Energy EPC

EPC stands for engineering, procurement and construction: the contractors who design a solar or storage project, buy the modules, inverters, cables and structures, and build it on the ground until it produces power. India is adding solar faster than almost any country, so these contractors have record order books. What they do not have is reliable margins — and this section contains both one of the market's best-run small contractors and one of its worst corporate-governance failures.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Big orders, thin margins, and everything depends on execution

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Imagine someone wants a huge solar farm but does not know how to build one. They hire a builder, who plans the layout, buys thousands of solar panels and all the wires and frames, hires workers, and builds the whole thing in the desert. That builder is an EPC company. It gets paid a fixed price for the whole job. If panels get more expensive, the rain comes early, or the power line to the farm isn't ready, the builder's profit can disappear — because the price was fixed before the work started. Being an EPC company is like being a wedding caterer: lots of big orders, but you only make money if everything goes to plan.

Say it to a client in one minute

Solar EPC is a growth market with contractor economics: order books say little until they turn into profitable revenue.

  • Orders are at records: Sterling and Wilson ₹13,024 cr, Oriana about ₹7,000 cr, Waaree Renewable Technologies above ₹5,300 cr, Advait's nearly doubled.
  • Execution is the problem: Sterling and Wilson's revenue fell 10%; Oriana missed its FY26 targets on commodity costs, currency, delays and a deferred asset sale.
  • The grid is a bottleneck: 8,133 GWh of solar curtailed in April–June; about a third of new renewable capacity runs on temporary grid access with heavy daytime curtailment.
  • The best operator earns 15–19%: Waaree RTL's EBITDA margin was 18.8% with a stated 15% floor; most contractors earn much less.
  • Governance matters more than usual: Gensol collapsed after SEBI found that loans meant for electric vehicles had been diverted; IREDA has filed for its insolvency.

Analogy

An EPC contractor is a general contractor for a building. The owner decides what to build and pays for it; the architect-and-builder designs it, buys the bricks, cement and fittings, and hands over the keys. The builder's margin is the difference between the fixed contract price and what everything actually costs — which is why a rise in the price of steel, or a monsoon that floods the site, goes straight to the builder's bottom line.

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • The order: a power company wins permission to sell solar electricity at a fixed price.
  • The plot and the plug: it finds land and asks for a connection to the big power lines.
  • The plan and the shopping: the builder designs the farm and buys all the parts.
  • The build: workers put up frames, panels, cables and a small substation.
  • The upkeep: panels are cleaned and fixed for many years.
  • The income: the power company sells the electricity.
Fund-manager lens

The critical asset in an EPC business is not the order book; it is the ability to convert orders into cash at the bid margin. That requires procurement skill (buying modules and cables before prices move), a strong balance sheet (to fund working capital and give bank guarantees), and customers who pay. The most reliable margins in this section belong to Waaree Renewable Technologies, which buys modules from its parent at a time when domestic-content modules are scarce (see 045) — an advantage that is also a related-party question. For every other contractor, the right way to value the business is on through-cycle margins and cash conversion, not on the headline order book.

Why margins are volatile

  1. Fixed-price contracts with moving inputs: module, copper and steel prices all moved sharply in 2025–26.
  2. Delays cost money: late land, late grid connection or monsoon disruption extend overheads and can trigger liquidated damages (see 039).
  3. Working capital: contractors fund procurement before milestone payments arrive; slow-paying customers strain cash.
  4. Bank guarantees: each order ties up guarantee limits, capping how fast a small contractor can grow.
  5. Revenue recognition: percentage-of-completion accounting can book profit before cash, and reverse it later.
Analogy

A fixed-price EPC contract is like agreeing today to cater a wedding in six months for a set fee. If vegetable prices double, the guest list changes, or the venue is not ready on time, the caterer absorbs the cost. The best caterers buy ahead, write escalation clauses, and choose clients who pay.

7 · The business calendar

A year shaped by the monsoon and the March rush

  • April–June: new orders and the best construction weather before the monsoon.
  • July–September: monsoon slows site work, especially in the west and south; execution dips.
  • October–December: construction resumes at full pace.
  • January–March: the commissioning rush before the financial year ends; the heaviest billing quarter.
  • Tender calendar: SECI, NTPC, NHPC, SJVN and state auctions through the year.

8 · What the stocks do

Seasonality test on TradingView prices

The exceptions, and why

  • April–July has no failures in seven years; the smallest gains were in 2026 (+11.2) and 2021 (+15.3), the largest in 2024 (+85.9) and 2020 (+45.1). Even 2025, the year of the Gensol collapse, delivered +22.0.
  • January–March succeeded only in 2024 (+61.5), when solar and storage ordering was at its most exuberant. It failed badly in 2025 (−36.2) and 2020 (−29.9).
  • October–December worked only 2 of 7 times; 2019 (−34.7) and 2022 (−23.2) were the worst, both driven by Sterling and Wilson — its promoter's debt troubles in 2019 and legacy-project losses in 2022.
How to use this

The April pattern is consistent but based on very few years and a basket dominated by small caps and recent listings. Treat it as a tendency — new orders and pre-monsoon execution in the first quarter of the financial year — rather than a rule, and remember that single events (a fraud, a loss-making quarter) move this section far more than the calendar does.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Eight drivers

  1. Auction volumes: utility solar, hybrid and storage tenders set the order pipeline.
  2. Transmission readiness: no grid, no commissioning; curtailment also discourages new orders.
  3. Module and cell prices: the largest cost item (see 045).
  4. Storage tenders: 130+ GWh tendered in 2025 (see 046); a new line of EPC work.
  5. C&I demand and open access: factories and data centres buying green power.
  6. Rooftop and farm schemes: PM Surya Ghar and PM-KUSUM for small contractors.
  7. Balance sheets and guarantees: the limit on growth.
  8. Customer payments: discom and developer payment discipline.

The longer story

  1. The first solar wave (2014–2019): auction-led growth with falling tariffs; EPC margins were squeezed and Sterling and Wilson listed in 2019 at the top of its first cycle.
  2. Legacy losses (2019–2022): Sterling and Wilson's international projects lost money and its original promoter was in financial distress; Reliance took control in 2022.
  3. The SME boom (2023–2024): a wave of small solar EPC listings rose several-fold on record ordering.
  4. The reckoning (2025): Gensol's collapse, Sterling and Wilson's loss, and falls of 39–53% at several contractors.
  5. Record orders, slow execution (2026): order books at highs, revenue held back by grid connectivity and input costs.

Patterns that repeat

  • Order books peak before margins do — and sometimes instead of them.
  • Listings cluster at the top of ordering cycles: Sterling and Wilson in 2019; the SME wave in 2023–25.
  • Governance failures are concentrated in fast-growing, promoter-dominated small caps.

11 · The global picture

A local business with global competitors at the top end

Solar EPC is mostly a local business: projects need local labour, permits and site knowledge. At the utility scale, a few firms compete internationally — Chinese state-owned engineering groups, Spanish and other European contractors, and Sterling and Wilson, which has built projects across the Middle East, Africa and Australia. Indian contractors' international work has been profitable when priced carefully and very costly when not; Sterling and Wilson's losses between 2019 and 2022 came largely from overseas contracts. Its new Egyptian joint venture is a return to that market with a partner sharing the risk.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • From developer-owned EPC to independent contractors and back: large developers increasingly build in-house, leaving independent EPC firms to compete for third-party and public-sector work.
  • From solar-only to solar plus storage plus transmission: contractors are widening scope to find margin (Waaree RTL, Bondada, Advait).
  • From utility to C&I and rooftop: smaller jobs, better margins, more credit risk.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Solar plus storage as standard: NTPC's 250 MW plus 200 MWh award to Bondada is typical of new tenders.
  2. Trackers and bifacial modules: more energy per acre (see 038, 045).
  3. Robotic cleaning and drones: lower O&M cost in dusty regions.
  4. Digital monitoring: performance analytics and predictive maintenance.
  5. Grid-forming inverters: required as solar share rises.
  6. Agrivoltaics and floating solar: new sites where land is scarce.

The honest read

Demand for renewable construction is not the question; conversion is. Most contractors in this section are small, capital-constrained and exposed to fixed-price risk at a time when their biggest inputs are rising and grid connections are late. The market has marked most of them down sharply, which is rational until margins and cash flow show that record orders will translate into profits. The exception is the contractor with a secure, captive supply of the scarcest input (Waaree RTL), whose margin is the highest here — though investors should watch related-party pricing with its parent. Gensol is a reminder that in fast-growing small caps, governance risk can outweigh every industry tailwind.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What share of the order book is fixed-price, and how are module and copper price changes handled?
  2. How much of the order book has land and grid connectivity in place?
  3. What is the expected revenue conversion over the next four quarters?
  4. What is operating cash flow, and why does it differ from profit?
  5. What are bank guarantee limits and utilisation?
  6. What share of procurement is from related parties, and on what terms?
  7. What liquidated damages or claims are outstanding?
  8. How are customer payments tracking, by customer type?

Red flags

  • Profits without operating cash flow for several periods.
  • Rapidly rising contract assets and receivables.
  • Large related-party flows or loans to promoter entities.
  • Frequent changes of auditor or senior finance staff.

Solar project terms

MWp and MWac
A plant's DC module capacity and its AC grid-export capacity.
DC:AC ratio
Module capacity relative to inverter capacity; above 1 to use inverters fully.
String and central inverters
Many small inverters versus a few large ones.

The steel frames that hold panels at the right angle.

Actual output against theoretical output, a measure of plant quality.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. What does an EPC contractor do?

Designs a project, buys all the equipment and builds it, usually for a fixed price.

2. Why can a record order book coexist with falling revenue?

Orders turn into revenue only as work is done; land, grid-connection and input delays slow execution — Sterling and Wilson's revenue fell 10% with a record ₹13,024 cr order book.

3. Why does Waaree RTL earn higher margins than most peers?

Secure supply of domestic-content modules from its parent at a time when they are scarce, plus selective bidding; its EBITDA margin was 18.8%.

4. What happened at Gensol?

SEBI found diversion of loans taken for electric vehicles, barred the company and promoters, and IREDA filed for insolvency over a ₹510 cr default.

5. How much solar was curtailed in April–June 2026?

8,133 GWh, because transmission could not carry it.

6. What is T-GNA, and why does it matter?

Temporary grid access, curtailed first; about a third of new renewable capacity relied on it in May 2026.

7. Which is the strongest seasonal window?

April–July: the basket beat the Nifty in all 7 years, median +25.2 points.

8. Why was Oriana's price history adjusted?

It split its shares 1:5 on 18 September 2026 and the price series had not yet been adjusted.

9. What is the largest single cost in a utility solar project?

Modules, at about half of cost.

10. What is the most useful check on an EPC company's profits?

Operating cash flow and the trend in receivables and contract assets.
