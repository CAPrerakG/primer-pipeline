1 · The industry in 60 seconds

Oil in, plastic granules out

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Crude oil and natural gas are made of long chains of carbon. A giant machine called a cracker breaks them into small building blocks, like Lego bricks, with names like ethylene and propylene. Another plant clicks thousands of bricks together into long chains; that is plastic. It comes out as small beads, which other factories melt and shape into buckets, pipes, milk pouches and car bumpers. Whoever gets the bricks cheapest earns the most. Right now China has built so many brick factories that everyone's profit got squeezed, and in 2026 a war in the Gulf made the bricks suddenly scarce and expensive.

A kitchen analogy that holds up all the way down the chain:

Say it to a client in one minute

Petrochemicals are a global commodity game. India consumes far more polyethylene, polypropylene and PVC than it makes, so domestic prices track the landed import price. Margins are spreads over feedstock, and those spreads are set by global capacity cycles. China built roughly 70% of the world's new ethylene capacity in 2020–25, which pushed spreads to multi-decade lows in 2023–25. The Iran war that closed Hormuz from March 2026 sent naphtha and polymer prices to four-year highs, and Indian niche producers posted record quarters (Supreme Petrochem's Q1 FY27 profit nearly tripled) even as their volumes fell. In November 2025 India removed its own protection: 14 quality-control orders were withdrawn and the PVC anti-dumping duty was dropped. The listed pure plays in your watchlist are niche polymer makers (polystyrene, ABS, propylene oxide, PIB, oxo alcohols). The big crackers sit inside Reliance, IOC, GAIL, HPCL's new Barmer complex and unlisted companies.

2 · What the products are

Two families of building blocks, four families of plastics

Olefins (from crackers)

  • Ethylene (C2) → polyethylene, PVC (via EDC/VCM), MEG (polyester), styrene.
  • Propylene (C3) → polypropylene, propylene oxide (polyols), acrylonitrile (ABS, acrylic fibre), oxo alcohols.
  • Butadiene and C4 streams → synthetic rubber, ABS, polyisobutylene (PIB).

Aromatics (from reformers)

  • Benzene → styrene, cumene/phenol, LAB (detergents).
  • Toluene → solvents, TDI (polyurethane foam).
  • Paraxylene → PTA → polyester fibre and PET bottles.

Plastics by family

  • Commodity polymers: polyethylene (LLDPE and LDPE for film, HDPE for pipes and containers), polypropylene (moulded goods, woven sacks, fibre), PVC (pipes, profiles, cables), polystyrene (appliance parts, packaging, EPS thermocol).
  • Engineering plastics: ABS and SAN (appliance housings, car interiors), polycarbonate, nylon, PBT; tougher and higher-priced.
  • Polyurethane chain: polyols plus MDI/TDI → foams for mattresses and car seats, and coatings.
  • Specialty derivatives: PIB (fuel and lubricant additives), oxo alcohols (plasticisers, solvents), glycol ethers and ethoxylates (brake fluid, surfactants).

End uses in India: packaging is the largest, then construction and pipes (PVC, HDPE), agriculture (irrigation pipes, mulch film), automobiles, appliances and electronics, and textiles (polyester, via the PTA/MEG chain).

Everyday examples

Your milk pouch is LLDPE; the water tank on the roof is HDPE; the chair on the verandah is polypropylene; the drainpipe is PVC; the thermocol in a TV box is EPS (Supreme Petrochem); the refrigerator's inner lining and the TV's back cover are HIPS and ABS (Supreme, Styrenix, Bhansali); the foam in your mattress started as propylene oxide (Manali); the additive keeping your car's fuel injectors clean is PIB (Kothari).

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Feedstock: like buying flour sacks; whoever buys cheapest (US and Gulf gas users) starts ahead.
  • Cracker: a giant oven the size of a town that costs more than a big dam; only a few companies can afford one, and it must run all day, every day, to pay for itself.
  • Monomers: the individual Lego bricks. Styrene is a brick India doesn't make at all, so Indian polystyrene makers must import every brick.
  • Polymerisation: clicking bricks into long chains. Different chain lengths make different "grades", like thin and thick noodles from the same dough.
  • Converters: the shops that melt the beads and mould them into things you can buy.

Fund-manager lens

Profit sits where you are advantaged on feedstock (gas-based or integrated) or protected by a niche (a product few import, or a customer-qualified specialty grade). A non-integrated polymer maker buying imported monomer earns a thin conversion spread. Its edge is plant efficiency, product mix and being the only local supplier with fast delivery. And a standalone plant that depends on one refinery's propylene (Andhra) can be shut by a supply cut it has no control over.

5 · How money is made

Spreads, import parity and inventory

Import parity pricing. India is short of polyethylene, polypropylene, PVC and styrene, so domestic producers price off the landed cost of imports: the international price (CFR India) × USD/INR + customs duty + port and freight costs. Anything that raises landed cost (duties, a weak rupee, freight, a supply shock) raises domestic prices and margins. Anything that lowers it, such as Chinese dumping or duties being removed, compresses them. In 2026 all the "raise" levers fired at once: war freight, a rupee at ~₹96 per dollar (TradingView, 18 September) and Gulf supply cuts.

Three mechanics every analyst must know

  1. Spread, not price. Revenue can fall 20% while EBITDA rises, if feedstock falls faster than product. Always ask for the spread per tonne.
  2. Inventory gains and losses. Producers hold 30–60 days of monomer. When prices rise, cheap old stock flatters margins; when they fall, margins get hit. Strip these out to see the underlying business. Q1 FY27's record margins with falling volumes are a textbook inventory-plus-scarcity quarter.
  3. Grade and mix. Commodity grades earn import parity. Specialty grades (high-impact polystyrene, auto-grade ABS, customer-approved polyols) earn premiums and stickier customers.
Analogy

A non-integrated polymer maker is like a baker who buys flour at the market price every week and sells bread at whatever the bakery across the border charges. If the border bakery raises prices (imports get dearer), our baker earns more. If a flood of cheap bread arrives, he earns less, however good his oven.

6 · The business calendar

Demand is steady, prices are not

  • Monsoon (June–September) slows construction, so PVC and pipe-grade demand dips. Pre-monsoon stocking (April–May) and post-monsoon construction (October–March) are the strong periods.
  • Festive season (September–November) lifts appliance and electronics demand: polystyrene, ABS and polypropylene for housings.
  • Summer (March–May) is peak season for air conditioners and refrigerators, which use HIPS, ABS and EPS packaging.
  • Chinese New Year (January–February) slows Asian trade and usually softens prices after the holiday restocking.
  • Price revisions. Indian producers (Reliance, IOC, Supreme Petrochem) announce prices every fortnight or month. Buyers destock ahead of expected cuts and stock up ahead of expected hikes, which amplifies swings. Reliance announced PE increases effective 16 September 2026.
  • Turnarounds. Planned cracker shutdowns (usually spring or autumn in Asia) tighten supply for weeks.
  • Hurricane season (June–November) can shut US Gulf capacity, as the February 2021 Texas freeze did, briefly lifting global prices.

The exceptions, and why

  • April misses (2019: −2.4 pts; 2025: −1.2 pts). 2019 was the trade-war squeeze in a small-cap bear market; April 2025 came as Chinese capacity was peaking and polymer prices were falling. Every other April since 2012 beat the Nifty.
  • Biggest Aprils (2021: +22.1; 2016: +19.7; 2026: +17.8). 2021 was peak-spread euphoria; 2016 was the rebound after crude's $27 bottom; 2026 came right after the Hormuz closure lifted polymer prices.
  • April–July failures (2018: −8.7; 2019: −14.7; 2022: −4.8). 2018: crude rising while small caps sold off. 2019: trade war. 2022: the post-Ukraine feedstock spike and Chinese lockdowns.
  • Biggest April–July runs (2021: +62.7; 2024: +45.7). 2021: record spreads. 2024: the styrenics rally (Styrenix, Bhansali).
  • February is the worst month (beat in 3 of 15). The deepest Februaries were market shocks, not industry ones: 2020 (−13.4, COVID), 2016 (−10.0, the global crude and small-cap sell-off) and 2022 (−8.2, the Russian invasion on 24 February).

8 · What moves demand and margins

Capacity waves beat demand every time

Margins (the big driver)

  • Global capacity additions. China added ~26 mt of ethylene capacity between 2021 and 2025. Its 2025 surplus over local demand was forecast at a record 11.5 mt for ethylene and 20.3 mt for propylene.
  • Feedstock cost: crude, naphtha cracks, ethane. Brent averaged $68 in 2025 and $88 so far in 2026 (TradingView).
  • Supply shocks: war (the 2026 Hormuz crisis; Maersk suspended bookings from most Gulf ports), plant outages, hurricanes.
  • Trade protection: duties, anti-dumping measures, quality-control orders.
  • Rupee: a weaker rupee lifts import parity; USD/INR averaged ₹87 in 2025 and ~₹94 in 2026.
  • Feedstock security: standalone derivative plants depend on one refinery's propylene or C4 (Andhra's 2026 shutdown).

Demand

  • Packaging and FMCG are the steady base.
  • Construction and infrastructure drive PVC and HDPE pipes; Jal Jeevan Mission water pipelines were a big boost in 2020–24.
  • Autos and appliances drive ABS, polypropylene compounds and polystyrene; appliance production-linked incentives (PLI) pull more of this demand onshore.
  • Agriculture drives irrigation pipes and films.
  • Exports: Supreme Petrochem suspended exports in Q1 FY27 as domestic prices beat export parity.
  • India's plastics demand grows far faster than GDP, but that growth mostly benefits converters, not producers facing import parity.

The anatomy of a petrochemical cycle

  1. Shortage: an outage, war or demand surge lifts product prices faster than feedstock (2015's crude crash; the 2021 Texas freeze; 2026 Hormuz). Spreads widen, niche producers post record quarters.
  2. Capacity response: fat margins trigger new crackers, above all in China. They take 3–4 years to build.
  3. Glut: the new plants arrive together, operating rates fall below ~85% and spreads collapse to cash cost (2023–25).
  4. Cure: high-cost plants close (Japan and Korea from 2024), demand catches up, and the next shock starts the cycle again.

Patterns that repeat

  • Crude down is often good for these stocks. The best years (2015, 2020) came when crude collapsed and spreads widened. The worst (2012, 2018, 2022) had rising crude.
  • Small-cap cycles amplify everything. The basket beat the Nifty by 111 points in 2017 and lagged by 38 in 2019, much more than spreads alone would explain.
  • Record quarters come with falling volumes. Q1 FY27 (Supreme, Styrenix) and 2021 show that the most profitable quarters are scarcity quarters, which don't last.
  • Standalone plants are fragile. Andhra Petro (a single product, a single feedstock supplier) fell 77% from its 2021 peak and is now shut.

11 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  • Crude-to-chemicals: new refineries convert more of each barrel into petrochemicals rather than fuels, as fuel demand plateaus with EVs. HPCL's Barmer complex is India's newest example.
  • PDH and ethane imports: making propylene and ethylene from cheaper gas liquids instead of naphtha (Reliance imports US ethane).
  • Recycling mandates: India's plastic waste rules impose extended producer responsibility (EPR) on brand owners and rising minimum recycled content in rigid packaging. Recycled PET and HDPE compete with virgin polymer.
  • Single-use plastic ban (identified items since July 2022) and bio-based or compostable polymers (PLA) for niche uses.
  • Better catalysts and specialty grades: metallocene PE, specialty ABS/ASA for cars and appliances, XPS insulation boards, move margin from volume to performance (Supreme's XPS and compounding lines, Bhansali's AES/ASA).
  • Electrification and lightweighting: EVs use more engineering plastics per car (battery housings, connectors), a tailwind for ABS and compounds.

15 · Adjacent industries and proxies

Who wins when polymers get cheap, or dear

  • Converters gain when polymers are cheap and plentiful: Plastic Pipes (Supreme Industries, Astral, Finolex), Flexible Packaging (Uflex, Cosmo First), Rigid Plastic Packaging, Industrial Plastics. They lose on inventory when prices fall fast.
  • Upstream integrated: Reliance (O2C segment), GAIL (petrochemical segment), IOC, HPCL (Barmer ramp-up).
  • Sister chains: Chlor-Alkali (chlorine for PVC), Synthetic Fibres (polyester via PTA and MEG), Synthetic Rubber (butadiene), Paints (monomers and solvents), Alcohol & Solvent Chemicals (formaldehyde resins like ARCL's).
  • End demand: consumer appliances (Voltas, Blue Star, Amber: big ABS and HIPS users), autos (PP compounds), FMCG packaging, infrastructure.
  • Shipping and tank terminals: Kandla and Mundra chemical terminals handle styrene and polymer imports.

16 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What is your spread per tonne over monomer this quarter against the five-year average?
  2. How much of the EBITDA change was inventory gain or loss?
  3. Where do you source styrene, AN, butadiene and propylene; what are the contract terms; how many days of stock do you hold?
  4. What share of volume is specialty or customer-approved grades?
  5. What is utilisation, and what is the payback on new capacity at today's spread?
  6. What is the import landed price against your list price?
  7. Why did volumes fall while margins rose, and when do exports resume?

Red flags

  • Margin expansion that is entirely inventory gains.
  • Capex justified by expected duties.
  • A single large customer, a single product or a single feedstock supplier.
  • Rising receivables in a falling-price market.
  • Plants idle for months (Andhra) or recurring technical problems (Supreme's ABS).

"India's low per-capita plastics use means producers will boom."Demand growth is real, but producers price at import parity, and China's surplus caps that parity. Growth accrues mostly to converters.
"Anti-dumping duties will protect margins."The PVC duty was dropped and the quality-control orders withdrawn in November 2025. Policy now prioritises downstream users.
"Oil up is bad, oil down is good."What matters is spreads. A supply shock (2026) can lift product prices faster than feedstock at first; a crude crash (2014–15) widened spreads.
"Q1 FY27 margins show a new era."Styrenix's own management called it an "abnormal snapshot"; volumes fell 25%+ at both Supreme and Styrenix. Scarcity margins historically fade within a few quarters.
"The downcycle is ending."China's build-out slows after 2026, but analysts see normal profitability only in the early 2030s; closures in Japan and Korea are part of the cure.
"New domestic crackers help Indian polymer makers."They help converters (cheaper, closer supply) but pressure non-integrated producers, as prices move from import parity toward export parity.

Economics and trade

Spread
Product price minus feedstock or monomer cost per tonne.
Crack spread
The margin of a product (e.g. naphtha) over crude.
Integrated margin
The combined cracker-plus-polymer margin earned by integrated producers.
Import parity
Domestic price set equal to landed import cost.
Export parity
The lower price a producer must accept once it has surplus to export.
Deemed export
Domestic sales to export-oriented units, priced like exports.
CFR / FOB
Price including freight to the destination / at the loading port.
Landed cost
CFR × exchange rate + duty + port and inland charges.
Inventory gain / loss
The margin effect of holding stock bought at a different price.
Turnaround
A planned maintenance shutdown.
Force majeure
A supplier suspending contracts after an uncontrollable event.
Operating rate
Utilisation of capacity; below ~85% for crackers usually means weak spreads.
Cost curve
Producers ranked by cash cost; the marginal (highest-cost) plant sets the price.
QCO
BIS quality-control order: mandatory certification that can block uncertified imports.
ADD
Anti-dumping duty against imports priced below fair value.
DGTR
Directorate General of Trade Remedies, which investigates dumping.
EPR
Extended producer responsibility: brand owners must collect and recycle packaging.
SUP
Single-use plastics.
Converter
A company that turns granules into finished plastic products.
Scarcity margin
An unusually high margin earned during a supply shock, often with falling volumes.

19 · Test yourself

Eleven questions; answers hidden

1. Why can domestic polymer prices rise when the rupee weakens, even if global prices are flat?

Domestic prices follow import parity (CFR × USD/INR + duty), so a weaker rupee raises landed cost and domestic prices.

2. Revenue fell 20% but EBITDA rose. How?

Feedstock or monomer prices fell faster than product prices, so the spread widened. Inventory effects can also swing it.

3. Why are US and Middle East producers the low-cost suppliers?

They crack cheap ethane from gas, while Asian crackers use naphtha priced off crude.

4. What did India change in November 2025, and who benefits?

It withdrew 14 polymer and chemical quality-control orders and dropped the PVC suspension-resin anti-dumping duty. That benefits converters and MSMEs, and hurts domestic producers.

5. Why is styrene a choke point for Supreme Petrochem and Styrenix?

India has no styrene plant; they import all of it, so their margin is a conversion spread exposed to global styrene swings.

6. What caused the 2023–25 downcycle?

China added ~16 mt of ethylene capacity between 2023 and 2025 into weak demand, creating record surpluses and pushing spreads to multi-decade lows.

7. What is an inventory gain, and why strip it out?

Selling product made from stock bought cheaper before a price rise. It is temporary, so it overstates the underlying margin.

8. Who gains most from India's plastics demand growth?

Converters and brand owners with distribution. Producers are capped by import parity in a globally oversupplied market.

9. Why did Andhra Petrochemicals stop production in March 2026?

HPCL halted the propylene it supplies by pipeline from its Visakhapatnam refinery during the war; a standalone plant with one feedstock supplier has no fallback.

10. Why did Supreme Petrochem's profit triple in Q1 FY27 while its volumes fell 25%?

The war lifted polymer prices faster than costs (a scarcity margin with inventory gains) while exports were suspended and demand softened. It's unlikely to persist.

11. Which years were the basket's best, and what did they share?

2015, 2017, 2020 and 2021: collapsing or low crude and widening spreads (2015, 2020), plus small-cap booms (2017, 2021).
