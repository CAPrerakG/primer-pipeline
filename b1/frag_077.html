Metal Trading & Distribution

Thirteen primers in this family have described people who dig metal up, melt it, roll it or recycle it. This one is about the people who simply move it from one owner to another — and it earns less than any of them. A metal trader's gross margin is a few percent; its return depends entirely on how many times a year it can turn its working capital; and it has no moat that a well-capitalised rival cannot copy in a season. This section contains both the endpoint of that logic and its modern answer: a state trading corporation whose auditors now question whether it is a going concern, and a distributor building steel service centres because pure trading was never going to be enough.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

A few percent, or nothing at all

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Imagine buying a crate of mangoes for ₹100 and selling it for ₹103. You only make ₹3 — but if you can do it every week instead of once a year, those ₹3 add up. That is a trader. The danger is obvious: if the price of mangoes falls while the crate is still in your van, you lose money, and anybody with ₹100 can do exactly what you do. So the clever traders stop just moving crates and start slicing the mangoes, because nobody pays extra for carrying, but they will pay for cutting.

Say it to a client in one minute

One cautionary tale and one real business, in the same watchlist section.

  • MMTC (~₹8,972 cr): revenue from operations of ₹0.68 cr in Q1 FY27, down 50%. Profit of about ₹104 cr came almost entirely from ₹145.44 cr of other income. Auditors have flagged material uncertainty over going concern after the ministry directed a manpower scale-down and business-exit roadmap, and qualified the accounts over an unprovided ₹82.82 cr Anglo Coal claim.
  • SG Mart (~₹8,785 cr): Q1 FY27 revenue ₹1,308.57 cr (+14%), profit before tax ₹58.23 cr (+36.7%), profit ₹45.58 cr (+41%) — a 3.5% net margin. Service centres handle 40,000 tonnes a month, with five more a year planned and a 3.5–4% EBITDA margin targeted in that vertical.
  • The capital ask: SG Mart plans over ₹1,500 cr of capex across two to three years, ₹400–500 cr in FY27, and guides EBITDA from about ₹200 cr in FY26 to ₹400 cr in FY27.
  • The rest: ten names, several of them 89–98% below old highs, and one with no price data at all.
  • The calendar: February–March has worked twice in thirteen years, and August–September twice in twelve.

2 · What the business actually is

Four ways to stand between a mill and a buyer

ModelWhat it doesWhere the margin comes fromTypical spread
Pure tradingBuys and resells metal without touching itPrice arbitrage and information1–2%, and negative when prices fall
Canalised or agency tradeImports on behalf of the state or under a licenceA commission fixed by policyWhatever the policy allows — until it is withdrawn
DistributionHolds stock, breaks bulk, offers credit and deliveryService, credit and availability2–4%
Service centreCuts, slits, shears and processes to the customer's sizeConversion, not trading3.5–4% at the EBITDA line, and stickier

The whole investment question in this section is which row a company sits in and which way it is travelling. Rows one and two have no defence: a pure trader's information advantage has been eroded by price transparency, and an agency's commission exists only while the government wants it to. Rows three and four are real businesses, because holding inventory near a customer and cutting metal to size are services that somebody must perform and that carry switching costs.

The chain in one breath

  • Mills sell big; fabricators buy small.
  • Somebody must own the metal in between, and carry the price risk.
  • Breaking bulk is worth a little.
  • Cutting to size is worth more.
  • Extending credit is worth more again, and is the real risk.
Fund-manager lens

Distribution is a return-on-capital business disguised as a revenue business. A 3% net margin is perfectly adequate if capital turns six times a year and terrible if it turns twice — which is why SG Mart's decision to lease some service centres rather than own them, explicitly to improve return on capital, is more informative than its revenue growth. Two numbers settle any thesis here: inventory days plus receivable days, and EBITDA per tonne handled. Revenue growth on its own tells you only that the steel price went up.

The audit: why MMTC's record starts in 2014

MMTC's price series runs back to 2010 and shows a high of about ₹1,249 on 18 January 2010 against ₹60 today — a 95% fall. Before anyone uses that figure, look at what happens in 2013: the series closes 2012 at ₹425.7 and 2013 at ₹35.1, a decline of roughly 92% in one year, and yet no single day in the entire series falls more than 10%. A fall of that size with no step is consistent with two very different things — a long run of successive circuit-limited declines, which MMTC genuinely experienced as a notoriously illiquid, thin-float PSU, or an unadjusted change in face value, MMTC's having moved from ₹10 to ₹1. Price data alone cannot distinguish them.

So we do not try. We start the record on 1 January 2014, make no level claim across 2013, and flag that any long-run MMTC chart should be checked against the corporate-action history before being quoted. This is the fourth corporate-action trap in this family — see also Orient Ceratech in 070, HEG in 071 and Vedanta in 072 and 075 — and the pattern is worth internalising: in a sector full of restructurings, splits and demergers, a fifteen-year price series is a claim to be verified, not a fact.

Ten of the twelve names here are not investable on any reading: four are 77–98% below old highs, two have under 300 days of data, one has no data at all, and the data provider classifies members of a metal-trading section as coal, an investment manager and — in SG Mart's case — an electric utility. SG Mart's classification in particular should be corrected: it is a steel distributor, not a utility.

5 · How money is made

Turns, not margin

The arithmetic of distribution is worth writing out, because it explains why a 3% margin can be a good business and why revenue growth is almost meaningless.

Return on capital ≈ (net margin) × (capital turns per year)

  • At a 3% margin and two turns, the business earns about 6% on its capital — below its cost of debt, and not worth doing.
  • At a 3% margin and six turns, it earns about 18% — a genuinely good business.
  • The difference is entirely inventory days and receivable days, neither of which appears in a revenue headline.

This is why SG Mart's management talks about leasing service centres rather than buying them and why it quantifies EBITDA rather than revenue: on its own guidance, EBITDA roughly doubles from about ₹200 cr in FY26 to ₹400 cr in FY27, and it has been explicit that the revenue number depends on where steel prices go. That is the right way for a distributor to guide, and the right way to hold one to account.

The two risks that actually kill distributors

  1. Inventory price risk. A trader owns metal it has not yet sold. When steel falls 10% in a month, a business earning a 3% margin loses several quarters of profit on the stock in its yard. This is why distributors fail in falling markets, not rising ones.
  2. Receivable risk. The customer base is thousands of small fabricators who are financed by their suppliers. In a downturn the metal price falls and the receivables go bad at the same time — the two risks are correlated, which is precisely what makes them dangerous.

5b · The MMTC lesson

What happens when the privilege goes away

MMTC is the most instructive corpse in Indian metals, and it deserves a section rather than a footnote, because the mechanism that hollowed it out applies to any business whose margin comes from a rule rather than from a service.

For decades MMTC was a canalising agency: certain imports and exports of minerals, metals and bullion could legally be routed only through designated state trading corporations, and MMTC earned a commission on that flow. It did not need to be efficient, or to hold inventory cleverly, or to build service centres, because the volume arrived by statute. At its peak the company's market value was among the largest on the exchange, on a tiny public float — one of the more famous anomalies in Indian market history.

Then the privilege was progressively withdrawn. Gold and silver import channels were opened to banks and private agencies; canalisation of minerals was relaxed; and the commission business simply evaporated. What is left, thirteen years later, is visible in one line of the June quarter: revenue from operations of ₹0.68 crore, against ₹145.44 crore of other income. The company earns money on its cash and its legacy assets, not on trade.

Read the accounts, not the profit line

Three things in MMTC's June quarter are more important than its reported profit.

  • The going-concern flag. Auditors noted material uncertainty about the company's ability to continue as a going concern, after its administrative ministry directed it to prepare a manpower scale-down and a business-exit roadmap. That is the ministry, not the market, deciding the company's future.
  • The audit qualification. A ₹82.82 crore Anglo Coal claim is carried as a contingent liability rather than provided for. Whatever the legal merits, an auditor disagreeing with management about a provision of that size relative to reported profit is a disclosure worth reading in full.
  • The reported profit differs by source. Coverage of the same quarter cites consolidated profit of about ₹104 crore in one place and ₹93.73 crore in another. Both cannot be right, and the difference is likely standalone versus consolidated. Take the figure from the filing, not from a summary.

The investment conclusion is not that MMTC is worthless — a company with substantial cash and legacy assets, including its stake in a bullion refining joint venture, has a value. It is that MMTC must be analysed as an asset-realisation situation, not as a trading company, and that its ₹8,972 crore market value has to be justified entirely by what can be sold and distributed, on a timetable the government controls. Anyone valuing it on a price-to-earnings multiple is capitalising treasury income.

5c · Why the market stays fragmented

A multi-tier chain, and the mills now coming for it

Understanding the opportunity here requires understanding why it still exists. Indian steel distribution is unusually fragmented: the large integrated producers run their own distribution arms and authorised dealer networks, but a very large share of steel — especially construction-grade long products — still reaches the end user through a multi-tier chain of wholesale dealers, stockists and retailers, each taking a slice.

Two structural facts keep that chain alive. First, supply itself is fragmented: secondary and MSME producers accounted for roughly 47% of crude steel capacity in FY2024-25, meaning hundreds of electric-arc and induction-furnace operators whose output has no captive distribution at all. Second, demand is fragmented: the buyer is typically a small fabricator wanting two tonnes cut to size, on credit, tomorrow — a customer no large mill wants to serve directly.

That gap is the entire commercial logic of an organised distributor, and it is genuinely large. But it is also now contested from above. JSW Steel has invested heavily in direct digital distribution under the JSW One brand, aggregating availability across its network specifically to reach the small and medium consumers who previously depended on multi-tier dealers — explicitly to strip out intermediary cost. When a producer with its own metal, its own brand and its own balance sheet decides to serve your customer directly, an independent distributor's answer has to be something the mill cannot easily replicate.

  • Multi-brand availability. A fabricator often wants whichever grade is cheapest today, not one producer's catalogue.
  • Processing. Cutting and slitting to a drawing is a service business, not a sales channel — which is why service centres, not platforms, are the defensible part of SG Mart's model.
  • Credit judgement. Deciding which small fabricator is good for thirty days is local knowledge that a national mill's credit policy handles badly.

For an investor the test is simple: is the distributor growing because the organised share of a fragmented market is rising, or merely because the steel price is? The first is durable and the second is not — and only tonnes handled and EBITDA per tonne can tell them apart. India's steel consumption is forecast to keep compounding for a decade, so the market will grow either way; the share question is what decides whether a distributor earns its cost of capital while it does.

7 · The business calendar

The working-capital year

  • January–March: positions are cut and balance sheets dressed before the fiscal year end; the weakest stretch, with February winning once in thirteen years.
  • April–July: new credit limits, new budgets and restocking; 8 wins of 13, median +11.1 points.
  • August–September: the monsoon slows construction and fabricators stop buying; 2 wins in 12 years, median −13.2.
  • October–December: the post-monsoon construction season is when a distributor makes its year; 8 of 12, and December 8 of 12.
  • Through the year: a falling steel price hurts immediately and a rising one helps immediately, because the inventory is already owned.

How to use this — barely at all

Be blunt about what this table measures. It is one state-owned company with almost no operating business, whose share price for the last six years has been driven by PSU sentiment, divestment speculation and treasury income. The February–March and August–September weakness is consistent with the pattern across every other Metals basket, which is mildly reassuring, but nobody should trade a seasonal signal derived from a single going-concern-qualified holding company. Its value on this page is as a record of what happened, not as a signal.

9 · What moves margins

Eight drivers

  1. Steel and metal price direction. Rising prices create inventory gains; falling prices destroy a year's profit.
  2. Working-capital turns. Inventory days plus receivable days — the real determinant of returns.
  3. Cost of credit. The business is financed borrowing.
  4. Processing mix. How much volume goes through service centres rather than straight resale.
  5. Fabricator demand. Construction and small-scale engineering activity.
  6. Mill direct-selling behaviour. When mills court large customers, distributors lose share.
  7. Receivable quality. Bad debts arrive with falling prices, not independently.
  8. Policy, for agency businesses. Canalisation and import channel rules.

The anatomy of the decline

  1. Erosion (2015–2019): canalised trade shrank year after year; the stock lost to the index in three of five years and fell 37% and 39% in 2018 and 2019.
  2. The PSU re-rating (2020–2021): reflation and divestment hopes produced +41% and +67% with no improvement whatsoever in the underlying trade.
  3. The second PSU wave (2023–2024): +58% and +21%, again on sentiment and treasury earnings rather than operations.
  4. Reality (2025–2026): the exit roadmap emerged, the going-concern flag appeared, and the shares gave back ground.

Patterns that repeat

  • The share price has decoupled from the business entirely. Operating revenue fell throughout the +67%, +58% and +21% years.
  • Every strong year was a sentiment year — reflation, divestment, PSU re-rating — and none was an earnings year.
  • The weak seasons match the rest of the family, which suggests the residual metal-sector association still drives short-term flows.

The international evidence is consistent and unkind to pure intermediation. Every trading business that has endured has escaped trading: Glencore bought mines, the Japanese houses became investors, and the American metal distributors became processors. None of them stayed in the business of buying metal and selling it unchanged, because that business has no defence. SG Mart's service-centre strategy is therefore not a diversification — it is the only known survival route.

12 · Where the model is going

Six shifts

  1. Service centres over yards. Processing to size converts a trading spread into a conversion margin.
  2. Digital B2B platforms. Order, price and delivery online, which lowers the cost of serving small fabricators.
  3. Embedded finance. Formalising the credit that distributors already extend, sometimes with a lender partner.
  4. Asset-light expansion. Leased service centres to lift return on capital, as SG Mart is doing explicitly.
  5. Mills going direct. The competitive threat: producers building their own distribution and retail brands.
  6. Price transparency. Indices and online benchmarks steadily erase the information advantage that pure trading relied on.

What is priced in

Two completely different things, which is why this section should never be traded as a group. MMTC at ₹8,972 crore is priced as an asset-realisation story: cash, land and a bullion refining joint venture, to be monetised on a government timetable, with a going-concern qualification and an ₹82.82 crore disputed claim attached. Whether that is cheap depends entirely on a net-asset estimate that nobody can build from a quarterly result — and the profit line, being other income, tells you nothing about it.

SG Mart at ₹8,785 crore is priced for the guidance being delivered: EBITDA doubling to about ₹400 cr in FY27 and a service-centre network compounding. The stock is up 96% this year, so a good deal of that is already in the price. What would justify it is evidence on the two ratios that matter — working-capital days holding steady as volumes scale, and EBITDA per tonne rising as the processing share grows. What would break it is a falling steel price arriving while ₹400–500 cr of capex is in flight, because a 3.5% margin does not absorb inventory losses.

The honest read — and the family's conclusion

This section closes the Metals family on the right note, because it makes the through-line explicit. Across fourteen primers, the businesses that earned durable returns were the ones that did something a customer could not easily switch away from: a certified wire rope (069), a total-refractory-management contract (070), a qualified superalloy (075), an integrated smelter with its own power and bauxite (072), a low-cost orebody (074). The ones that struggled were the ones that merely converted or moved material — the extruders squeezed by their own metal price, the standalone smelters on negative treatment charges, and here, most completely, a trader whose margin came from a rule that no longer exists. In metals, proximity to the commodity is not the risk. Interchangeability is.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What were tonnes handled, and what share went through service centres?
  2. What is EBITDA per tonne, and how has it moved?
  3. What are inventory days and receivable days, and where are they heading as you scale?
  4. How is inventory price risk managed — back-to-back sales, hedging, or not at all?
  5. What were bad-debt provisions, and what is the receivable ageing profile?
  6. What proportion of new service centres will be leased rather than owned, and why?
  7. What return on capital do you expect from the announced capex, and by when?
  8. How much business do your mill suppliers now take direct to end customers?
  9. For MMTC: what assets remain, at what book and realisable value, and on what disposal timetable?
  10. For MMTC: what is the company's response to the going-concern observation and the Anglo Coal claim?

19 · Glossary

Every term you'll meet

The business

Trader
A buyer and reseller of metal who does not change the product.
Distributor
A stockist who breaks bulk, delivers and extends credit.
Service centre
A facility that cuts, slits and shears metal to a customer's size.
Slitting
Cutting a wide coil into narrower strips.
Shearing
Cutting coil or plate into flat sheets of set length.
Breaking bulk
Splitting a large consignment into quantities a small buyer can use.
Stockist
A distributor who holds inventory on its own account.
Back-to-back sale
Buying only against a confirmed sale, which removes price risk.
Canalisation
A policy routing specified imports or exports only through designated state agencies.
Canalising agency
The state trading company granted that role — MMTC's original business.
Agency commission
A fee earned for handling trade on someone else's account.
B2B platform
A digital marketplace serving business buyers rather than consumers.

Financial mechanics

Gross spread
Selling price less purchase cost, before any operating expense.
Capital turns
How many times a year working capital cycles through the business.
Inventory days
Average days stock is held before sale.
Receivable days
Average days a customer takes to pay.
Working-capital cycle
Inventory days plus receivable days less payable days.
Return on capital employed
Roughly margin multiplied by turns; the only metric that matters here.
Inventory price risk
Loss on metal already owned when the price falls.
Bad-debt provision
A charge for receivables unlikely to be collected.
Other income
Earnings from investments and treasury rather than operations — MMTC's entire profit.
Asset-light
Growing with leased rather than owned assets, to raise return on capital.
EBITDA per tonne
Operating profit divided by tonnes handled; the honest growth measure.

Accounting and corporate terms

Going concern
The assumption that a company can continue operating; its removal is the gravest audit flag.
Material uncertainty
The auditor's formal language for doubt about that assumption.
Audit qualification
An auditor's statement that the accounts are not fully in order.
Contingent liability
A possible obligation disclosed but not provided for — the ₹82.82 cr dispute here.
Provision
A charge taken for a liability considered probable.
Administrative ministry
The government department that directs a state-owned company.
Scale-down roadmap
A plan to reduce manpower and exit businesses.
Asset realisation
Valuing a company on what its assets would fetch, not on its earnings.
Face value
The nominal value of a share; MMTC's moved from ₹10 to ₹1.
Circuit limit
The daily price band beyond which a stock cannot move, which can mask a collapse over many days.
Thin float
A small proportion of shares actually tradeable, which distorts price discovery.
Price discovery
Whether a stock trades enough for its price to mean anything.
Multi-tier chain
The sequence of wholesaler, stockist and retailer a tonne passes through.
Authorised dealer
A distributor appointed by a mill to sell its brand.
Secondary producer
A smaller, usually electric-furnace steelmaker — about 47% of capacity.
Organised share
The proportion of trade passing through formal, audited businesses.
Disintermediation
A producer bypassing distributors to sell direct, as with JSW One.
Long products
Bars, rods and sections — the most dealer-dependent steel category.

20 · Test yourself

Ten questions; answers hidden

1. Why does a 3% margin not tell you whether a distributor is a good business?

Return on capital is roughly margin times turns: 3% at six turns is about 18%, at two turns about 6%.

2. What is a service centre, and why does it matter?

A facility that cuts and slits metal to the customer's size. It converts a trading spread into a conversion margin, which is stickier and better.

3. What was canalisation?

A policy routing specified imports and exports only through state agencies, which gave MMTC a commission business by statute rather than by service.

4. What did MMTC report in Q1 FY27?

Revenue from operations of ₹0.68 cr, down 50%, with profit of about ₹104 cr coming almost entirely from ₹145.44 cr of other income.

5. What are the two flags in its audit report?

Material uncertainty over going concern after a ministry-directed exit roadmap, and a qualification over an unprovided ₹82.82 cr Anglo Coal claim.

6. How should MMTC be valued?

As an asset-realisation situation — cash, land and its bullion joint venture — not on a multiple of treasury income.

7. Why is its record started in 2014?

The series falls about 92% during 2013 with no single-day step worse than 10%, which could be successive circuit-limited falls or an unadjusted change in face value; price data cannot settle it.

8. What are the two correlated risks in distribution?

Inventory price risk and receivable risk — both worsen together when metal prices fall, which is what makes them dangerous.

9. What does the international evidence say about pure trading?

Every enduring trading business escaped it — Glencore bought mines, the Japanese houses became investors, US distributors became processors.

10. What is the through-line of the whole Metals family?

Interchangeability is the risk, not proximity to the commodity. The businesses that earned durable returns sold something a customer could not easily switch away from.
