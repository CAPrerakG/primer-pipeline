A cylinder looks like the simplest product in engineering: a steel tube closed at both ends. But it holds gas at up to 250 times atmospheric pressure, rides in cars and ambulances, and must be approved, tested and re-tested by law for decades. This primer follows the one company in the watchlist that makes high-pressure cylinders at scale, the LPG-cylinder makers beside it, and the names that share the label without the business or the trading history.

The industry in 60 seconds

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Think of a bicycle pump pushing air into a ball. Now imagine squeezing a whole room of gas into a bottle the size of a suitcase. The bottle has to be very strong so it never bursts, even if it gets hot or bumped. Cars that run on CNG carry these bottles, hospitals keep oxygen in them, and factories use them for welding gases. Kitchens use a different, lighter bottle for cooking gas. A few companies make these bottles, and the government checks every design and tests the bottles again every few years.

Say it to a client in one minute

Only two of eight names form a usable price basket. Everest Kanto Cylinder (EKC) is India’s leading maker of seamless high-pressure steel cylinders for CNG vehicles, industrial gases and medical oxygen, with plants at Tarapur, Kandla and Mundra in India and in Dubai, Pittsburgh and Egypt — about 1.8 million cylinders a year. Mauria Udyog makes LPG cylinders and valves. The rest of the watchlist is an LPG bottler, a closed cylinder maker, a steel-barrel maker, a motor maker, and two companies whose share prices rose 709% and 1,221% in single years before falling more than 80%.

The demand story is real: India had 8.2 million CNG vehicles in March 2025, up about 25% in two years, and the gas regulator targets more than 18,000 CNG stations by 2034. But EKC’s June-quarter revenue fell 10% and profit 42%, which management attributed to temporary supply and operating constraints and West Asia logistics. The basket is down 24.1% this year. The investment question is whether a structurally growing market translates into steady earnings for a capital-intensive, cyclical manufacturer.

Gas cylinders come in two families. High-pressure seamless cylinders are made from steel billet or tube, forged or spun closed, heat-treated and tested; they hold compressed gases — CNG at about 200 bar, oxygen, nitrogen, argon, hydrogen — and are sold to vehicle makers, gas companies and hospitals. Low-pressure welded cylinders hold LPG, a liquefied gas at modest pressure; they are welded from steel sheet and sold mostly to the public-sector oil marketing companies through tenders. A third, newer family is composite cylinders (Type 3 and Type 4) that wrap carbon or glass fibre around a thin liner, saving weight for CNG and hydrogen vehicles.

Why a seamless cylinder is a processing product

A seamless cylinder starts as a steel tube or billet. It is heated and spun or drawn to close the ends without a weld, then quenched and tempered to give the steel the strength it needs, and every cylinder is hydro-tested and stamped. The value lies in metallurgy, forming and test discipline rather than in the weight of steel, which is the forging test of 080 applied to a pressure product. A failure is catastrophic, so the regulator approves each design and each manufacturer, and cylinders in service must be re-tested periodically. That approval system is the main barrier to entry.

Why an LPG cylinder is a tender product

An LPG cylinder is welded from steel sheet, tested and fitted with a valve. The process is simpler and capacity more widespread. Most domestic LPG cylinders are bought by public-sector oil marketing companies through periodic tenders that allocate quantities among approved makers at competitive prices. Volumes follow new LPG connections and replacement of old cylinders; margins follow tender prices against steel costs.

The margin pool sits with approved high-pressure manufacturers that can run plants at high utilisation and pass steel costs through. EKC’s operating margin of about 14% in FY26 is respectable for a steel-processing business. LPG cylinder makers earn less, on tender pricing. Bottlers and distributors earn service margins and are not cylinder manufacturers at all.

Fund-manager lens

For a cylinder maker, the key numbers are plant utilisation, the share of revenue from CNG versus industrial gases, the steel pass-through lag and the approval pipeline for new products such as composite and hydrogen cylinders. Order books matter less than in project industries; volumes follow vehicle and gas demand month by month.

How LPG cylinder economics work

An LPG cylinder maker sells most of its output to oil marketing companies under tenders that fix price and quantity for a period. Its margin is the gap between the tender price and the cost of steel sheet, valves, labour and energy. When steel prices rise after a tender is fixed, the maker absorbs the difference until the next tender or an escalation clause applies; when steel falls, margins widen. Volume depends on new connections and on the oil companies’ replacement programmes for old cylinders, which must be retired after their certified life.

Mauria’s FY26 operating margin of 8.2% and June-quarter 7.8% are consistent with this model. Its revenue fell 18% in June against a year earlier, which in a tender business usually reflects allocation timing rather than end-demand. The more distinctive feature is the absence of current tax, which points to accumulated losses from earlier years still being used; once they are exhausted, reported profit will fall for the same operating result.

Global peers: what cylinder margins look like elsewhere

Two listed overseas cylinder businesses provide a useful benchmark for EKC’s margins and cyclicality. Luxfer, a US-listed maker of aluminium and composite cylinders for SCBA, medical, alternative-fuel and specialty gases, reported 2025 Gas Cylinders segment sales of $174.8 million, down 6.2%, with an adjusted EBITDA margin of 8.6%, citing softer demand in SCBA and clean-energy markets and stronger aerospace and specialty industrial demand. Against those benchmarks, EKC’s FY26 operating margin of about 14% is strong. Its advantage is India’s large CNG vehicle market, which gives steel Type 1 cylinders a volume base that alternative-fuel cylinder makers in Europe and North America lack, and a low-cost manufacturing base. Its exposure is the same as theirs: alternative-fuel cylinder demand is cyclical and policy-dependent, and composite technology could shift share in the segments where weight matters most.

The peers also show that cylinder makers diversify toward specialty and aerospace applications when vehicle-fuel demand weakens. Luxfer cited aerospace and specialty industrial strength; EKC’s release emphasises semiconductors, defence and specialised industrial gases. That shift is the right direction for margins, but it is a gradual one, and none of these companies yet earns most of its profit from it.

Profit, EPS and reconciliation

EKC’s June-quarter profit of ₹30.16 crore compares with ₹51.58 crore a year earlier, but the prior-year figure includes a ₹12.63 crore exceptional gain; on a pre-exceptional basis pre-tax profit fell from ₹52.80 crore to ₹38.54 crore, or 27%. Its FY26 profit of ₹146.67 crore was helped by a net deferred-tax credit of ₹24.70 crore, mostly in the March quarter, when deferred tax was a credit of ₹23.97 crore. Comparing FY26 profit with FY27 run-rates without adjusting for that credit would overstate the decline.

Mauria’s quarterly and annual figures reconcile within the statement; its deferred-tax-only charge is the notable feature. Confidence Futuristic’s FY26 profit of ₹7.00 crore included a tax credit (total tax −₹2.27 crore) from an earlier-year adjustment of ₹4.78 crore, so pre-tax profit of ₹4.73 crore is the better measure of its year. Sarthak’s results are dominated by traded goods: in the June quarter, purchases of stock-in-trade (₹84.44 crore) exceeded revenue, offset by an inventory build.

No EPS denominator check is presented for this roster beyond these attribution points: none of the four companies reports a minority interest that changes the owner numerator, and the excluded names’ per-share figures are not used anywhere in the study.

Capacity, working capital and cash

Cylinder manufacturing is capital-intensive: forming lines, heat-treatment furnaces and test benches. EKC has added a greenfield plant at Mundra, which its FY26 call said had commenced operations to serve domestic demand, and a new platform in Egypt; it also completed the divestment of its Hungary operation. The test to apply

Watch utilisation at Mundra and the overseas plants, depreciation against revenue, and steel inventory days. A cylinder maker that adds capacity ahead of demand will show falling margins until volumes catch up.

When a share price stops describing a business

This watchlist contains three of the clearest examples in the series so far of share prices that do not describe the business behind them. Recognising the pattern matters beyond this primer, because a price study built on such series produces impressive-looking but meaningless statistics.

Three patterns in one roster

The slow collapse. Mauria Udyog traded between about ₹19 and ₹27 for most of 2019, rose to about ₹34.5 in November, and then fell almost every trading day until it reached ₹0.91 in September 2020 — a 97% decline with no single day of −30% or worse, because daily price bands cap each day’s fall. Its business did not lose 97% of its value: it remains an LPG cylinder maker with ₹424 crore of FY26 revenue. A decline of that shape usually reflects the unwinding of an artificially supported price, a surveillance restriction or a collapse in trading interest rather than a change in fundamentals.

The vertical rise. Sarthak rose 1,221.4% in 2021 and Confidence Futuristic 709.2% in 2022. Neither company’s revenue or profit rose remotely as much. Both are now 83–91% below the highs they set in November 2022. Sarthak’s filings show that most of its revenue is traded goods; Confidence Futuristic’s show revenue of ₹158 crore and profit of ₹7 crore for FY26. These are small, thinly traded shares whose prices were driven by trading flows.

The long silence. Gujarat Containers, JSL Industries and Sarthak itself have stretches of many weeks without trades — 13 to 18 gaps of more than 45 days since 2011. A month-end price after six weeks without a trade is not a market price for that month.

How the series handles them

The rules applied here are the same throughout the series. A business outside the activity is excluded regardless of price. A dormant or shell company is excluded. A series with long trading gaps is used only after its last long gap, through a documented entry date. And a series whose price path is inconsistent with the business — an 80–98% fall from an old high, or a multi-hundred-per-cent spike unexplained by results — is treated as a price-discovery failure. Each exclusion is shown with its evidence, and the page reports what the basket would look like if the excluded names were included, so a reader can judge the decision.

The cost of these rules is a thin basket: for most of the study, one company. The alternative — a broader basket including spikes of 700–1,200% — would have produced a seasonal pattern and calendar-year returns that described trading episodes in three small shares rather than anything about gas cylinders.

The practical test

Before using any small company’s price history, compare its largest annual move with its revenue and profit change that year, count its trading gaps, and check how far it sits below its old high. If the price moved ten times more than the business, the price is not evidence about the business.

Everest Kanto through the cycle

Because EKC is the basket for most of the history, its own cycle deserves a direct reading. Its annual share-price moves since 2012 have ranged from −57.9% to +361.1%. It fell sharply in 2013 and 2014, recovered strongly from 2015 to 2017, fell 54.8% in 2018, rose 361.1% in 2021, fell 57.9% in 2022, and has fallen in 2025 and 2026. Few industrial shares in this series have swung as hard.

The business explains part of that volatility. A cylinder plant has high fixed costs, so profit moves much more than revenue. In the June 2026 quarter, a 10.5% fall in revenue reduced the operating proxy by 22.7%, from ₹61.31 crore to ₹47.38 crore, and pre-exceptional pre-tax profit by 27%. Over a full cycle the effect compounds: a few strong years of high utilisation can multiply earnings, and a demand pause can halve them.

Capital allocation adds a second layer. EKC expanded overseas aggressively before 2012, carried the cost of that expansion into the weak years, and has since restructured — completing the sale of its Hungary operation, building a new Indian plant at Mundra, and starting a new platform in Egypt. New capacity raises depreciation before it raises revenue: quarterly depreciation rose from ₹10.69 crore to ₹14.97 crore between the June 2025 and June 2026 quarters. Whether the new plants earn their cost depends on CNG and industrial-gas demand over the next several years.

The financial position today is stronger than in the stress years. Finance cost was about ₹4 crore in the June quarter against operating profit of ₹47 crore, and the company reports more than 30 million cylinders in service and capacity of about 1.8 million a year. Those are the attributes of a durable franchise; the share-price history is a reminder that a durable franchise in a cyclical, fixed-cost business still produces large drawdowns.

How to use the history

EKC has repeatedly given back most of a large rally within a year or two. The share has been most attractive after demand-driven drawdowns when utilisation was low and new demand was visible, and least attractive after vertical rallies. That pattern, not a monthly seasonal effect, is the useful lesson of this basket.

What a one-company history can and cannot say

April beat the Nifty in 9 of 15 years by 8.0 points on average; March is the weakest month (4 of 15). The February–March window was negative in 11 of 15 years with a median of −10.9 points. With one company for most of the history, these are characteristics of EKC’s share price, not of an industry. They should be read as a description of how a volatile mid-cap industrial has traded, not as a seasonal rule.

The volatility is the more important finding. EKC’s annual moves range from −57.9% to +361.1%, and the stock fell more than 45% in three separate years (2013, 2018 and 2022). A cylinder maker is a cyclical, operationally geared business, and its share price has amplified that cyclicality.

How to use this

Do not trade the seasonal pattern; it rests on one company. Use the history to size positions: this group has repeatedly lost half its value within a year after strong rallies.

The biggest swing factor for EKC is volume through its plants. A cylinder line has high fixed costs (furnaces, presses, test equipment), so a 10% drop in volume can cut operating profit by 20% or more, as the June quarter showed. The second is product mix: industrial and specialty cylinders (semiconductors, defence, hydrogen) earn more than standard CNG cylinders, which is why management emphasises them.

The long-term question is electric vehicles. CNG has grown because it is cheaper per kilometre than petrol and diesel for high-mileage users. If electric cars, buses and three-wheelers take that segment, CNG cylinder demand would slow; if gas remains part of the mobility mix, as management argues, and hydrogen vehicles emerge, cylinder makers gain new markets.

The global industry and India’s place

High-pressure cylinder manufacturing is global and concentrated among a handful of makers of seamless steel cylinders and a growing set of composite-cylinder specialists. EKC is one of the few Indian companies with a global footprint: plants in Dubai, Pittsburgh (through CP Industries, which makes large seamless cylinders and tubes) and now Egypt, alongside three Indian plants, with more than 30 million cylinders in service according to its June 2026 release.

India’s domestic market is shaped by CNG. Few large economies use CNG in cars and three-wheelers as widely as India, which makes the Indian CNG cylinder market unusually large and gives local makers scale. The export opportunity lies in industrial gases, hydrogen and composite cylinders, where qualification for foreign approvals is the barrier.

Composite cylinders are the main technology shift globally, because they cut vehicle weight; Type 4 designs with plastic liners are standard for hydrogen vehicles. Confidence Futuristic’s annual report describes a Type-4 high-pressure composite cylinder project at Butibori, Nagpur, developed through a subsidiary. That is a plan to enter the segment, not yet evidence of approved, commercial production.

How a composite cylinder differs in manufacture and risk

A Type 4 cylinder is built in a completely different way from a seamless steel one. A plastic liner, usually moulded polyethylene or polyamide, holds the gas; a metal boss at the neck takes the valve; and continuous carbon or glass fibre soaked in resin is wound over the liner by machine in carefully calculated patterns, then cured. The fibre carries the pressure load. The product is far lighter than steel, which matters for buses, trucks and hydrogen vehicles where weight reduces payload or range, but it uses expensive carbon fibre, needs precise winding and curing control, and has a different failure mode: damage to the fibre from impact, abrasion or fire must be inspected for, and service life is defined differently from steel.

For a steel-cylinder maker, entering composites means new equipment, new materials supply, new approvals and new test procedures — closer to building a new business than extending an old one. For an investor, the practical signals are approvals, a named vehicle-maker customer and recurring revenue; a capacity announcement alone does not establish any of them. The global peer data above show that even established composite makers saw sharp demand swings in 2025, so composite capability is a growth option, not a guarantee.

Composite cylinders are both an opportunity and a threat to steel makers. If vehicle makers move to Type 4 for weight, makers without composite capability lose share. EKC’s and Confidence Futuristic’s progress on approved composite products is therefore worth tracking even though neither has reported material composite revenue.

Policy and regulation

Gas Cylinders Rules, 2016. PESO approves cylinder designs, manufacturers, importers and test stations, and requires periodic testing of cylinders in service. The rules have been amended several times; the approval process is the main entry barrier and determines how quickly new products reach market.

City-gas distribution. PNGRB authorises city-gas networks and tracks CNG station and PNG connection targets; it has encouraged states to adopt city-gas policies and rationalise VAT on natural gas. Faster roll-out supports CNG vehicle adoption and therefore cylinder demand.

LPG programmes. Domestic LPG connections, including those under government schemes, drive LPG cylinder tenders by the oil marketing companies. Once connections saturate, demand shifts to replacement.

Hydrogen and biogas. India’s green-hydrogen and compressed-biogas programmes could create demand for new cylinder types; companies’ references to them are, at this stage, strategic intentions rather than orders.

Withdrawn machinery order. The 2024 machinery omnibus technical regulation was withdrawn in January 2026, as recorded in 089 — Machine Tools; cylinders are governed by their own rules under PESO.

Where the cycle stands now

Demand fundamentals for CNG and industrial gases are healthy, but EKC’s June quarter shows volume and margin pressure from supply constraints, new-capacity ramp-up and overseas weakness. Mauria’s LPG volumes fell. The share prices of both have fallen this year. The industry is between a strong demand trend and a weak near-term earnings print.

The valuation backdrop has also reset. EKC trades 64% below its January 2022 high and 28% lower than a year ago, and Mauria 62% below its August 2025 high. Neither price embeds the 2021 enthusiasm any more. That does not make either cheap on its own: the June quarter showed earnings falling, and in a fixed-cost business a further volume decline would cut profit sharply again. But it does mean that the next upturn in utilisation would start from much lower expectations than the last one, which is the configuration in which this group has historically produced its best returns.

Where conviction should come from

EKC: September and December quarters showing volume recovery, Mundra utilisation rising and margins returning toward 15–16%; progress in industrial, specialty and composite products. Mauria: tender volumes and steel pass-through. The excluded names should not be used as evidence of anything until their prices and businesses align.

The consolidated-minus-standalone comparison is the simplest way to watch the overseas business. In the June 2026 quarter EKC’s standalone (India) revenue was ₹203 crore with an 18% EBITDA margin, and consolidated revenue ₹346 crore with a 14% margin, which implies that the overseas operations earned well below the Indian margin. If the overseas plants converge toward India’s profitability, group margins can rise without any change in Indian demand; if they do not, the Indian business is subsidising them.

The profit-quality check matters because FY26 reported profit was flattered by a net deferred-tax credit of ₹24.70 crore. A reader comparing FY27 quarterly profit with FY26 should use pre-tax, pre-exceptional profit, which fell 27% in June, rather than reported profit, which fell 42% partly because of last year’s one-off gain.

Turning the checklist into a view

A constructive view needs at least four of the seven supportive readings in the same year. In June 2026 the end-demand reading was supportive; utilisation, overseas earnings and profit quality were warnings. That is a hold-and-watch position until two more quarters are reported.

The EV question deserves care. CNG has grown in India because it lowers running costs for high-mileage users — taxis, three-wheelers, light commercial vehicles and buses — and because vehicle makers now offer factory-fitted CNG models. Electric vehicles compete for exactly those users, and electric three-wheelers and buses are already common in some cities. The likely outcome is segment-by-segment: CNG may keep cars and light commercial vehicles longer where charging is scarce, while urban buses and three-wheelers shift faster to electric. For a cylinder maker, the important point is that industrial, medical, specialty and possibly hydrogen gases are unaffected by that shift, which is why diversification away from vehicle CNG matters.

The central debate is whether EKC is a structural compounder riding CNG and industrial gases, or a cyclical manufacturer whose earnings swing with utilisation and whose share price swings even more. The history supports the second description; the demand data support the first. Both can be true: a structurally growing market served by a cyclical business.

The decision rule

Own the cylinder theme only through companies with approved high-pressure capacity and real price discovery, size for volatility, and buy when utilisation is low and demand data are rising, not after a rally.

Glossary: find a term

Products and processes

Seamless cylinder
A cylinder formed from tube or billet without a welded seam, for high pressures.
Welded cylinder
A cylinder made by welding steel sheet; used for LPG at lower pressure.
CNG
Compressed natural gas, stored at about 200 bar for vehicles.
LPG
Liquefied petroleum gas, stored as a liquid at modest pressure.
Type 1 cylinder
An all-metal cylinder.
Type 2 cylinder
A metal liner hoop-wrapped with fibre.
Type 3 cylinder
A metal liner fully wrapped with composite fibre.
Type 4 cylinder
A plastic liner fully wrapped with composite fibre; lightest, used for hydrogen.
Jumbo cylinder
A large seamless tube for bulk gas transport.
Cascade
A bank of linked cylinders for storage or transport.
Heat treatment
Quenching and tempering steel to reach required strength.
Hydro-test
Pressurising a cylinder with water to check strength and leaks.
Spinning
Forming the closed end of a seamless cylinder by rotating hot tube.
Self-closing valve
The valve on a domestic LPG cylinder.
Composite
Fibre and resin material wrapped around a cylinder liner.
Industrial gases
Oxygen, nitrogen, argon, CO₂ and others used in industry and healthcare.
Specialty gases
High-purity gases for electronics, laboratories and medicine.
Hydrogen storage
Holding hydrogen at 350–700 bar in specialised cylinders.

Regulation and market

PESO
Petroleum and Explosives Safety Organisation, India’s regulator for cylinders.
Gas Cylinders Rules, 2016
The rules governing cylinder design, manufacture, filling and testing in India.
Periodic statutory test
Mandatory re-testing of cylinders in service at intervals.
Test station
A facility recognised to test cylinders.
PNGRB
Petroleum and Natural Gas Regulatory Board, which oversees city-gas distribution.
City-gas distribution
Networks supplying CNG to vehicles and piped gas to homes and industry.
CNG station
A filling station dispensing compressed natural gas.
Oil marketing company
Public-sector companies that sell LPG and buy LPG cylinders by tender.
Compressed biogas
Purified biogas compressed for fuel use.
Retrofitment
Converting an existing vehicle to run on CNG.

Financial statements

Operating proxy
Pre-tax profit before exceptional items plus finance cost and depreciation, less other income.
Exceptional item
A separately disclosed unusual gain or charge.
Employee Retention Credit
A US government payroll credit; EKC’s US subsidiary received one in 2025.
Deferred tax
Tax effect of timing differences; a credit can lift reported profit without cash.
Utilisation
Output as a share of installed capacity.
Operating leverage
Profit swinging more than revenue because of fixed costs.
Stock-in-trade
Goods bought for resale rather than manufactured.
Tender
A competitive bid process used by oil companies to buy cylinders.
Divestment
Selling a subsidiary or business.

Results including subsidiaries; for EKC, the overseas plants.

Results of the listed company alone; for EKC, mainly the Indian plants.

Average revenue per unit sold.

Passing input-cost changes on to customers through prices.

The capacity use at which a plant covers its fixed costs.

Past losses used to reduce future taxable profit.
