Electric Meters

Every unit of electricity that India's distribution companies sell is — or should be — measured by a meter. For decades those meters were read by hand, bills were estimated, and a large share of power was never paid for. Since 2021 the government has been replacing them with prepaid smart meters that report consumption automatically and let customers pay in advance. That programme has created a large, policy-driven industry, with one listed company at its centre.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

A national replacement programme, run as a long-term service

Explain it to a 10-year-old

Every house has an electricity meter that counts how much power the family uses, like a taxi meter counting kilometres. Old meters had to be read by a person who walked from house to house, and sometimes the readings were wrong or the bill was never paid. A smart meter sends its reading to the electricity company by itself, and a prepaid smart meter works like a mobile phone recharge — you pay first, then use. India is replacing hundreds of millions of old meters with these new ones, which is a huge job for the companies that make and install them.

Say it to a client in one minute

Smart metering is one of India's largest power-sector programmes, and it is executed slowly and paid for over a decade.

  • Scale: 20.33 crore smart meters sanctioned; over 6 crore installed by August 2026 against an original target of 25 crore by March 2025.
  • Model: an AMISP (advanced metering infrastructure service provider) installs and runs the meters and is paid per meter per month over 7–10 years.
  • Leader: Genus Power — revenue +45% to ₹1,365 cr in Q1 FY27, order book about ₹24,020 cr, a target of at least 1 crore installations in FY27.
  • Pressure: Genus's EBITDA margin fell to 19.1% from 21.2% on raw-material costs; discom payments can run 90–180 days.
  • Stock: +3% in 2026 after −21% in 2025; 33% below its August 2024 peak.

Analogy

The shift is like moving from postpaid to prepaid mobile phones. Postpaid required billing, collection and chasing unpaid accounts; prepaid collects the money first. For India's financially stressed distribution companies, which lose a large share of revenue to theft, estimation and non-payment, that change matters more than the technology itself.

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Parts: chips, switches and plastic cases.
  • Meters: factories put them together and test each one.
  • Service company: a company installs the meters in homes and looks after them for years.
  • Network: the meters send readings by radio or mobile network.
  • Computers: software turns readings into bills and prepaid balances.
  • Electricity company: pays the service company every month.
Fund-manager lens

The critical shift is that meter makers have become service providers. Under the AMISP model, a company like Genus no longer just sells meters; it installs, operates and maintains them for 7–10 years and is paid a monthly fee per meter. That turns a manufacturing business into something closer to an infrastructure annuity — with higher capital needs (often funded through project SPVs and partners such as GIC), long receivables, and exposure to the payment discipline of state discoms. The order book is therefore a mix of near-term manufacturing revenue and long-dated service income, and investors should separate the two.

How an AMISP contract works

Under RDSS, a discom appoints an AMISP through a tender. The AMISP funds and installs the meters, communications and software, and the discom pays a fee per meter per month over the contract life, with part of the central government's grant passed through during installation. The model moves the capital burden from the discom to the AMISP, and it gives the AMISP a long, predictable revenue stream — provided the discom pays. Where discoms pay late, receivables of 90–180 days can build up, and an AMISP's cash flow depends on how reliably the fees arrive.

Analogy

An AMISP is like a company that installs and runs water coolers in offices for a monthly rent. The upfront cost is the company's; the rent pays it back over years. It works beautifully when tenants pay on time — and becomes a financing problem when they do not.

The table explains why national targets slip. Manufacturing capacity is not the constraint — Genus alone can make over 1.8 crore meters a year. The constraints are on the ground: indexing millions of consumers, reaching homes, maintaining communication coverage and persuading households to accept prepaid billing. Installation speed therefore depends on each discom's administration and on state politics as much as on the contractor.

AMISP economics, in one illustration

The broad shape of an AMISP contract can be understood without company-specific numbers. The provider spends heavily at the start — meters, communication equipment, software and installation labour — funded by equity and debt in a project company, plus the portion of the central grant passed through during roll-out. It then receives a fixed fee per meter per month for the contract life. The return depends on four things: the fee won in the tender, the cost of each installed meter, the cost of financing, and whether the discom pays on time. A small rise in meter costs or a few months' delay in payments can erode a meaningful share of the return, because the fee is fixed for years. That is why rising component prices and slow-paying discoms matter so much to the listed company's valuation, even while its revenue grows.

Order-book arithmetic

Genus's order book of about ₹24,020 cr is roughly four years of revenue at its FY27 guidance of ₹6,000–6,500 cr. But part of it is long-dated service income spread over 7–10 years, which will be recognised slowly and depends on discom payments; the manufacturing and installation part converts faster. Separating the two is the most useful thing an analyst can do with the headline number.

5c · The human factor

Consumer acceptance and state politics

Prepaid smart meters change the relationship between households and the electricity company. Consumers who were used to paying late, or not at all, now face disconnection when their balance runs out; others worry about faster-running meters or unfamiliar recharge systems. In several states these concerns have produced protests, slowed installations or led governments to pause prepaid conversion, particularly ahead of elections. The programme's success therefore depends on communication, grievance handling and gradual migration as much as on technology. For investors, the practical implication is that installation pace can vary sharply by state and can change with political circumstances — a risk that sits outside any contractor's control.

7 · The business calendar

Tenders, budgets and installation seasons

  • April–June: new fiscal-year budgets; tender awards and installation ramp-ups.
  • July–September: monsoon slows door-to-door installation in many states.
  • October–March: peak installation season; year-end targets for states.
  • RDSS milestones: central grant releases tied to installation progress.
  • State politics: consumer resistance to prepaid meters can pause roll-outs, especially before elections.

How to use this

This is one company's calendar, so it reflects Genus's own reporting and order flow as much as any industry season. The February–March weakness is consistent over 15 years, and the business logic — new-year tender awards in spring, a heavy fourth-quarter billing push priced in beforehand — is plausible. Treat it as a tendency to be aware of, not a trading rule.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Eight drivers

  1. RDSS sanctions and installation pace: the volume engine.
  2. Discom finances: whether AMISP fees are paid on time.
  3. Consumer acceptance: resistance to prepaid meters has slowed roll-outs in some states.
  4. Component costs: chips, relays, copper and plastics.
  5. Capital availability: AMISPs need funding partners to take on more contracts.
  6. Competition in tenders: fee levels per meter per month.
  7. Replacement cycle: meters replaced after 7–10 years, creating future demand.
  8. Data services: analytics that could add revenue beyond metering.

11 · The global picture

Smart metering everywhere, prepaid mostly in emerging markets

Smart meters are now standard in much of Europe, the US, China and parts of Asia, where utilities use them for automated billing, time-of-day pricing and grid management. Global leaders include Landis+Gyr, Itron, Siemens, Honeywell (Elster) and large Chinese manufacturers. Prepaid metering is most common in emerging markets — South Africa, parts of Africa, Pakistan and now India — where utilities' main problem is collection rather than data. India's programme is among the largest in the world by number of meters, and its AMISP model, which puts financing and operation on private providers, is unusual.

India's choice of the AMISP model was deliberate. Many discoms could not fund a national meter replacement from their own balance sheets, and earlier meter-purchase programmes often ended with meters installed but systems poorly maintained. Paying a private provider a monthly fee tied to performance shifts both the financing and the operating responsibility, in exchange for a long-term claim on discom cash flows. Whether that bargain works depends on discoms honouring payments — the same question that has shaped every earlier reform of India's power distribution.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • From imported to domestic meters as local manufacturing scaled.
  • From product sales to service contracts under the AMISP model.
  • From meters to data: the value increasingly lies in software and analytics.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Prepaid by default: remote recharge and disconnection.
  2. Time-of-day tariffs: smart meters enable different prices at different hours.
  3. Communication choices: RF mesh, cellular (NB-IoT) and power-line communication.
  4. Rooftop solar and net metering: two-way meters for homes that export power (045).
  5. Data analytics: theft detection, load forecasting, outage management.
  6. Domestic components: localising chips, relays and communication modules (050, 051).

What comes after RDSS

A natural question is what happens when the programme ends. Three sources of demand outlast it. First, meters have finite lives, so the tens of crores being installed now will need replacing in the 2030s. Second, the AMISP contracts themselves run for 7–10 years, so the service income continues well after installation stops. Third, the data from smart meters enables new services — time-of-day tariffs, rooftop-solar net metering, outage management and theft analytics — that utilities may pay for. The risk is a gap between the end of the installation wave and the start of replacement, which is why the long-dated service revenue matters so much to the durability of the business.

The honest read

Genus is executing one of the country's largest infrastructure programmes and its revenue is growing fast, yet the stock sits a third below its 2024 peak. The market is weighing three questions: whether installation targets will keep slipping; whether discoms will pay AMISP fees on time over a decade; and whether component costs will keep squeezing margins on fixed-price contracts. The order book gives unusual visibility — about four years of revenue at the FY27 guidance — but its value depends on those payments. The metrics to watch are installation run-rates, receivable days and the margin trend, not new order announcements.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. How many meters were installed this quarter, and what is the monthly run-rate?
  2. What share of the order book is manufacturing versus long-term service fees?
  3. What are receivable days by state, and how many discoms are paying late?
  4. How much equity and debt does each AMISP project need, and who funds it?
  5. What is the gross margin trend, and how are chip and copper costs hedged?
  6. Where has consumer resistance slowed installations?
  7. What fee per meter per month are new tenders clearing at?
  8. What revenue do you expect from data and software services?

Red flags

  • Rising receivables from a few large states.
  • Installation targets repeatedly cut.
  • Winning tenders at fees that do not cover financing costs.
  • Heavy borrowing at the SPV level not visible in the parent's headline numbers.
