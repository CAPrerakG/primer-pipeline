Boilers, Turbines & Power Equipment

The heavy machinery inside a power station: boilers the size of office buildings, steam turbines, generators, gas turbines and the balance of plant around them. For a decade this industry had almost no customers — nobody was ordering coal plants. Now the government plans at least 80 GW of new coal capacity by 2032, and the question has flipped from where the orders will come from to whether anyone can build them on time.

1 · The industry in 60 seconds

The orders came back; now comes the hard part

Explain it to a 10-year-old

A coal power station is really a giant kettle. The boiler burns coal to turn water into very hot, high-pressure steam; the steam spins a turbine like wind spins a pinwheel; the turbine turns a generator, which makes electricity. These companies build those kettles, pinwheels and generators — some weighing hundreds of tonnes, each designed for one particular plant. For about ten years almost nobody in India ordered new coal power stations, so these companies nearly ran out of work. Now the country needs much more electricity in the evenings when the sun is not shining, so the orders have come back in a rush. The new challenge is building all of them on time without losing money.

Say it to a client in one minute

A capital-goods upcycle that has moved from the order phase to the execution phase.

  • The demand is policy-driven and large: at least 80 GW of new coal capacity by 2031–32; required coal and lignite capacity of 283 GW against 217.5 GW installed; 18.2 GW already awarded by states in two years.
  • BHEL is the main beneficiary: order book ₹2,60,255 cr (+27%), Q1 FY27 inflows ₹26,745 cr, revenue +40% to about ₹7,700 cr and a swing to a profit of about ₹380–400 cr from a ₹460 cr loss.
  • Gas turbines and grid equipment are the other engine: Siemens Energy India's revenue rose 39% and profit 68%, at a 21.9% operating margin.
  • Execution is the risk: Thermax booked a ₹91 cr overrun on a legacy project and its profit fell 83%; BHEL's history is one of slow execution on a large book.
  • The section is polluted: several members make induction furnaces for steel, not power equipment, and one of them — Electrotherm — produced the sector's headline returns in 2023 and 2024.

Analogy

Building a power station is like building a ship: each one is custom, it takes four to six years, and the shipyard is paid in instalments as it reaches milestones. A shipyard with a record order book is not rich — it is committed. Whether it makes money depends on building each ship for what it quoted, and the history of this industry is full of yards that won the orders and lost on the steel.

The high-margin, steady part

Each stage for a 10-year-old

  • Planning: the government works out how much reliable power the country will need in ten years.
  • Award: a power company decides to build a plant and picks a builder.
  • Equipment: the builder designs the boiler, turbine and generator for that plant.
  • Manufacturing: enormous steel parts are forged and machined in factories.
  • Erection: everything is carried to site and assembled — a job of several years.
  • Service: for the next thirty years, the maker supplies spare parts and repairs.
Fund-manager lens

The order book is the easy number and the least useful one. What decides returns in heavy engineering is execution — how quickly the book converts to revenue and at what margin — and contract risk, meaning who bears cost escalation on a fixed-price, multi-year job. BHEL has a ₹2.6 lakh cr book and a long history of converting it slowly; Thermax has a ₹14,045 cr book and just lost ₹91 cr on one legacy project. The right metrics are revenue as a share of the opening order book, margin on recently booked work, and working capital, because heavy-equipment contracts consume cash long before they generate it. The improvement in BHEL's free cash flow — ₹5,240 cr against ₹1,890 cr — is the most encouraging number in the section for exactly that reason.

Audit note: two industries share this bucket

This section mixes genuine power-equipment makers — BHEL, Siemens Energy India, Thermax, Triveni Turbine, ISGEC, GE Power India — with induction-furnace makers that supply the steel industry: Electrotherm, Megatherm, Garg Furnace and Inducto Steel. The two groups have different customers and different cycles, and the furnace makers produced the basket's most spectacular numbers: Electrotherm rose 241% in 2023 and 409% in 2024, which is a large part of why the basket shows +116% and +81% in those years. Recommend moving the furnace makers to steel equipment, moving the financial and semiconductor misclassifications out entirely, and restoring Triveni Turbine's price series so the one missing genuine member is counted.

Why a record order book is not the same as record profits

A heavy-equipment contract is priced years before it is delivered. Steel, forgings, labour and logistics costs move in the meantime, and unless the contract carries escalation clauses the contractor absorbs the difference. Execution delays add to it, because overheads continue while milestones slip. That is the arithmetic behind Thermax's quarter: revenue up 7%, one ₹91 cr overrun on an old government project, and profit down 83%. BHEL's recovery is the mirror image — after years in which a shrinking book could not cover fixed costs, a 40% revenue increase on a larger book is producing operating leverage, and the swing from a ₹460 cr loss to a profit of roughly ₹380–400 cr is mostly fixed costs being spread over more work. The test for the next three years is whether margins hold as the book is executed, because this industry's history is that the orders arrive first and the problems a few years later.

Analogy

A heavy-equipment maker is a builder who quotes a fixed price for a house to be finished in five years. When the order book fills, the builder feels rich; the profit, or the loss, only becomes visible when the houses are handed over and the steel bills are totalled.

7 · The business calendar

Orders in spring, deliveries at year-end

  • April–July: the new financial year releases budgets and state and central generators place orders — historically the best window for these stocks (median +11.4).
  • July–September: monsoon slows site erection; July is the weakest single month.
  • October–December: the construction season resumes and milestone billing picks up.
  • January–March: the year-end billing push, followed historically by weak share prices.
  • Award cycle: large thermal awards are lumpy; a single 3×800 MW contract worth ₹21,000 cr can move a quarter's inflows by most of its total.

8 · What the stocks do

Seasonality test on TradingView prices

The exceptions, and why

  • April–July worked in 9 of 15 years (median +11.4); the failures were 2018 (−14.4), 2020 (−7.5), 2012 and 2013 (−4.7 each) and 2022 (−4.3) — each a year when ordering was weak or the market was risk-averse.
  • The best runs were 2024 (+43.8) and 2023 (+36.4), the ordering revival — though both are inflated by the furnace makers in the basket.
  • January–March fails more than it works (7 of 15, median −5.6), with 2013 (−24.3), 2025 (−19.9) and 2022 (−17.4) the worst.
  • July is the weakest month (4 of 16), and 2026's July was among the worst at −12.3.
How to use this

The April–July pattern has a real cause — ordering follows budget releases at the start of the financial year — and a strong median. But the basket includes induction-furnace makers whose 2023–24 returns were driven by the steel cycle, so the magnitudes overstate what the power-equipment makers did. Use the window as a tilt for the genuine names, and track the award calendar directly: large thermal EPC awards are public and move these stocks more than any month does.

9 · What moves demand and margins

Eight drivers

  1. Thermal capacity awards: 18.2 GW in FY25–26; at least 80 GW needed by 2032.
  2. Execution speed: revenue conversion of the book, the main determinant of profit growth.
  3. Contract risk: escalation clauses and liquidated damages decide who absorbs cost inflation.
  4. Raw-material costs: special steels and forgings, priced years after the contract.
  5. Diversification: BHEL's ₹70,000 cr of non-thermal work — nuclear, transmission, defence, gasification, hydro.
  6. Gas turbine demand: peaking capacity and data centres, where Siemens Energy India is positioned.
  7. Service and upgrade demand: an ageing coal fleet needing life extension and emission retrofits.
  8. Working capital: milestone timing, advances and retention money.

The anatomy of a heavy-equipment cycle

  1. A capacity boom fills order books — India's 2008–2011 thermal ordering wave.
  2. Orders stop abruptly when the customers overbuild; backlogs run off for a decade and fixed costs are carried on shrinking revenue — 2012 to 2020, with BHEL at its low in 2019.
  3. A shortage revives ordering, and the stocks re-rate long before the revenue arrives — 2023 and 2024.
  4. Execution determines whether the re-rating is earned. 2026 is the first year of that test, and the early evidence is split between BHEL's operating leverage and Thermax's overrun.

Patterns that repeat

  • The share prices anticipate orders by years — BHEL re-rated in 2023 on the expectation of thermal ordering that is only now reaching revenue.
  • Orders arrive before problems. The ordering boom of 2008–11 produced the stressed plants and slow execution of the 2010s.
  • Services protect the downside: GE Power India and Siemens Energy India lean on installed-base work that continues in any cycle.
  • Mixed-industry baskets mislead: the furnace makers' 2023–24 surge came from steel, not power.

11 · The global picture

Two booms at once: coal in India, gas turbines everywhere

Globally, the heavy power-equipment industry is enjoying its strongest demand in two decades, but for different reasons in different places. In the United States and Europe, data-centre electricity demand and grid reliability concerns have created a shortage of gas turbines and transformers, with delivery slots stretching years ahead — which is why Siemens Energy's global business and its Indian arm are both reporting sharply higher margins, and why Thermax won a boiler order for an American data-centre project. In India, the driver is the evening peak: renewables cannot yet meet demand after sunset, so the government has returned to coal, targeting at least 80 GW of new capacity by 2031–32 and requiring 283 GW of coal and lignite capacity against 217.5 GW installed. Chinese equipment, which took share at the bottom of the Indian market in the last cycle, faces restrictions this time, which favours domestic makers.

Leadership shifts, and what caused them

  • From BHEL monopoly to Chinese competition (2000s): private developers bought cheaper imported equipment, eroding BHEL's share and later leaving poorly performing plants.
  • From a thermal drought to a revival (2012–2023): a decade of almost no coal ordering, ended by the evening-peak problem that renewables created.
  • From coal to gas turbines globally: data-centre demand has made gas turbines the scarcest item in power equipment, a structural positive for the Siemens and GE affiliates.

12 · Where technology is going

Six shifts

  1. Supercritical and ultra-supercritical units: higher-efficiency coal plants, the standard for new Indian capacity (see 037).
  2. Advanced ultra-supercritical technology: still higher temperatures and efficiency, requiring new alloys.
  3. Flexible operation: coal plants designed to ramp around solar output, which changes boiler and turbine design.
  4. Nuclear components: BHEL's ₹12,000 cr of nuclear orders as India's nuclear programme expands.
  5. Coal gasification equipment: ₹8,000 cr in BHEL's book, tied to policy support for gasification (see 036).
  6. Hydrogen-ready and co-firing systems: gas turbines and boilers able to burn hydrogen or biomass blends.

The honest read

This is a genuine upcycle with a policy anchor — the grid needs firm evening capacity and the government has committed to coal to provide it — and the order books prove the demand is real. The investment question has moved on from whether orders arrive to whether they are executed profitably, and the industry's own record is the reason for caution: the last ordering boom produced a decade of slow execution and stressed plants. BHEL's quarter is the best evidence yet that this time can be different, because revenue grew 40% and free cash flow nearly tripled. Thermax's quarter is the reminder of what a single legacy contract can do. The cleanest exposure to the global side of the cycle is Siemens Energy India, whose margins reflect a worldwide shortage of gas turbines and grid equipment rather than an Indian policy decision. And any sector statistic here should be discounted for the furnace makers in the basket, which have nothing to do with power.

17 · Questions for management

Concall checklist

  1. What share of the order book is fixed-price, and what share carries escalation protection?
  2. What margin has been built into orders booked in the last two years?
  3. What revenue is expected from the book this year and next, and what constrains conversion?
  4. What manufacturing capacity is available, and what bottlenecks exist in forgings and castings?
  5. What legacy contracts remain, and what further overrun provisions might be needed?
  6. What are working capital days and advances received against the book?
  7. What proportion of the book is non-thermal, and how fast is it growing?
  8. (Siemens Energy India, GE Power India) What share of revenue is service and upgrade work?

Red flags

  • Order book growth far outpacing revenue growth for several years.
  • Repeated one-off overruns labelled as legacy.
  • Receivables and contract assets rising faster than revenue.
  • Guidance that assumes every awarded plant receives its approvals on time.

The share of fuel energy that becomes steam; a guaranteed performance parameter in contracts.

6. Why should the 2023–24 basket returns be discounted?

Induction-furnace makers that supply the steel industry — Electrotherm rose 241% and 409% — are in the basket and inflate the averages.

7. What is the key risk now that orders are in?

Execution: fixed-price contracts priced years ahead, with overruns and delays borne by the contractor.

8. Which seasonal window works, and why?

April–July, 9 of 15 years with a median of +11.4: ordering follows budget releases at the start of the financial year.

9. What changed about competition since the last cycle?

Chinese equipment, which took share in the 2000s, is now restricted in power-sector procurement.

10. Which genuine member is missing from the data?

Triveni Turbine, an industrial steam-turbine maker worth about ₹17,200 cr, has no usable price series in this dataset.

Where the numbers come from

  • Prices: TradingView daily closes; equal-weighted basket of section members with sufficient history and scale versus NSE:NIFTY, 2012 to 18 September 2026. Triveni Turbine has no usable series; the furnace makers in the basket are flagged in section 4.
  • BHEL: Q1 FY27 revenue and swing to profit; the order book composition and free cash flow; the all-time high; the record order book and large EPC wins; execution as the key risk.
  • Siemens Energy India: June 2026 quarter results; margin, backlog and nine-month figures.
  • Thermax and GE Power India: Thermax's Q1 FY27 results and the ₹91 cr overrun; order booking, order balance and the data-centre order; GE Power India's February rally; its September upper circuit.
  • Policy and demand: thermal awards in FY25–26 and the capital required; the 80 GW coal capacity requirement; the national resource adequacy plan; total capacity and the 2032 target.
